# AI features

Law as of: 2026-10-20

## Purpose

This clause covers a feature built on or powered by an AI system: a chatbot or voice assistant a
person talks to directly, a feature that generates synthetic audio, image, video or text, a feature
that infers emotion or categorises a person from biometric data, and a feature that generates or
manipulates deep-fake content or AI-written text on public-interest matters. It states the disclosure
the product has to give for each of these, the practices the clause may not describe the product as
performing, and the design standard the surrounding interface, beyond the clause's wording alone, has to
meet so a disclosure actually counts as given. It does not state the high-risk AI system requirements
regime, the general-purpose AI model rules, or AI-system governance, market-surveillance and penalty
provisions, none of which sit in the reference file this clause is built from; it does not restate the
general acceptable-use, user-content or liability clauses, which belong in their own files.

## Model wording

Some features of [the service] are powered by an artificial intelligence system. Where [user name]
interacts directly with such a system, for example through a chat or voice interface, [trader name]
will tell [user name] this at the latest when that interaction starts, unless it is already obvious
from how the feature is presented.

Where a feature of [the service] generates synthetic audio, image, video or text, [trader name] marks
that output as artificially generated or manipulated, in a machine-readable way, to the extent this is
technically feasible for the feature in question.

Where a feature of [the service] would expose [user name] to a system that recognises emotion or
categorises a person from biometric data, [trader name] tells [user name] that the system is operating
before [user name] is exposed to it.

Where a feature of [the service] generates or manipulates image, audio or video content that
convincingly resembles a real person, place or event and would appear authentic, or generates or
manipulates text published to inform the public on a matter of public interest, [trader name] discloses
that the content was artificially generated or manipulated, subject to the narrower form of that
disclosure where the content is part of an artistic, satirical or similar work, or where the text
underwent human editorial review with a named party holding editorial responsibility for it.

[Trader name] does not use an AI feature of [the service] to materially distort [user name]'s behaviour
through subliminal, manipulative or deceptive techniques, to exploit a vulnerability tied to [user
name]'s age, disability, or social or economic situation, to score [user name] for general social
trustworthiness, to predict the likelihood that [user name] will commit a crime from profiling or
personality assessment alone, or to infer [user name]'s emotions in a workplace or education setting
other than for a medical or safety purpose.

## Modules

### EU consumers

Give the AI-interaction disclosure at the latest at the first interaction, clearly and distinguishably,
unless it is already obvious from the circumstances and context of use to a natural person who is
reasonably well informed, observant and circumspect; this is the only basis on which the disclosure can
be left out (Art. 50(1), first sentence; Art. 50(5) Regulation (EU) 2024/1689). Mark synthetic audio,
image, video or text output in a machine-readable format detectable as artificially generated or
manipulated, using technical solutions that work reliably, hold up over time and interoperate across
systems, as far as this is technically feasible given the type of content, the cost of implementation
and the state of the art; this marking duty narrows where the feature only performs an assistive,
standard-editing function or does not substantially alter the input the user or deployer provided, and
whether that narrowing applies turns on what the feature actually does (Art. 50(2) Regulation (EU)
2024/1689). Give the emotion-recognition or biometric-categorisation operation notice to the exposed
person, and handle the personal data involved under the GDPR, Regulation (EU) 2018/1725 or Directive
(EU) 2016/680 as applicable (Art. 50(3), first sentence, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689). For deep-fake image,
audio or video content, disclose that it was artificially generated or manipulated, narrowing to a
notice of the existence of such content that does not hamper display or enjoyment where the content is
part of an evidently artistic, creative, satirical, fictional or similar work; for AI-generated or
manipulated text published to inform the public on matters of public interest, give the same disclosure
unless the content underwent human review or editorial control and a natural or legal person holds
editorial responsibility for publishing it (Art. 50(4), first and second paragraphs, Regulation (EU)
2024/1689). Every one of these disclosures has to be clear and distinguishable and given at the latest
at the time of the first interaction or exposure, conforming to applicable accessibility requirements
(Art. 50(5) Regulation (EU) 2024/1689); these duties sit alongside, not instead of, any other
transparency duty Union or national law places on the deployer (Art. 50(6) Regulation (EU) 2024/1689).

State which Art. 3 role the business plays for each AI feature (provider, deployer, or downstream
provider where the feature integrates a foundation model supplied by another party), since the
AI-literacy duty, the prohibited-practices duty and these transparency duties attach to that role; a
business building a feature on a third-party model is itself a downstream provider and carries its own
provider-side duties for that feature (Art. 3, points 3, 4, 68; Art. 4(1); Art. 5(1); Art. 50(1) to (4)
Regulation (EU) 2024/1689). Where staff or representatives operate or use the AI feature on the
business's behalf, describe the measures taken to support their AI literacy, proportionate to their
technical knowledge, experience, education, training, the context of use and the people the feature will
be used on (Art. 4(1) Regulation (EU) 2024/1689).

Where a feature would process special-category personal data to detect or correct bias, present this as
an available, safeguarded option, not a legal requirement: the processing is permitted only to the
extent strictly necessary, under the GDPR and related law, where the goal cannot be met with other data
including synthetic or anonymised data, subject to technical re-use limits, state-of-the-art security
and pseudonymisation, strict access controls and documentation against misuse, no transmission to other
parties, deletion once the bias is corrected or the retention period ends (whichever comes first), and a
processing-record entry stating why the processing was necessary (Art. 4a(1), points (a) to (f); Art.
4a(2) Regulation (EU) 2024/1689).

### EU business customers

The same role classification, AI-literacy and transparency duties apply where the recipient of the
feature is a business rather than a consumer; the Regulation's duties attach to the provider, deployer
and downstream-provider roles as such and are not limited to a consumer-facing deployment (Art. 3,
points 3, 4, 68; Art. 4(1); Art. 50(1) to (4) Regulation (EU) 2024/1689).

### US

The US sources behind this clause (`references/us-federal/ftc-act-dark-patterns.md`) do not prescribe a
specific sentence for an AI-interaction label, a synthetic-content mark or a disclosure of AI
involvement; instead they set a design standard every AI-feature disclosure has to meet. A disclosure needed to prevent a design, claim or interaction
from being deceptive or unfair must be clear and conspicuous, judged by how consumers actually perceive
it within the whole flow, not by whether the information exists somewhere a consumer could find it; a
business cannot rely on a general terms of service document, a privacy policy, a hyperlink, a pop-up or
a drop-down menu to carry that disclosure (FTC Act § 45(n); FTC Staff Guidance, .com Disclosures, Section
III.B; .com Disclosures, Section III.C.2; FTC Staff Report, Bringing Dark Patterns to Light, Section II,
Section IV). Where an AI feature
drives a claim of urgency, scarcity, popularity, or a personalised recommendation, the claim must be
true and not merely a design choice that creates a false impression (Bringing Dark Patterns to Light,
Section I). A business describing what an AI feature collects or does with personal information for a
stated purpose may not use that information for a different purpose or share it with a different
recipient without the consumer's consent (Bringing Dark Patterns to Light, Section IV). None of this
depends on the feature using AI specifically: the unfairness and deception standard in FTC Act § 45
reaches the interface and its net impression on a consumer regardless of what technology produces it
(§ 45(a)(1), (n)).

## Must not

- No clause may describe, as a feature of the product, an AI system using subliminal, manipulative or
  deceptive techniques to materially distort a person's behaviour in a way that causes or is reasonably
  likely to cause significant harm, or exploiting a vulnerability tied to age, disability, or a social or
  economic situation to the same effect; both are prohibited outright (Art. 5(1)(a), (b) Regulation (EU)
  2024/1689).
- No clause may describe a general social-scoring feature that evaluates or classifies people over time
  by social behaviour or personal characteristics where the resulting score leads to detrimental
  treatment unrelated to the context the data came from, or disproportionate to the person's actual
  conduct (Art. 5(1)(c), points (i), (ii) Regulation (EU) 2024/1689).
- No clause may describe a feature that assesses or predicts a person's likelihood of committing a crime
  based solely on profiling or personality assessment; a feature that supports a human assessment
  already grounded in objective, verifiable facts linked to an actual criminal activity is not covered by
  this prohibition (Art. 5(1)(d) Regulation (EU) 2024/1689).
- No clause may describe a feature that builds or expands a facial recognition database by untargeted
  scraping of facial images from the internet or from camera footage (Art. 5(1)(e) Regulation (EU)
  2024/1689).
- No clause may describe a feature that infers a person's emotions in a workplace or education setting,
  other than for medical or safety reasons (Art. 5(1)(f) Regulation (EU) 2024/1689).
- No clause may describe a biometric-categorisation feature that individually categorises people from
  biometric data to deduce race, political opinion, trade union membership, religious or philosophical
  belief, sex life or sexual orientation; this does not bar labelling or filtering a lawfully acquired
  biometric dataset on that basis (Art. 5(1)(g) Regulation (EU) 2024/1689).
- No clause for a general consumer product may claim a real-time remote biometric identification
  capability for publicly accessible spaces; that use is reserved to law enforcement under narrow
  conditions this clause's sources do not cover for a commercial product (Art. 5(1)(h) Regulation (EU)
  2024/1689).
- No clause may describe a feature generating realistic intimate-image or sexually-explicit content of
  an identifiable person without that person's freely given, specific, informed, unambiguous and
  explicit consent to the generation or manipulation itself; a general acceptance of the service's terms
  does not supply this consent (Art. 5(1)(ba), Art. 5(1a) Regulation (EU) 2024/1689). This prohibition
  applies only from 2 December 2026 (Art. 113(a) Regulation (EU) 2024/1689).
- No clause may rely on a disclosure to cure a practice listed above; a disclosure is a defence only to
  the intimate-image consent rule, not to any other Art. 5(1) prohibition (Art. 5(1)(ba); Art. 5(8)
  Regulation (EU) 2024/1689).
- No clause may place the AI-interaction label, the synthetic-content mark, or any other disclosure this
  file requires only inside a general terms of service document, a privacy policy, a hyperlink, a pop-up
  or a drop-down menu; none of these counts as clear and conspicuous on its own (FTC Act § 45(n); .com
  Disclosures, Section III.B; .com Disclosures, Section III.C.2; Bringing Dark Patterns to Light, Section
  II, Section IV).
- No clause may describe an AI-driven personalisation, urgency or popularity claim in terms the product
  cannot actually back with a true, current value at the moment the claim displays (Bringing Dark
  Patterns to Light, Section I).

## Drafting notes

`references/conflicts.md` has no entry specific to this clause, but its C-17 (deceptive design and
manipulative interface patterns) sets the pattern this clause follows for its US module: the FTC's
dark-patterns guidance is enforcement risk under a functional standard, not a prescribed form of words,
while the EU side ties the same conduct to a specific binding duty with its own legal consequence. Here
that EU duty is the Art. 50 transparency regime rather than the UCPD or DSA, but the drafting
consequence is the same: write the design standard (clear and conspicuous, not buried in a general
document) once, and let the EU module state its own binding consequence on top of it, rather than
drafting two separate AI-disclosure standards for the two markets.

Check Art. 5(1)(f) and (g) before drafting any Art. 50(3) emotion-recognition or biometric-categorisation
notice: the notice duty only makes sense for a feature that is lawful to offer in the first place, and a
feature that infers emotion in a workplace or education setting, or categorises people from biometric
data to deduce one of the protected attributes listed above, cannot be rescued into compliance by adding
a notice.

Whether a specific synthetic-content feature actually meets the assistive-function or
no-substantial-alteration narrowing in Art. 50(2), or whether specific AI-generated public-interest text
actually underwent the human review and editorial-responsibility process needed for the Art. 50(4)
narrowing, is a question about that feature's real behaviour, not something this clause can settle by
asserting the narrowing applies; see Open points in `references/eu/ai-act.md`.

Germany's own standard-terms-control check, whether an AI-features clause drafted from this file
survives §§ 305c, 307 BGB review as a surprising or unreasonably disadvantaging clause, is not covered
in this file; it is covered generally in `references/checklists/de-agb.md` and
`references/de/bgb-agb.md`, and should be run from there against the clause drafted here.
