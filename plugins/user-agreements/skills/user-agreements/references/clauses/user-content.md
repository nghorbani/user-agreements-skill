# User content

Law as of: 2026-10-20

## Purpose

This clause states what rights a user grants in the content they post, upload or submit through the
service (text, images, video, reviews, code, or anything else a user contributes), what the provider
may do with it, and on what basis the provider is not itself liable as the publisher of it. It does
not state the moderation process (notice handling, statements of reasons, internal complaints,
suspension for misuse), which belongs in
`references/clauses/moderation-and-suspension.md`, and it does not state what a user may or may not
do with the service (prohibited conduct, spam, scraping), which belongs in
`references/clauses/acceptable-use.md`. It works alongside the provider's own intellectual property in
the service itself (`references/clauses/intellectual-property.md`) and, for a product that also
grants a software licence, alongside `references/clauses/licence-grant.md`.

## Model wording

[User name] keeps ownership of the content [user name] posts, uploads or submits through [the
service]. By posting it, [user name] gives [trader name] a non-exclusive, worldwide, royalty-free
licence to host, store, reproduce, adapt for technical formats, publicly display and distribute that
content, only to the extent needed to operate, promote and improve [the service]. This licence ends
when [user name] deletes the content or closes the account, except for a copy kept as required by law,
for a backup kept on the ordinary backup cycle, or for content other users have already shared further
within [the service] before the deletion.

[User name] confirms that posting the content does not infringe a third party's rights and that
[user name] has the rights needed to grant this licence. [Trader name] does not check content before
it is posted and is not responsible for it; [trader name] reviews, restricts, labels or removes
content under the process in [the moderation and suspension terms], on [trader name]'s own initiative
or in response to a notice, and doing so does not by itself make [trader name] the author or publisher
of that content.

[Notice-and-action module: add the infringement-notice and counter-notice process from [the
notice-and-action page] for a product that stores material at a user's direction.]

## Modules

### EU consumers

Where the service is a hosting service under the Digital Services Act, state in the terms and
conditions, in clear, plain, intelligible and user-friendly language, any restriction the provider
imposes on the use of the service for user-provided information, and the policies, procedures,
measures and tools used for content moderation, including whether algorithmic decision-making or
human review applies (Art. 14(1) Regulation (EU) 2022/2065). Tell users of a significant change to
those terms (Art. 14(2) Regulation (EU) 2022/2065). Write the restriction so it can actually be
applied diligently, objectively and proportionately, with due regard to the affected rights and
legitimate interests on both sides, including a user's freedom of expression (Art. 14(4) Regulation
(EU) 2022/2065).

Do not draft the user-content licence, or the moderation sentence that follows it, as a blanket
disclaimer of liability for user-provided content: the hosting exemption that actually shields the
provider applies only while its own conditions hold, and does not apply where a user acts under the
provider's authority or control (Art. 6(1), (2) Regulation (EU) 2022/2065). Where the service is an
online platform that lets consumers buy from third-party sellers, do not present a seller's listing in
a way that would lead an average consumer to believe the platform itself, or a person acting under its
authority or control, is the one providing the product (Art. 6(3) Regulation (EU) 2022/2065); see
`references/clauses/acceptance-and-formation.md` for that formation question in full.

Do not draft a content-moderation sentence that reads as a general monitoring duty or a promise to
screen content before it is posted: the Regulation puts no general obligation on an intermediary
service to monitor transmitted or stored information, or to seek out facts indicating illegal activity
(Art. 8 Regulation (EU) 2022/2065). Running voluntary, good-faith, diligent checks on top of that is
fine and does not cost the provider its hosting exemption; state any such voluntary checks as
discretionary, not as a service guarantee (Art. 7 Regulation (EU) 2022/2065).

Where the service is a hosting service, the notice-and-action, statement-of-reasons and (for an online
platform) internal complaint mechanics sit in the moderation and suspension clause; do not restate or
shorten them here. Point to that clause rather than duplicating its content (Art. 16, 17, 20 Regulation
(EU) 2022/2065).

### EU business customers

The same Digital Services Act duties run regardless of whether the user is a consumer or a business
recipient of the service, since the Regulation's user-facing duties (terms-and-conditions content,
notice-and-action, statements of reasons) are not limited to consumer recipients (Art. 3(b)
Regulation (EU) 2022/2065). Where the business customer is itself a trader using an online platform to
offer products or services to consumers, the trader-onboarding and marketplace duties in Art. 29 to 32
Regulation (EU) 2022/2065 apply on top of this clause and belong in the onboarding flow, not in the
user-content licence.

### Germany

Germany's own standard-terms-control rules are not covered here; they sit in
`references/checklists/de-agb.md` and `references/de/bgb-agb.md`. Scope the licence to the
operational purpose in the model wording regardless of jurisdiction; see Drafting notes below for
the German-specific content-control check that belongs in those files rather than in this one.

### US

Section 230 lets a terms document state that the provider is not treated as the publisher or speaker
of information a user provides, and that reviewing, filtering, labeling, restricting or removing
content does not make it one (47 U.S.C. § 230(c)(1)). Draft that statement to match what the product
actually does: the immunity runs to a provider or user of an interactive computer service carrying
someone else's content, not to content the provider itself creates or develops, in whole or in part,
which falls outside the shield for that specific content (47 U.S.C. § 230(f)(3)). Do not promise full
or guaranteed review of user content in the licence or anywhere near it; that kind of promise can
support an argument that the provider co-developed the content it reviewed, which risks the very
carve-out above (47 U.S.C. § 230(f)(3)).

Describe content-moderation tools (removal, restriction, filtering, demotion, labeling) as a
voluntary, good-faith exercise of discretion over material the provider considers objectionable,
rather than a guaranteed or contracted-for review, since this voluntary-discretion framing is what
the separate good-faith-moderation immunity actually covers (47 U.S.C. § 230(c)(2)(A), (c)(2)(B)).

Where the product stores, caches, links to, or transmits material at a user's direction, state the
licence and the moderation sentence so they sit consistently with the Digital Millennium Copyright Act
safe harbor the product relies on for that same content: do not draft a monetisation or
promotion mechanic tied to specific uploads in a way that pays out directly from infringing activity,
since that risks the financial-benefit condition that forfeits the hosting and linking safe harbors
(17 U.S.C. § 512(c)(1)(B), (d)(2)). Keep the repeat-infringer termination policy and the
designated-agent notice described in the notice-and-action module, not folded into the user-content
licence itself (17 U.S.C. § 512(c)(2), (i)(1), (i)(2)). Do not condition any of the four safe harbors
on the provider monitoring the service or seeking out facts indicating infringing activity, beyond
what a standard technical measure under § 512(i) requires (17 U.S.C. § 512(m)(1), (m)(2)).

Section 230 and the DMCA safe harbor answer different questions and do not stand in for each other:
the publisher and speaker immunity above does not limit or expand any law on intellectual property, so
it gives no answer, either way, to a copyright claim over user content (47 U.S.C. § 230(e)(2)). Whether
the provider is shielded from a copyright claim over specific user content runs on the § 512 conditions
stated above, not on the Section 230 language that precedes them.

## Must not

- No clause may state or imply that the hosting or caching exemption, or a safe harbor under the
  Digital Millennium Copyright Act, applies unconditionally or regardless of the provider's own conduct
  (Art. 4(1), 5(1), 6(1), (2) Regulation (EU) 2022/2065; 17 U.S.C. § 512(c)(1), (d)(1), (i)(1), (i)(2)).
- No clause may claim the Section 230 publisher and speaker immunity for content the provider itself
  creates or develops, in whole or in part (47 U.S.C. § 230(f)(3)).
- No clause may promise full or guaranteed review of user content (47 U.S.C. § 230(f)(3)).
- No clause may state or imply that the Digital Services Act requires, or that the provider will run,
  blanket pre-screening or general monitoring of user content (Art. 8 Regulation (EU) 2022/2065).
- No content-moderation sentence may present a seller's listing on an online platform in a way that
  would lead an average consumer to believe the platform itself is the one providing the product
  (Art. 6(3) Regulation (EU) 2022/2065).
- No monetisation or promotion mechanic tied to specific uploads may pay out directly from infringing
  activity; that risks forfeiting the hosting and linking safe harbors under the DMCA
  (17 U.S.C. § 512(c)(1)(B), (d)(2)).
- No clause may condition a DMCA safe harbor on the provider monitoring the service or affirmatively
  seeking out facts indicating infringing activity, beyond a standard technical measure's own
  requirements (17 U.S.C. § 512(m)(1), (m)(2)).

## Drafting notes

No conflicts.md entry decides a module choice for this clause yet; the pattern it would belong to is
close to C-5's point that a US liability-shield clause commonly reads as broader than what EU and
German law let a provider claim unconditionally, applied here to the hosting exemption and Section 230
rather than to a liability cap.

Whether the licence grant or the no-pre-check sentence survives §§ 305c, 307 BGB review is a German
standard-terms-control question; it is not worked through in this clause. That check is in
`references/checklists/de-agb.md` and `references/de/bgb-agb.md`, and a drafter should run it there
rather than treat the Germany module above as covering it.

Keep the licence grant itself and the not-the-publisher sentence as two separate statements rather
than one. A licence broad enough to cover active editorial use (featuring, re-formatting for
promotion, licensing to a third party) sits uneasily next to a sentence that also says the provider
does not act as publisher of the same content: the wider the rights the provider takes under the
licence, the more a regulator or a court is likely to ask whether that use crosses into creating or
developing the content for the Section 230 carve-out, or into the kind of involvement that defeats the
hosting exemption's own conditions under the Digital Services Act. Scope the licence to what the
product actually needs operationally (the model wording's hosting, storing, displaying and improving
language) rather than drafting it broader on the assumption that more rights mean more protection,
since a broader grant does not add legal protection here and only adds exposure on the
publisher-immunity side.

Write the moderation sentence as a statement about discretion and process, not as a guarantee of
outcome: describing moderation as something the provider does voluntarily and in good faith, rather
than as a promised or contracted-for check on every item, keeps the sentence consistent with both the
Section 230 good-faith immunity and the Digital Services Act's ban on treating voluntary checks as a
service guarantee. Where the terms also carry a trader-facing or user-facing claim that the platform
reviews or protects content, cross-check that claim against this clause so the two documents do not
describe two different levels of review for the same product.

Point to, rather than restate, the fuller mechanics that belong in a sibling file: the notice-and-action
and statement-of-reasons process in `references/clauses/moderation-and-suspension.md`, the prohibited
conduct a user agrees to in `references/clauses/acceptable-use.md`, and the DMCA designated-agent
filing and counter-notice timeline in the notice-and-action template. A reader who only has this clause
should still be told where those live, since the rights-and-immunity question in this file and the
process question in the sibling files are routinely drafted, and read, together.
