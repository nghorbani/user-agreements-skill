# Privacy and data clause

Law as of: 2026-10-20

## Purpose

This clause states what a terms document or privacy notice must say about processing the
customer's personal data: which lawful basis supports each processing purpose, what a consent
request must look like when consent is the basis chosen, what happens to personal data where it
is itself the price of the contract, what the pre-contract and e-commerce information duties add
for a German consumer, and what a California business must disclose and build into its request
channels. It does not cover cookies or other device-storage consent, which sits in a separate
clause built from the ePrivacy and TDDDG reference files, and it does not cover the security-
breach or cross-border-transfer regime, which belongs in a clause built from those dedicated
reference files once they exist.

## Model wording

We process your personal data only where we have a lawful basis for it, and we tell you, for each
purpose, which basis we rely on: your consent, the need to perform our contract with you, a legal
duty we are under, or [our legitimate interest in [purpose], which we have weighed against your
own interests and rights].

Where we ask for your consent, we ask separately for each purpose, in plain language, with a
genuine option to decline that is as easy to use as the option to agree. You can withdraw consent
at any time, as easily as you gave it, through [settings location]; withdrawing does not affect
anything we did before you withdrew, and it will not cost you anything or reduce the rest of the
service beyond what that one withdrawn purpose enabled.

Where you pay for [the service] by letting us process your personal data instead of, or alongside,
a money price, we only use that data to provide [the service] or to meet a legal duty we are under,
unless we tell you otherwise and ask for your separate agreement first.

## Modules

### EU consumers

State, for every processing purpose, which Art. 6(1) GDPR basis supports it; a clause saying data
is processed as permitted by law without naming the basis for each purpose meets neither the
purpose-limitation nor the transparency duty (Art. 5(1)(a), (b); Art. 6(1) GDPR). Where several
purposes exist, give each its own stated basis (GDPR Guidelines 2/2019, para. 17; GDPR Guidelines
1/2024, para. 10).

Where consent is the basis: ask for it separately from acceptance of the contract or general
terms, never through a pre-ticked box, silence, inactivity, or scrolling through the page, and
never bundled with another purpose under one tick (Art. 4(11), 7(2) GDPR; Recital 32 GDPR). Tell
the customer, before asking, who is asking, what each processing purpose is, what data is involved,
the right to withdraw, and any automated-decision or transfer risk the consent covers (GDPR
Guidelines 05/2020, paras. 64 to 65). Let withdrawal happen through the same interface used to
consent, free of charge, with no service downgrade beyond what the withdrawn purpose itself
enabled (Art. 7(3) GDPR). Do not make performance of the contract conditional on consent to a
purpose that is not necessary for that contract, and do not call the resulting consent freely
given if it was (Art. 7(4) GDPR; Recital 43 GDPR).

Where the processing is said to be necessary for the contract (Art. 6(1)(b) GDPR), test it against
the contract's own rationale and essential elements, not against whatever the small print happens
to list; service-improvement analytics, fraud-prevention profiling, behavioural advertising, and
engagement-driven personalisation do not qualify on this basis alone (GDPR Guidelines 2/2019,
paras. 28 to 29, 48 to 53, 56 to 57).

Where the processing rests on legitimate interest (Art. 6(1)(f) GDPR), name the specific interest
relied on, document the necessity and balancing assessment before relying on it, and make a
summary of that assessment available to the customer on request (GDPR Guidelines 1/2024, paras.
17 to 23, 59, 67 to 68). State the customer's unconditional right to object to direct marketing at
any time, with no justification needed, and the separate, conditional right to object to processing
based on legitimate interest or public task on grounds relating to the customer's own situation
(Art. 21(1), (2) to (4) GDPR).

Where data collected for one purpose will be used for a new, different purpose not covered by
fresh consent or by a qualifying law, test compatibility against the link between the two
purposes, the context in which the data was collected, the nature of the data, the consequences
the further processing would have for the customer, and any safeguard such as encryption or
pseudonymisation (Art. 6(4) GDPR).

A consent clause stating only that the customer has been informed of, and has consented to, a
collection does not by itself prove valid consent: it fails wherever the box was pre-ticked by the
trader's own staff with no evidence the clause was read, the surrounding wording could mislead the
customer into thinking the contract cannot be concluded without that consent when the processing
is not in fact necessary for it, or refusing is made harder than accepting (Case C-61/19, Orange
Romania).

Where a processing purpose reaches a child, or the service could be offered directly to a child,
gate the relevant signup with an age check calibrated to the applicable national floor, which runs
between 13 and 16 depending on the Member State (Art. 8(1), (2) GDPR). Where special-category
data (health, biometric, genetic, sex life, and the other Art. 9(1) categories) is processed, name
the specific Art. 9(2) ground relied on in addition to, not instead of, the Art. 6(1) basis for the
same processing, and where that ground is explicit consent, obtain it separately from any general
consent for the relationship (Art. 9(1), (2)(a) GDPR).

### EU business customers

The GDPR lawful-basis regime applies the same way regardless of whether the data subject is a
consumer or a business customer's own staff; name the basis for processing a business contact's
personal data (an employee, officer, or representative of the counterparty) the same way as for a
consumer, most often contract necessity for performing the commercial relationship or legitimate
interest for account management (Art. 6(1)(b), (f) GDPR).

Where personal data is supplied in exchange for a digital content or digital service contract with
a business customer acting for purposes within its trade (outside the Digital Content Directive's
own consumer-only scope), that Directive's data-as-price rule does not apply to the contract, so
state the processing purpose and basis under the GDPR rule above instead (Art. 3(1), second
subparagraph DCD, read with its consumer-contract scope).

### Germany

Where personal data is itself the price, or part of the price, of a digital content or digital
service contract (the customer pays nothing, or pays only in part, and provides personal data
instead), the Digital Content Directive applies to that contract on this ground alone, unless the
only processing is to supply the content or service itself or to meet a legal duty, and for no
other purpose (Art. 3(1), second subparagraph DCD). On termination of such a contract, data
protection compliance runs through the GDPR directly: state that the applicable GDPR obligations
continue to apply to the customer's personal data regardless of how the contract ends (Art. 16(2)
DCD).

Where the contract is a Verbrauchervertrag under § 312 BGB and data is provided instead of, or
alongside, a price, the Chapter 1/2 BGB consumer-contract duties apply on that basis as well as
wherever a price is paid (§ 312 Abs. 1a BGB). Give the EGBGB Art. 246a (or, for financial services,
Art. 246b) pre-contract information for an off-premises or distance contract, stated clearly before
the order is placed, and confirm it on a durable medium per § 312f BGB.

Where the customer can conclude a continuing paid contract through a website and that contract
also involves personal-data processing terms, those processing terms do not excuse the separate
Kündigungsschaltfläche duty: the online cancellation button and its confirmation page still apply
in full, built exactly as `references/de/bgb-fernabsatz.md` states (§ 312k BGB), labelled PW-DE-2
and PW-DE-3 per `references/prescribed-wording.md`, and the confirmation page may carry no further
content, including no re-consent request or privacy settings prompt, beyond the five statutory
input fields (§ 312k Abs. 2 Satz 3 BGB; BGH, judgment of 16 July 2026, I ZR 200/25).

### US

State, at or before collecting personal information from a California resident: the categories
collected and the purpose of collecting or using each, whether each category is sold or shared,
the same for sensitive personal information if collected, and the retention period for each
category or the criteria for setting it (Cal. Civ. Code 1798.100(a)(1) to (3)). Maintain a privacy
policy, updated at least every 12 months, covering the categories collected in the preceding 12
months, the sources, the business or commercial purpose, the categories sold or shared (or a
statement that none were) with recipient categories and purpose, the categories disclosed to
service providers or contractors (or a statement that none were), whether sensitive personal
information is used or disclosed outside the listed authorized purposes, an explanation of each
CCPA right and how to exercise it, a contact channel matching how the business primarily interacts
with consumers, and the date of the last update (Cal. Civ. Code 1798.130(a)(5); 11 Cal. Code Regs.
7011(c) to (e)); reach the policy from a conspicuous homepage link using the word "privacy," or,
for a mobile app, from the download page and from inside the app (11 Cal. Code Regs. 7011(c) to
(d), 7003(d)).

Give at least two designated request channels for delete, correct, or know requests (at minimum a
toll-free number, plus a webform if the business has a website; an online-only business dealing
directly with the consumer need only give an email address), and at least two for opt-out and
limit requests, with one matching the business's primary channel (Cal. Civ. Code 1798.130(a)(1);
11 Cal. Code Regs. 7020(a) to (b), 7026(a), 7025). Never condition any of these rights on creating
an account (Cal. Civ. Code 1798.130(a)(2)(A); 11 Cal. Code Regs. 7026(c), 7027(d)).

Post a clear and conspicuous homepage link titled exactly "Do Not Sell or Share My Personal Information"
and one titled exactly "Limit the Use of My Sensitive Personal Information", or a single combined
link; both in the header or footer, matching the size and prominence of the business's other
homepage links (Cal. Civ. Code 1798.135(a)(1) to (3); 11 Cal. Code Regs. 7013(c), 7014(c)). In
place of both, a business may post a single Alternative Opt-out Link titled exactly "Your Privacy Choices"
or "Your California Privacy Choices", with the prescribed icon shown adjacent at a size matching
the business's other header or footer icons (11 Cal. Code Regs. 7015(a) to (b)). Neither link is
needed if the business honors a compliant opt-out preference
signal and processes it frictionlessly, provided the privacy policy discloses the right, the
frictionless processing, how to enable the signal, and any other method offered (Cal. Civ. Code
1798.135(b); 11 Cal. Code Regs. 7025(e) to (g)).

Where the business has actual knowledge a consumer is under 16, do not sell or share that
consumer's data without the age-tiered consent chain: for under 13, a documented, reasonable
parental or guardian consent method on top of whatever COPPA already requires, followed by telling
the parent or guardian of the right to opt the child back out; for 13 and under 16, a reasonable
two-step opt-in, followed by telling the consumer of the ongoing right to opt out (Cal. Civ. Code
1798.120(c); 11 Cal. Code Regs. 7070(a) to (b), 7071(a) to (b)).

Where the business uses an automated decisionmaking feature for a significant decision about a
consumer (financial or lending services, housing, education enrollment or opportunities,
employment or independent-contracting opportunities or compensation, or healthcare services), give
a Pre-use Notice: a plain-language, non-generic explanation of the feature's specific purpose; the
right to opt out and how to request it, or, where a human-appeal or other exception applies, which
one; the right to access the feature and how to request it; a non-retaliation statement; and, at
least behind a link, how the feature processes data to reach the decision, what the output is, and
what the alternative process looks like for a consumer who opts out (11 Cal. Code Regs. 7001(ddd),
7220(b) to (c)).

## Must not

- No clause may say data is processed as permitted by law without naming the Art. 6(1) GDPR
  basis relied on for each purpose (Art. 5(1)(a), (b); Art. 6(1) GDPR).
- No consent clause may be bundled into a general terms-acceptance checkbox, or rely on a
  pre-ticked box, silence, inactivity, or scrolling as the indication of agreement (Art. 7(2) GDPR;
  Recital 32 GDPR).
- No clause may make contract performance conditional on consent to a processing purpose that is
  not necessary for that contract and call the resulting consent freely given (Art. 7(4) GDPR;
  Recital 43 GDPR).
- No clause may describe a purpose as necessary for the performance of the contract where it
  only serves service improvement, fraud-prevention profiling, behavioural advertising, or
  engagement-driven personalisation that is not integral to the contracted service itself (GDPR
  Guidelines 2/2019, paras. 48 to 53, 56 to 57).
- No clause may state a legitimate interest as a vague purpose, such as improving our services,
  with no concrete activity behind it (GDPR Guidelines 1/2024, paras. 17 to 23).
- No clause may authorize special-category processing with a blanket "we may process sensitive
  data as needed"; the specific Art. 9(2) GDPR ground must be named (Art. 9(2) GDPR).
- No clause may rely on a statement that the customer "has been informed of, and has consented
  to" a collection as proof of valid consent where the box was pre-ticked by the trader's own
  staff with no evidence of reading, where the wording could mislead the customer into thinking
  consent is required for the contract, or where refusing is made harder than accepting
  (Case C-61/19, Orange Romania).
- No clause touching § 312k BGB may let a re-consent prompt, a privacy-preference screen, or any
  other content appear on the cancellation confirmation page beyond the five statutory input
  fields on the button labelled PW-DE-2 and the confirmation page labelled PW-DE-3 per
  `references/prescribed-wording.md` (§ 312k Abs. 2 Satz 3 BGB; BGH, judgment of 16 July 2026,
  I ZR 200/25).
- No clause may claim a data-as-price contract is outside consumer-protection law because no money
  changes hands; Art. 3(1), second subparagraph DCD and § 312 Abs. 1a BGB both reach a contract
  where the customer provides personal data instead of, or alongside, a price.
- No California clause may claim a representative-action waiver or any other provision limits a
  consumer's CCPA rights or remedies; such a provision is void by the statute's own terms
  (Cal. Civ. Code 1798.192).
- No California clause may condition a delete, correct, know, opt-out, or limit request on account
  creation, or demand more identifying information than a compliant verification tier requires
  (Cal. Civ. Code 1798.130(a)(2)(A); 11 Cal. Code Regs. 7026(c), 7027(d)).
- No California clause may authorize selling or sharing a consumer's data with actual knowledge
  the consumer is under 16 without the age-tiered consent chain (Cal. Civ. Code 1798.120(c)).

## Drafting notes

This clause states the GDPR lawful-basis regime and the CCPA notice-and-rights regime side by
side because both govern the same underlying act (processing personal data under a consumer
contract), not because the two regimes track each other; an EU consent flow and a California
opt-out flow are structurally different architectures, and C-10 in `references/conflicts.md`
states why a single cookie or data-sharing consent flow cannot serve both. Cookie and other device-storage consent mechanics are not covered here: draft that wording from
`references/eu/eprivacy-cookies.md` and `references/de/tdddg.md` instead.

Where personal data is the price of a digital content or digital service contract, draft the
Germany and EU consumer modules together: the Digital Content Directive's data-as-price scope rule
decides whether the contract is covered at all (Art. 3(1) DCD), and the BGB consumer-contract
duties layer on top once it is (§ 312 Abs. 1a BGB). Confirm which bucket a specific contract falls
into before relying on either module; see `references/de/bgb-fernabsatz.md` for the full BGB
scope-and-bucket rule this summarises.

Confirm, before drafting the US module for a specific product, that the business has crossed one
of the three CCPA thresholds (revenue, data volume, or revenue share) and that any vendor
relationship it describes has the written contract the service-provider, contractor, or
third-party definitions require; a transfer without that contract may itself be a sale or sharing
(`references/us-states/ca-ccpa.md`, which lists both as open points of its own).
