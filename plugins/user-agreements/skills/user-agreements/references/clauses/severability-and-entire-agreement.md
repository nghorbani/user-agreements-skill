# Severability and entire agreement

Law as of: 2026-10-20

## Purpose

This clause does two separate jobs that drafters often fold into one paragraph. The severability
half says what happens to the rest of the contract when one term fails, whether for not being
included at all or for being invalid. The entire-agreement half says that the written terms are
the whole deal between the parties, replacing earlier drafts, side conversations and other
documents. Neither half can rescue a term that the applicable law says must be read as a whole, and
neither half is a substitute for drafting a change clause, a termination clause or a limitation
clause correctly in the first place.

## Model wording

If a court or authority finds that a part of these terms is not valid or not enforceable, the rest
of these terms stays in effect. Where the law that applies to the finding supplies its own rule for
what replaces the invalid part, that rule applies instead of anything stated here. These terms are
the entire agreement between the user and the business about the subject matter they cover, and
replace any earlier agreement or statement about that subject matter. This does not limit any
right the user has that the law does not allow the business to exclude by agreement.

## Modules

### EU consumers

A severability clause is not a drafting device that can turn one term into two. Where what looks
like a single clause in fact contains two or more stipulations that are functionally distinct, each
one of them staying clear and assessable once the other is removed, a court may delete the unfair
one and keep the rest standing; this depends on the content and function of each stipulation, not on
how the clause is split or labelled in the document (Guidance section 4.3.1). Where a clause cannot
be split this way, because deleting part of it would change what the remaining part actually means
or does, the clause is not severable at all and the whole of it falls away (Guidance section 4.3.1).
Do not draft on the assumption that a severability clause will convert an inseverable penalty, cap
or condition into two separable halves; that depends on the clause's own content, not on this one.

Once a court finds a term unfair, it has no power to revise or reduce it to an acceptable level (for
example, scaling down a disproportionate penalty): the whole term is disapplied, and the rest of the
contract continues to bind the parties only where it remains capable of continuing in existence
without the unfair term (Art. 6(1) UCTD; Guidance section 4.3.1). Where deleting the term would mean
the contract cannot continue in existence at all, a national court decides that question objectively
under its own national law, not by reference to either party's preference, and may substitute a
supplementary provision of national law only where nullity would otherwise follow and would be
particularly unfavourable to the consumer (Guidance section 4.3.2). This means the model wording's
reference to the law that applies to the finding has to point at whatever national regime governs
the specific contract, since the UCTD itself gives no general substitute-term rule for a term it
voids.

An entire-agreement clause may not be used to bind the consumer irrevocably to terms the consumer
had no real opportunity to become acquainted with before concluding the contract (Annex point (i)
UCTD). Framing such terms as incorporated "by reference" through an entire-agreement clause does not
cure a missing opportunity to read them; the opportunity has to exist before conclusion, not be
asserted after the fact through the entire-agreement wording.

Nor may an entire-agreement clause be used to make the consumer waive, in advance, the protection
Art. 6(1) gives against an unfair term, whatever the clause is called: a signature acknowledgment,
an acceptance clause, or another form of declaration folded into the entire-agreement wording. The
imperative character of Art. 6(1) means a consumer cannot waive this protection in advance, neither
by contract nor by unilateral declaration, and such a waiver is ineffective before the dispute over
the specific term has been settled (Guidance section 4.1).

### EU business customers

The UCTD's unfairness test, and with it the Art. 6(1) non-revision rule and the Annex point (i)
opportunity-to-read requirement, applies only between a seller or supplier and a consumer (Art. 2(b),
(c) UCTD); it does not reach a contract with a business customer. A severability and entire-agreement
clause used with a business customer can rely on ordinary contract drafting without these
consumer-specific constraints, but state plainly what replaces an invalid term, since nothing in this
module supplies a default substitute-term rule either.

### Germany

Where a standard term is not included in the contract at all, or is invalid, the rest of the
contract stays valid; the content of the gap this leaves is then governed by statutory law, not by
whatever the severability clause itself proposes (§ 306 Abs. 1, 2 BGB). The whole contract is invalid
only where holding the other party to it, even with that statutory substitute filled in, would be an
unreasonable hardship for that party (§ 306 Abs. 3 BGB). No severability clause, alternative drafting,
relabelling or restructuring can do this work on the business's behalf instead: the rules of this
Abschnitt apply even where a different arrangement is used to achieve the same result in substance (§
306a BGB). This is why the model wording defers to the law that applies to the finding rather than
proposing its own substitute: for a German standard-terms contract, § 306 Abs. 2 BGB already supplies
the answer, and a clause purporting to supply a different one adds nothing a court will follow.

An entire-agreement clause does not override the priority an individually negotiated term has over a
standard term dealing with the same point: an individual agreement always prevails over conflicting
standard contract terms, however the standard terms are worded on the point (§ 305b BGB). Where the
business and a user in fact agreed something individually, in writing, by email or otherwise, an
entire-agreement clause in the standard terms cannot be used to tell a court that agreement does not
count.

### US

No module-specific content is drafted here: the EU and German references above do not reach US
contract doctrine, and nothing in this file should be presented as if it did. A severability clause
and an entire-agreement clause used in a US contract need their own basis in US law; the references
under `references/us-federal/` and `references/us-states/` do not cover severability or
entire-agreement drafting, so treat this as an open point for counsel rather than drafting
guidance.

## Must not

- A severability clause may not be relied on as a way to split an inseverable stipulation into a
  severable one; whether a clause can be split turns on whether it in fact contains separable
  stipulations that stay clear and assessable once one is removed, not on how it is drafted or
  presented (Guidance section 4.3.1).
- A severability or substitute-term clause may not let a court, or the business, revise or reduce an
  unfair term to an acceptable level for an EU consumer contract; an unfair term is disapplied in
  full, not scaled down (Art. 6(1) UCTD; Guidance section 4.3.1).
- An entire-agreement clause may not be used to irrevocably bind an EU consumer to terms the consumer
  had no real opportunity to become acquainted with before conclusion (Annex point (i) UCTD).
- An entire-agreement clause, a signature acknowledgment, an acceptance clause or another declaration
  folded into it may not be used to make an EU consumer waive, in advance, the Art. 6(1) protection
  against an unfair term; such a waiver is ineffective before the dispute over the specific term has
  been settled (Art. 6(1) UCTD; Guidance section 4.1).
- A severability clause may not supply its own gap-filling substitute for an invalid or non-included
  term in a German standard-terms contract in place of the statutory content that already governs the
  gap, and no alternative drafting or restructuring may achieve in substance what § 306a BGB forbids
  (§ 306 Abs. 2 BGB; § 306a BGB).
- An entire-agreement clause may not be read to override an individually negotiated term that
  conflicts with the standard terms in a German contract (§ 305b BGB).

## Drafting notes

See `references/conflicts.md` for how this clause interacts with clauses that set out specific
remedies, caps or conditions elsewhere in the document (liability, price and payment, term, renewal
and cancellation): a severability clause never substitutes for drafting those clauses so that each
one stands on its own if challenged, since both EU law and German law treat an inseverable or
unfair term as something a severability clause cannot retroactively fix. The entire-agreement half
is kept short and non-displacing precisely because neither `references/eu/uctd.md` nor
`references/de/bgb-agb.md` lets it override a mandatory consumer protection, an individually
negotiated term, or a term the consumer had no real chance to read before conclusion.
