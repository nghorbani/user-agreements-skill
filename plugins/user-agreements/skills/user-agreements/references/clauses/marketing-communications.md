# Marketing communications

Law as of: 2026-10-20

## Purpose

This clause sets the rules for commercial email the business sends to a user and for cookies and
similar tracking technology used to run marketing on the service. It covers what counts as a
commercial message rather than a service notice, the opt-out a commercial email must carry, and the
consent a cookie or tracking technology needs before it runs. It does not cover the notice and
record duties for non-marketing messages, which the notices-and-communications clause carries, and
it does not cover the substantive privacy or data-processing terms for what the business does with
data once collected, which the privacy-and-data clause carries.

## Model wording

Where the business sends the user a message whose primary purpose is advertising or promoting a
product or service, the message carries a clear way to stop receiving further such messages, and the
business honors a stop request within the time this clause states. The business will not disguise or
falsify who a marketing message is from, and will not use a subject line it knows, or has reason to
know, would mislead the user about the message's content.

A message confirming something the user already did, such as an order, a change to the user's
account, or a renewal, is not a marketing message even if it also mentions another product or
service, and carries no opt-out mechanism on that account.

Before the service stores or reads information on the user's device for a purpose that is not
strictly necessary to run the service the user asked for, such as advertising, analytics beyond
what the service itself needs, or cross-site tracking, the business asks for the user's consent
first, explains each purpose, and lets the user say no as easily as saying yes.

## Modules

### EU consumers

A commercial email or other marketing practice must not create a false impression of the product,
the trader's identity or affiliations, or the user's legal rights, whether through a false statement
or through an overall presentation that is factually accurate in each part but still likely to
mislead (Art. 6(1)(a) to (g) Directive 2005/29/EC). Do not call something "free" or "without charge"
where the user must pay anything beyond the unavoidable cost of responding and of delivery (Annex I
point 20 Directive 2005/29/EC). Do not send persistent, unwanted email, fax or other remote-media
solicitation to a user who has objected, except where national law lets this continue to enforce a
contractual obligation (Annex I point 26 Directive 2005/29/EC).

Where marketing is tied to a free trial or a subscription offer, disclose before the user commits,
in the same place as the headline offer: that signing up creates a recurring payment obligation, the
amount of the one-off and the recurring charge, the trader's identity and address, and the condition
that triggers the paid period (Art. 7(4) Directive 2005/29/EC).

Before the service stores or reads anything on the user's device for a purpose that needs consent,
state the purposes clearly and in full, and, where the stored item collects data for advertising or
a comparable third-party-facing purpose, also state its operating duration and whether any third
party can access it (Art. 5(3) Directive 2002/58/EC; Planet49, paragraphs 74 to 76, 80, 81). Give
each purpose its own control, start every non-essential control unticked, and require a distinct
affirmative action to turn each one on (Planet49, paragraphs 52, 57, 58, 59, 62, 63). Offer a genuine
reject option on the same layer as any accept option, with comparable visual prominence, and let the
user keep using the service without consenting from that first layer (EDPB Cookie Banner Taskforce
report, paragraphs 6, 8, 13, 14). Provide a way to withdraw consent that is reachable at any time,
through the same interface and with the same number of steps the consent flow itself took, from a
centralised, clearly linked place (EDPB Cookie Banner Taskforce report, paragraphs 32, 34, 35; EDPB
Guidelines 03/2022, Section 3.3). Do not label a cookie or tracking technology "essential" or
"strictly necessary" unless it meets that standard, and keep a current list able to justify the label
on request (EDPB Cookie Banner Taskforce report, paragraphs 26 to 30).

An environmental or sustainability claim used in marketing (terms such as *environmentally friendly*,
*eco-friendly*, *green*, *sustainable*, *climate friendly*, *biodegradable* or similar) needs
recognised excellent environmental performance relevant to that specific claim, shown in clear,
prominent terms in the same place as the claim; otherwise it is unfair outright (Annex I point 4a
Directive 2005/29/EC). A future environmental-performance claim (carbon neutral by a stated date, net
zero) needs a clear, objective, publicly available, verifiable implementation plan independently
audited, or it is reviewed case by case rather than treated as settled (Art. 6(2)(d) Directive
2005/29/EC). Advertising investment in carbon-offset projects stays permitted, but not framed as
making the product itself climate neutral (Annex I point 4c Directive 2005/29/EC). A sustainability
label on marketing material may run only where it rests on a recognised certification scheme or a
public authority's own scheme (Annex I point 2a Directive 2005/29/EC). Do not advertise a consumer
benefit that is irrelevant to, and not connected with, any actual feature of the specific product or
business, where this could suggest the product or business is more beneficial than comparable
products or traders (Art. 6(2)(e) Directive 2005/29/EC).

A marketing message naming a reference price (crossed out, recommended retail, or otherwise) states,
as prominently as the price itself, whether the comparison is the trader's own genuine prior price or
a third-party comparison. Headline marketing of a product's price states the unavoidable total; do
not add mandatory charges only later in the flow (Art. 6(1)(d), Art. 7(4)(c) Directive 2005/29/EC).

Keep evidence for a factual marketing claim from when it is first used, ready to produce on request;
where a court or authority asks the business to substantiate a claim and the business cannot, the
claim is treated as inaccurate (Art. 12(a), (b) Directive 2005/29/EC).

### EU business customers

The commercial-email and cookie-consent rules above apply by the medium and the device used, not by
who the recipient is; keep the same opt-out and consent-first wording where the business sends
marketing to, or runs tracking technology against, a business customer's own staff using the service
in their personal capacity on the business's device.

### Germany

Follow the EU consumers module above; Directive 2002/58/EC and Directive 2005/29/EC apply at EU
level, and the German transposition of the cookie-consent rule, the § 25 TDDDG consent standard and
its exemptions, is in `references/de/tdddg.md`. The German transposition of the unfair-commercial-
practices rule (UWG) is an open point: the references here do not cover it, so treat the UWG
wording itself as a matter for counsel.

### US

Every commercial email message, one whose primary purpose is the commercial advertisement or
promotion of a product or service rather than completing a transaction, giving warranty or recall
information, or reporting on an ongoing relationship such as a subscription, carries a working return
address or internet-based opt-out mechanism, clearly and conspicuously displayed, able to receive an
opt-out request for at least 30 days after the message was sent (15 U.S.C. § 7702(2)(A), (17)(A)(i) to
(v); § 7704(a)(3)(A)). It also carries clear and conspicuous identification that it is an
advertisement or solicitation, clear and conspicuous notice of the opt-out opportunity, and the
business's valid physical postal address; the identification duty alone drops out where the user
already gave prior affirmative consent to the message, but the opt-out notice and postal address
stay (15 U.S.C. § 7704(a)(5)(A), (B)). "Affirmative consent" means the user expressly agreed to
receive the message, either by answering a clear and conspicuous consent request or by acting on
their own initiative (15 U.S.C. § 7702(1)(A), (B)).

Once a user opts out, the business, and anyone acting on its behalf, stops sending messages within
the scope of that request within 10 business days, and does not sell, lease, exchange, or otherwise
transfer that user's address for any purpose other than complying with this chapter or other law (15
U.S.C. § 7704(a)(4)(A)(i) to (iv)). The opt-out does not expire with time; it only stops applying if
the user later gives a fresh affirmative consent after the opt-out (15 U.S.C. § 7704(a)(4)(B)).

No message, commercial or transactional, carries header information that is materially false or
materially misleading, and no commercial message carries a subject heading the business knows, or
has reason fairly implied from the circumstances to know, would likely mislead a reasonable
recipient about the message's content (15 U.S.C. § 7704(a)(1), (2)).

Where the business pays an affiliate or partner to promote it by email, the business watches for
false or misleading header information in messages that promote it and either stops the sending or
detects and reports it; knowing of a violation, expecting a benefit from it, and doing nothing is
itself the trigger for liability (15 U.S.C. § 7705(a)(1) to (3)). A business that only supplies
goods, products, property or services to the promoted business is not itself liable under this
provision unless it owns, or holds a greater than 50 percent ownership or economic interest in, that
business, or has actual knowledge that its goods, products, property or services are being promoted
in a message that violates § 7704(a)(1) and receives, or expects to receive, an economic benefit from
the promotion (15 U.S.C. § 7705(b)(1), (2)(A), (B)).

A platform whose customers compose and target their own marketing messages keeps its own role within
automatic transmission, routing, relaying, handling or storage for messages whose recipients the
customer selected, so the platform itself is not an initiator of those messages; if the platform's
own staff or systems instead pick recipients or write subject lines for a customer's message, that
activity is tracked separately, since it can make the platform an initiator regardless of what this
agreement says (15 U.S.C. § 7702(9), (12), (15)).

## Must not

- No clause may let a message confirming an order, a recall notice, or a periodic account statement
  carry no opt-out mechanism and then rely on that same label to also cover a promotional message
  added to the same send; the primary-purpose test looks at the message's content and circumstances,
  not its label (15 U.S.C. § 7702(2)(D), (17)(A)).
- No clause may offer an accept option for cookies or tracking technology with no reject option on
  the same layer, or pre-tick a non-essential control (Planet49, paragraphs 57, 63, 68 to 71; EDPB
  Cookie Banner Taskforce report, paragraphs 6 to 10).
- No clause may condition access to the service on consenting to tracking that is not strictly
  necessary to provide the service the user asked for (Art. 5(3) Directive 2002/58/EC; EDPB Cookie
  Banner Taskforce report, paragraph 13).
- No clause may require more steps, more navigation, or a less visible placement to withdraw cookie
  consent than the original consent flow required (EDPB Cookie Banner Taskforce report, paragraphs
  34, 35).
- No clause may call a cookie or tracking technology "essential" or "strictly necessary" without
  meeting that standard (EDPB Cookie Banner Taskforce report, paragraphs 26 to 30).
- No clause may claim an opt-out request expires, resets, or needs renewal after a stated period; it
  only stops applying on a fresh affirmative consent given after the opt-out (15 U.S.C. §
  7704(a)(4)(B)).
- No clause may claim this agreement's opt-out or unsubscribe mechanism displaces a state's
  anti-falsity or anti-deception rule for commercial email; only a state rule that expressly
  regulates commercial email beyond anti-falsity or anti-deception is displaced (15 U.S.C. §
  7707(b)(1), (2)(A)).
- No clause may use an unsubstantiated generic environmental claim (environmentally friendly, green,
  sustainable, or similar) without evidence of recognised excellent environmental performance
  relevant to that specific claim, held ready from when the claim is first used (Annex I point 4a;
  Art. 12(a), (b) Directive 2005/29/EC).
- No marketing message may falsely claim a product or offer is available only for a very limited
  time, or only on particular terms for a very limited time, to push the user toward an immediate
  decision (Annex I point 7 Directive 2005/29/EC).
- No clause may rely on legitimate interest, under Art. 6(1)(f) GDPR or any other basis, as the legal
  ground for placing or reading a cookie or tracking technology itself; only consent or one of the
  Art. 5(3) exemptions can serve as that basis (EDPB Cookie Banner Taskforce report, paragraph 24).

## Drafting notes

See C-10 in `references/conflicts.md` for the cookie-consent architecture conflict: the EU and German
module needs opt-in, per-purpose consent with a genuine reject option, while a US-only module can run
on an opt-out design; this clause's model wording follows the EU module's opt-in default and the US
module states its own narrower, functional opt-out duty for commercial email specifically, which is a
separate mechanism from cookie consent and is not displaced by C-10's decision.

The CAN-SPAM opt-out duty (US) and the ePrivacy/UCPD consent-and-fairness duties (EU) answer
different questions, one message-level opt-out, one device-level opt-in plus a marketing-fairness
screen, so this clause runs both modules side by side rather than choosing one; a business sending
marketing email to EU and US users needs both the US opt-out mechanism and, separately, EU-compliant
cookie consent for any tracking tied to that marketing.
