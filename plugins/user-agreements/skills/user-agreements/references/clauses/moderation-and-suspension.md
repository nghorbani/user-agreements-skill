# Moderation and suspension

Law as of: 2026-10-20

## Purpose

This clause states the process the provider runs when it restricts, removes, demotes or disables
access to content, or suspends, restricts or terminates an account or a business user's access to the
service: how a notice of allegedly illegal content is handled, what statement of reasons a recipient
gets, how an internal complaint is raised and decided, what the out-of-court settlement route looks
like, and how a misuse policy against repeat bad actors is set out. It does not state what rights the
provider or the user hold in content itself (the licence grant, ownership, or the basis on which the
provider is not the publisher of user content), which belongs in
`references/clauses/user-content.md`, and it does not state what a user may or may not do with the
service (prohibited conduct, spam, scraping, the conduct that can trigger the process stated here),
which belongs in `references/clauses/acceptable-use.md`. Read it alongside
`references/clauses/term-renewal-and-cancellation.md` for an account ending that is not a moderation
or misuse measure, and alongside `references/clauses/dispute-resolution.md` for how a claim that
survives this clause's internal and out-of-court steps is then resolved.

## Model wording

We set out, in plain language, the restrictions we place on using [the service] in connection with
content you or another user provides, and the policies, tools and procedures we use to apply them,
including whether a decision is made by a person, by an automated process, or both. We will tell you
if we make a significant change to this clause.

We may remove, restrict the visibility of, or disable access to content, or restrict, suspend or
terminate your access to [the service] or your account, where we reasonably consider the content or
your use of [the service] is illegal or breaches these terms. Where we do, and we have a way to reach
you, we will give you a statement of reasons covering what we decided, why, whether a notice or our
own review led to it, and how to challenge it, except where the law does not require this or a
narrower rule in a module below applies.

If you disagree with a decision we made about your content or your account, you can raise an internal
complaint through [the complaint channel]; we will not decide it solely by automated means, and we
will reverse our decision where your complaint shows we had it wrong. [EU module: add the
out-of-court settlement paragraph for a recipient located in the EU.] [Business-user module: add the
P2B notice-period, reinstatement and complaint-system paragraphs for a business user of an
intermediation service.]

Anyone can tell us about content they believe is illegal through [the notice channel]; we will
confirm we received a notice that identifies the content and explains why it is said to be illegal,
and tell the notifier what we decided.

Where you, or an entity submitting notices or complaints, do this so often, and so far out of
proportion to genuine notices or complaints, that it becomes clearly abusive, we may warn you first
and then suspend, for a period that fits the pattern, our handling of what you send us or your access
to [the service]; the terms accompanying this clause give worked examples of what we treat this way
and how long a suspension of this kind typically lasts.

## Modules

### EU consumers

Where the service is a hosting service under the Digital Services Act, state the content
moderation restrictions, the tools and policies used to apply them (including whether algorithmic
decision-making, human review, or both apply) and the internal complaint system's rules of procedure
in clear, plain, intelligible and user-friendly language, publicly and in a machine-readable format,
and tell recipients of a significant change (Art. 14(1), (2) Regulation (EU) 2022/2065). Where the
service is primarily directed at, or predominantly used by, minors, put the restrictions and
conditions in terms minors can understand (Art. 14(3) Regulation (EU) 2022/2065).

Build the notice-and-action channel to be easy to access, user-friendly, usable only by electronic
means, and able to take a notice that gives a substantiated explanation of why the content is said to
be illegal, the content's exact location, the notifier's name and email address (except for the
narrow offence categories the file exempts), and a good-faith, accurate-and-complete statement (Art.
16(1), (2) Regulation (EU) 2022/2065). Confirm receipt without undue delay where the notifier gave
contact details, and tell the notifier the decision taken, with redress information, also without
undue delay (Art. 16(4), (5) Regulation (EU) 2022/2065). Say in that notification whether the decision
used automated means (Art. 16(6) Regulation (EU) 2022/2065). A notice meeting all four elements gives
the provider actual knowledge of the content for liability purposes, so route it to a timely, diligent
decision rather than a generic queue (Art. 16(3) Regulation (EU) 2022/2065).

Give a statement of reasons to a recipient affected by a visibility restriction, a payment restriction,
or a suspension, termination or restriction of the service or the account, whether the restriction
started from a notice or from the provider's own review, from the moment it takes effect and wherever
the provider holds the relevant contact details (Art. 17(1), (2) Regulation (EU) 2022/2065). Cover in
it: what was decided, its scope and duration; the facts relied on and whether they came from a notice
or an own-initiative review; whether automated means were used; the legal or contractual ground and
why the content or conduct fails it; and the redress routes, written clearly enough for the recipient
to actually use them (Art. 17(3), (4) Regulation (EU) 2022/2065).

Keep the internal complaint system open for at least six months from the notification, let a complaint
be lodged electronically and free of charge against any of the Art. 20(1) decisions, decide every
complaint in a timely, non-discriminatory, diligent, non-arbitrary way, reverse the decision without
undue delay once the complaint shows sufficient grounds, and never decide it solely by automated means
(Art. 20(1) to (4), (6) Regulation (EU) 2022/2065). Tell the complainant the reasoned outcome and the
Art. 21 out-of-court settlement route without undue delay (Art. 20(5) Regulation (EU) 2022/2065). State
that the recipient can bring an unresolved Art. 20(1) matter to any certified out-of-court settlement
body, that the provider engages with it in good faith with only the narrow repeat-dispute refusal
ground, that going to court stays open at every stage, and that the losing provider pays all the
body's fees and the recipient's other reasonable expenses while a recipient who loses owes nothing
for the provider's own costs unless the recipient clearly acted in bad faith (Art. 21(1), (2), (5)
Regulation (EU) 2022/2065).

Set out the misuse-suspension policy with concrete examples of the facts and circumstances the
provider weighs and the suspension's duration, rather than a bare statement that abuse may be
suspended, and give the prior warning the policy promises before any such suspension (Art. 23(1) to
(4) Regulation (EU) 2022/2065). Where the provider gives priority handling to a trusted flagger's
notices, state that the priority runs only within the flagger's designated area of expertise and that
a quality problem is reported to the awarding Digital Services Coordinator rather than resolved by the
provider unilaterally de-prioritising the flagger (Art. 22(1), (6), (7) Regulation (EU) 2022/2065).

### EU business customers

Where the recipient is a business user of an online intermediation service under the P2B Regulation
rather than, or in addition to, a DSA recipient, give it a reasonable, proportionate notice period of
at least 15 days (longer where it needs more time to adapt) before a terms change takes effect, and a
right to terminate within that period or to waive it by a clear statement or action (Art. 3(2)
Regulation (EU) 2019/1150). This 15-day minimum does not apply where the provider is subject to a
legal or regulatory obligation requiring a terms change on a shorter timeline, or must exceptionally
change the terms to address an unforeseen and imminent cybersecurity danger such as fraud, malware,
spam or a data breach (Art. 3(4)(a), (b) Regulation (EU) 2019/1150). Give a statement of reasons on a
durable medium no later than when a restriction or suspension of individual goods or services takes
effect, and at least 30 days before a
whole-service termination, except for the three narrow grounds the Regulation itself lists, each of
which still needs a statement without undue delay (Art. 4(1), (2), (4) Regulation (EU) 2019/1150). In
every case, let the business user use the Art. 11 internal complaint system to clarify the facts, and
where the measure is revoked, reinstate the business user, including its access to its own data,
without undue delay (Art. 4(3) Regulation (EU) 2019/1150). Keep that internal system easily
accessible, free of charge, proportionate to the complaint's importance and complexity, and
decided and communicated to the business user individually in plain language (Art. 11(1), (2)
Regulation (EU) 2019/1150). The Digital Services Act's own recipient-facing duties (notice-and-action,
statement of reasons, internal complaints) run alongside this module rather than instead of it, since
a business user is still a recipient of the service under that Regulation (Art. 3(b) Regulation (EU)
2022/2065).

### Germany

Germany has no national moderation or suspension statute of its own; the EU consumers and EU
business customers modules above, both drawn from directly applicable EU Regulations, apply in
Germany without a separate national layer. The German standard-terms check on this clause's
wording (inclusion and content control under §§ 305c, 307 BGB) is covered separately in
`references/checklists/de-agb.md` and `references/de/bgb-agb.md`; run that check alongside this
clause rather than treating this module as the full German review.

### US

Section 230 lets the clause state that the provider is not treated as the publisher or speaker of
information a user or another information content provider supplies, and that reviewing, filtering,
labeling, restricting or removing it does not change that (47 U.S.C. § 230(c)(1)). Keep that sentence
limited to content the provider did not itself create or develop in whole or in part; as to content it
did create or develop, it is itself an information content provider for that content and the shield
does not reach it (47 U.S.C. § 230(f)(3)). Describe content moderation, including restricting access
to material the provider considers obscene, lewd, excessively violent, harassing or otherwise
objectionable, as a voluntary exercise of discretion in good faith, not a promised or guaranteed
review of every item; that voluntariness and good faith are what the separate moderation immunity
actually turns on, and a guarantee of full review risks reading as the provider co-developing the
content it reviewed (47 U.S.C. § 230(c)(2)(A), (f)(3)). Where the provider gives users or other
information content providers the technical means to restrict access to such material themselves, for
example blocking, filtering or personal moderation settings, describe that as the same kind of
voluntary, good-faith action (47 U.S.C. § 230(c)(2)(B)).

Where the provider, at the point a customer enters an agreement for the service, has not already done
so elsewhere, give the customer notice that parental control protections are commercially available
that may help limit a minor's access to harmful material, and identify, or point to, current providers
of such protections (47 U.S.C. § 230(d)). Place this duty in the onboarding flow itself rather than
leaving it to a terms document the customer may never open.

State plainly that nothing here limits the enforcement of federal criminal law, including the
obscenity and child-exploitation statutes, or any other federal criminal law; that nothing here
expands or limits intellectual-property law; and that nothing here limits the Electronic
Communications Privacy Act of 1986 or a similar state law (47 U.S.C. § 230(e)(1), (2), (4)). Do not
rely on the publisher/speaker immunity or the good-faith technical-means immunity as a defense to a
civil sex-trafficking claim tied to a violation of 18 U.S.C. § 1591, or to a matching state criminal
prosecution; only the separate good-faith-moderation immunity can apply there, and even that is
conditioned, for a prostitution-promotion prong, on the underlying conduct being illegal where it was
targeted (47 U.S.C. § 230(e)(5)(A) to (C)).

## Must not

- No clause may claim the Section 230 publisher or speaker immunity for content the provider itself
  creates or develops, in whole or in part (47 U.S.C. § 230(f)(3)).
- No clause may promise full or guaranteed review of user content; that kind of promise risks reading
  as the provider co-developing the content it reviewed, which is outside the Section 230(c)(1) shield
  for that content (47 U.S.C. § 230(f)(3)).
- No clause may state or imply that Section 230 displaces a state or local law consistent with it, or
  that it limits federal criminal law, intellectual-property law, or the Electronic Communications
  Privacy Act (47 U.S.C. § 230(e)(1), (2), (3), (4)).
- No clause may claim the Section 230(c)(1) or (c)(2)(B) immunity as a defense to a civil
  sex-trafficking claim tied to 18 U.S.C. § 1591, or to the matching state criminal prosecutions (47
  U.S.C. § 230(e)(5)(A) to (C)).
- No clause may suggest that EU law requires, or that the provider will run, blanket pre-screening or
  general monitoring of content; no such general obligation may be imposed (Art. 8 Regulation (EU)
  2022/2065).
- No clause may narrow the statement-of-reasons duty to notice-triggered restrictions only; it runs
  equally to an own-initiative restriction, with only two carve-outs (deceptive high-volume commercial
  content, an authority order) (Art. 17(2), (5) Regulation (EU) 2022/2065).
- No internal complaint clause may shorten the six-month minimum access window, treat a
  sufficiently-grounded reversal as discretionary, or decide a complaint solely by automated means
  (Art. 20(1), (2), (4), (6) Regulation (EU) 2022/2065).
- No misuse-suspension clause may describe the policy only in generic terms, such as a bare statement
  that abusive accounts may be suspended, with no concrete examples or stated duration, and no
  suspension of this kind may skip the prior warning or the case-by-case assessment (Art. 23(1) to (4)
  Regulation (EU) 2022/2065).
- No clause governing a business user of an online intermediation service may let a terms change,
  restriction, suspension or termination proceed without the Art. 3(2) notice period or the Art. 4
  statement of reasons it is owed, except the narrow grounds that Regulation itself states, each still
  needing a statement without undue delay (Art. 3(2), (3); Art. 4(1), (2), (4), (5) Regulation (EU)
  2019/1150).
- No clause may claim a trusted flagger's notices keep priority handling once the flagger's status is
  suspended or revoked, or let the provider unilaterally de-prioritise a flagger instead of reporting a
  quality problem to the awarding Digital Services Coordinator (Art. 22(1), (6), (7) Regulation (EU)
  2022/2065).

## Drafting notes

See C-19 in `references/conflicts.md` for the module choice behind the EU business customers module:
a US-style clause reserving an unqualified right to change or suspend a business user's account at
any time does not satisfy the P2B Regulation's notice-period, statement-of-reasons and reinstatement
duties for a business user of an online intermediation service, so draft the EU business customers
module from the Regulation's own text rather than adapting the US discretionary language.

Keep the liability-exemption statement (not the publisher or speaker of someone else's content) and
the accountability statement (the provider runs a documented notice, decision and complaint process)
as two separate sentences rather than one blanket disclaimer. The first answers whether the provider
is responsible for someone else's content; the second describes the provider's own process
commitments; satisfying one does not discharge or create the other, and folding them into a single
sentence invites a reading that a process failure also forfeits the liability exemption, or that the
exemption excuses the provider from running the process at all.

Write the moderation-restriction wording so it is specific enough to enforce in practice, rather than
merely clear on paper: once a restriction is stated in the terms, the internal complaint system or an
out-of-court body can hold the provider to that stated rule, so a vague restriction becomes exposure
rather than flexibility once an outside body is positioned to interpret it. For the same reason, keep
the misuse-suspension policy's worked examples current with what the provider actually treats as
abusive, since a policy that no longer matches practice is weaker evidence in a reversal dispute than
one that does.

Describe a user's own reporting duty, where the terms mention one, together with the actual notice
channel and its mechanics (how to submit, what happens after); a reporting expectation with no usable
channel behind it reads as the same kind of gap as a vague restriction. Likewise, describe any
user-facing control over moderation or recommendation outcomes by naming the specific feature rather
than a generic claim of user control.

Confirm the service's size and role before drafting the EU consumers module in full: the internal
complaint-handling, out-of-court settlement and trusted-flagger duties in Art. 20 to 24 (and the
marketplace duties in Art. 29 to 32) exempt a micro or small enterprise, and a service that is a
hosting service but not an online platform owes only the Art. 14 to 18 duties, not the fuller Art. 20
to 24 set. Also confirm whether the service is an online platform under Art. 3(i) at all, given the
minor-ancillary-feature carve-out in that definition, since the classification decides whether the
whole Art. 20 to 28 Section applies, and confirm separately whether the business user counterparty in
the EU business customers module is itself a small enterprise under the Annex to Recommendation
2003/361/EC, since that status exempts it from the P2B Art. 11 complaint system alone and from no
other article this clause draws on.

Where the provider is a very large online platform or search engine, the Art. 14(5), (6) summary and
full-language duties, the Art. 24(2) to (4) active-recipient reporting, and the broader Art. 33
onward systemic-risk regime are not covered here; treat them as an open point for counsel rather
than drafting those duties from this file.
