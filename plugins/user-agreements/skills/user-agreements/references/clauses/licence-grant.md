# Licence grant

Law as of: 2026-10-20

## Purpose

This clause states what the trader actually grants the other party to use software, and what the
other party may and may not do with their copy of it. It covers the scope of the grant (the acts a
licence must list as reserved to the rightholder, and the acts a licence cannot take away from a
lawful user), what happens when the arrangement amounts to a sale rather than a licence, and the
narrow decompilation and interoperability right a licence cannot override. It does not cover the
separate conformity and remedy regime for software delivered as digital content or a digital
service (see `references/clauses/warranty-and-conformity.md`), ownership of content the other party
uploads or creates (see `references/clauses/user-content.md`), or the general intellectual-property
ownership statement for the trader's trademarks and other material (see
`references/clauses/intellectual-property.md`).

## Model wording

[Trader name] grants [licensee name] a [personal, non-exclusive, non-transferable] right to install
and run [the software] on [licensee name]'s own devices, for [licensee name]'s own use, for as long
as this contract runs. [Trader name] does not transfer ownership of [the software] or any copy of
it; [trader name] keeps every right this clause does not expressly grant.

[Licensee name] may make one copy of [the software] where that copy is needed to install or run it,
or to keep as a backup, and may correct an error in [the software] where that is needed to use it
for its intended purpose. [Licensee name] may observe, study, or test how [the software] works,
including by loading, displaying, running, transmitting, or storing it, to work out the ideas and
principles behind any part of it, as long as [licensee name] does this only through acts [licensee
name] is otherwise entitled to carry out under this clause.

[Licensee name] must not copy, adapt, translate, or create a derivative work from [the software]
beyond what this clause allows; must not distribute, rent, lease, sublicense, or make [the
software] available to a third party; and must not reverse-engineer, decompile, or disassemble [the
software], except where [licensee name] needs specific information to make an independently created
program work together with [the software], that information is not already available to [licensee
name], and [licensee name] asked [trader name] for it first. Information obtained this way may be
used only to make the two programs work together, may be passed to a third party only where that is
necessary for the same purpose, and must not be used to build a program that competes with [the
software] or copies its expression.

[Where the software is supplied under a one-off fee for an unlimited period: once [licensee name]
has paid in full, [licensee name] may transfer this licence as a whole to another person, provided
[licensee name] stops using [the software] and deletes or destroys every copy [licensee name] kept,
including any update or patch merged into it, at the time of the transfer.]

## Modules

### EU consumers

State the three restricted acts a licence relies on to define its grant and its limits together,
rather than inventing a different list: reproduction (permanent or temporary, in whole or in part,
by any means, including the incidental reproduction that loading, displaying, running,
transmitting, or storing the program needs); translation, adaptation, arrangement, or any other
alteration, and reproducing the result, without prejudice to the rights of whoever carries out the
alteration; and distribution to the public, including rental, of the original or of copies (Art.
4(1), points (a) to (c), Directive 2009/24/EC).

Keep three exceptions open to the lawful acquirer or lawful user, and do not word the licence so
that it blocks them: a back-up copy necessary for the use the licensee is entitled to make (Art.
5(2) Directive 2009/24/EC); observing, studying, or testing the program's functioning to work out
the underlying ideas and principles, through acts the licensee is entitled to carry out (Art. 5(3)
Directive 2009/24/EC); and decompilation for interoperability, limited to the three conditions in
the model wording's decompilation paragraph and the two use restrictions that follow it, read so
that it may not be applied in a way that unreasonably prejudices [trader name]'s legitimate
interests in [the software] or conflicts with its normal exploitation (Art. 6(1), (2), (3)
Directive 2009/24/EC). A clause purporting to exclude any of these three has no effect to that
extent (Art. 8, second paragraph, Directive 2009/24/EC). The one exception left open to contract is
error correction and the reproduction or adaptation an intended-use copy needs: a licence may make
this subject to a specific provision, since the Directive's own default applies only in the absence
of one (Art. 5(1) Directive 2009/24/EC); state plainly whether the licence narrows this default or
leaves it as given.

Where the arrangement combines a one-off fee with a permanent, unlimited-duration right to use the
copy, this is a sale for the purpose of the EU distribution right, regardless of what the licence
calls itself; the rightholder's distribution right for that copy then exhausts within the Union, a
"non-transferable" label does not block resale, and the acquirer on resale is a lawful acquirer in
their own right under Art. 5(1), entitled to run the program without a licence agreement running
directly with the rightholder (Case C-128/11, UsedSoft GmbH v Oracle International Corp., paras.
45 to 49, 77, 80 to 85). State the transfer module above for this arrangement, and require the
transferring party to render their own retained copy unusable at the time of transfer, since the
reselling party keeps infringing the separate reproduction right otherwise (Case C-128/11, paras.
70, 78 to 79, 87). A maintenance or update agreement bundled with the sale does not on its own
defeat this exhaustion, but once an update merges permanently into the copy, exhaustion extends to
the updated copy rather than staying confined to the copy as first delivered (Case C-128/11, paras.
66 to 68). A group
or volume licence may be resold as a whole, or in whole surplus units, but a licensee cannot divide
it below the contracted minimum unit and resell part of it (Case C-128/11, paras. 69 to 71, 86).

Where the arrangement is instead genuinely time-limited, or charges a recurring fee tied to
continued use, the sale classification and UsedSoft exhaustion do not apply on the same terms; drop
the transfer module for that arrangement, since the Software Directive does not extend exhaustion
to it on the authority read here.

### EU business customers

Use the same restricted-act list, user-side exceptions, and exhaustion treatment as the EU
consumers module: the Software Directive does not distinguish a consumer licensee from a business
licensee on the scope of the grant, the acts reserved to the rightholder, or the exceptions a
licence cannot remove (Art. 4(1), 5(2), 5(3), 6(1), (2) Directive 2009/24/EC; Case C-128/11). Do not
narrow the back-up, study, or decompilation exceptions for a business licensee: the Directive gives
them to the lawful acquirer or lawful user as such, not to a consumer specifically.

### Germany

Track the same four restricted acts under UrhG's own numbering, since the German transposition
lists them separately from the Directive's three: reproduction, including the incidental
reproduction loading, displaying, running, transmitting, or storing the program requires (§ 69c Nr.
1 UrhG); translation, adaptation, arrangement, and other alteration, and reproduction of the
result, without prejudice to the rights of the person who carries out the alteration (§ 69c Nr. 2
UrhG); distribution, including rental, with distribution (but never rental) exhausting
once a copy is put into circulation by sale with the rightholder's consent inside the EU or another
EEA state (§ 69c Nr. 3 UrhG); and public communication, including making the program available so
members of the public can access it from a place and at a time they individually choose (§ 69c Nr.
4 UrhG).

Keep the same user-side exceptions a clause cannot override: a back-up copy necessary to secure
future use (§ 69d Abs. 2 UrhG); observing, studying, or testing through acts the licensee is
entitled to carry out (§ 69d Abs. 3 UrhG); and decompilation once the three conditions of § 69e Abs.
1 are met, limited to the two use restrictions of § 69e Abs. 2, both read so that they neither
impair normal exploitation of the program nor unreasonably prejudice [trader name]'s legitimate
interests (§ 69e Abs. 3 UrhG). A contract term in conflict with any of these is void (nichtig), not
merely unenforceable against the licensee who invokes it (§ 69g Abs. 2 UrhG). The one provision left open to a contrary agreement is § 69d Abs. 1 (reproduction and
alteration necessary for intended use, including error correction): state whether the licence
narrows this default, the same point the EU module raises for Art. 5(1).

Where the software is work product from an employee or a contractor under a service relationship,
state who holds the economic rights in it rather than leaving the point to the statutory default:
absent a contrary agreement, every economic right in a program an employee creates in carrying out
their duties, or on instruction, vests in the employer alone, and the same follows for a comparable
service relationship (§ 69b Abs. 1, 2 UrhG). A development or work-for-hire agreement that states
this expressly removes the point from later dispute; a silent agreement leaves it to the default.

Where the licence is a permanent grant for a one-off fee, the EU module's exhaustion and transfer
treatment applies here too, since § 69c Nr. 3 Satz 2 UrhG states the same EU-law exhaustion rule for
a sale inside the EU or EEA with the rightholder's consent, while confirming the rental right alone
never exhausts.

### US

State the licensee's statutory floor under copyright law only where the licensee is the owner of
the copy, since 17 U.S.C. § 117 is the US equivalent exception, available in parallel with, not
instead of, whatever the licence itself grants: a copy owner may make or authorise one new copy or
adaptation that is either an essential step to run the program and used in no other way, or purely
archival and destroyed once possession stops being rightful (17 U.S.C. § 117(a)(1), (a)(2)). This
copy or adaptation may be transferred only together with the original copy and only as part of a
transfer of all rights in the program (17 U.S.C. § 117(b)). Where [the software] is licensed, not
sold, to the licensee, state that plainly and do not rely on § 117 in the grant wording, since the
US exception, unlike the EU reference files above, turns on ownership of the copy rather than on
lawful acquisition or use; a recital that the software is licensed and not sold is doing real work
here.

Where [licensee name] operates a machine that runs [the software] and needs it serviced, a
machine-repair copy made solely because activating the machine triggers it may be used for no
other purpose and must be destroyed immediately after the maintenance or repair work finishes;
state "maintenance" as servicing the machine to its original specifications or any authorised
change to them, and "repair" as restoring it to that state (17 U.S.C. § 117(c)(1), (c)(2), (d)(1),
(d)(2)).

Reserve to [trader name] the rights 17 U.S.C. § 512 protects for an online service provider only
where [the software] is a hosting, caching, linking, or user-content product and the conditions
that provision sets are actually met; this clause does not restate them (see
`references/us-federal/copyright.md`).

## Must not

- No licence clause may purport to prohibit a back-up copy necessary for the use the licensee is
  entitled to make (Art. 5(2) Directive 2009/24/EC; § 69d Abs. 2, § 69g Abs. 2 UrhG).
- No licence clause may purport to prohibit observing, studying, or testing the program's
  functioning through acts the licensee is otherwise entitled to carry out, to work out the ideas
  and principles behind it (Art. 5(3) Directive 2009/24/EC; § 69d Abs. 3, § 69g Abs. 2 UrhG).
- No licence clause may purport to remove the decompilation right once its conditions are met;
  such a term is void, not merely read down (Art. 8, second paragraph, Directive 2009/24/EC; § 69e,
  § 69g Abs. 2 UrhG).
- No licence clause may rely on a "non-transferable" label, alone, to block resale once a one-off
  fee and an unlimited-duration right to use the copy together meet the Art. 4(2) sale
  classification; the label does not change that classification (Case C-128/11, paras. 49, 77, 82
  to 83).
- No licence clause may claim economic rights for the employer or licensor beyond what § 69b UrhG
  actually confers: that provision shifts rights only for an employee- or service-provider-created
  program, carried out in that capacity, and only absent a contrary agreement (§ 69b Abs. 1, 2
  UrhG).
- No US clause may hold out a § 117 copy right as part of the licence grant where the licensee does
  not own the copy; that section protects a copy owner, not a licensee as such (17 U.S.C. §
  117(a)).

## Drafting notes

See conflicts.md entry C-5 for the broader as-is disclaimer and liability cap pattern a US template
commonly carries alongside its licence grant; this clause does not restate that entry's reasoning,
only the grant and its limits.

A recital that the grant is a licence, not a sale, is not mere labeling: in the United States it is
the one lever that keeps § 117 and first-sale exhaustion from attaching to the copy at all, so a US
licence that wants to restrict transfer, sub-hosting, or redeployment needs that recital stated
plainly and paired with real restrictions on use and transfer, not left implicit. The EU reads the
same recital differently: a one-off fee plus an unlimited-duration grant is read as a sale for
exhaustion purposes whatever the contract calls itself, so the same recital that does real work in a
US module does not, by itself, stop EU exhaustion from applying once those two facts are present.
Draft the US and EU modules knowing they reach opposite results from a similar-looking grant clause,
and do not assume this wording travels between them.

Where software is delivered as a cloud or hosted service rather than as a copy the licensee
installs, the licensee receives no copy at all, so neither the EU back-up and study exceptions nor
the US § 117 copy right have anything to attach to; draft the grant for that arrangement as a right
to access and use the service, not a right to install and run a copy, and keep sub-hosting,
redistribution of access credentials, and service-bureau use as express restrictions in the grant
itself rather than relying on any copy-based exception to stop them, since a hosted deployment may
not trigger the same reproduction or distribution rights a copy-based deployment does.

A clause that restricts reselling, sub-licensing, or redeploying the software states that
restriction for what it actually is (a contractual limit on use, not a claim that the statutory
back-up, study, or decompilation rights do not apply), since drafting the restriction as if it
reached those statutory rights would itself fall under Must not above.
