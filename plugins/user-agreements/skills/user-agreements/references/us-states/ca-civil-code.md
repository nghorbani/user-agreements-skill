# California Civil Code: exculpatory clauses, unconscionability, and the Consumers Legal Remedies Act

Citation: Cal. Civ. Code § 1668; § 1670.5; §§ 1750 to 1756 (Consumers Legal Remedies Act, Chapter 1 general provisions)
Version: § 1668 unchanged since its 1872 enactment; § 1670.5 unchanged since Stats. 1979, Ch. 819; §§ 1750, 1753 to 1755 and 1751 as enacted by Stats. 1970, Ch. 1550, § 1752 as amended by Stats. 1975, Ch. 615
Official text: https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codesTOCSelected.xhtml?tocCode=CIV
Law as of: 2026-10-20

## When it applies

- § 1668 applies to all contracts, with no limit to consumer contracts, contracts of adhesion, or any particular industry. The test looks at a clause's object, including an object reached only indirectly, not at its label: a limitation-of-liability clause, release, waiver, or disclaimer in a terms of service document or a EULA falls within § 1668 if its effect is to exempt a party from responsibility for one of three things (§ 1668).
- § 1670.5 applies once a court is asked, or itself finds, that a contract or any clause of it was unconscionable at the time it was made; the finding is made as a matter of law and measured at formation, not at the time of the dispute (§ 1670.5(a)).
- §§ 1750 to 1756 are the general provisions of the Consumers Legal Remedies Act (CLRA), Civil Code Title 1.5 (§§ 1750 to 1784). § 1751's waiver bar reaches the whole title rather than this chapter alone, so a clause purporting to waive any CLRA right or remedy anywhere in §§ 1750 to 1784 falls within it, whether or not the clause calls itself a waiver (§ 1751).
- The CLRA title does not apply to a transaction for the construction, sale, or construction and sale of an entire residence or of all or part of a structure built for commercial or industrial occupancy, with or without land, or to the sale of a lot or parcel of real property, including site preparation incidental to that sale (§ 1754).
- The CLRA title does not reach the owners or employees of an advertising medium (newspapers, magazines, broadcast stations, billboards, transit ads, among others) who publish or disseminate an advertisement that violates the title, unless it is established that they knew of the deceptive methods, acts, or practices made unlawful by § 1770 (§ 1755).
- The CLRA's substantive and procedural provisions apply only to actions filed on or after January 1, 1971 (§ 1756).
- McGill v. Citibank, N.A. (Cal. Supreme Ct., No. S224086, decided April 6, 2017) applies specifically to a predispute arbitration clause in a consumer agreement that purports, in any forum, to waive a consumer's statutory right to seek public injunctive relief under the CLRA, the unfair competition law (UCL, Bus. & Prof. Code § 17200 et seq.), or the false advertising law (Bus. & Prof. Code § 17500 et seq.). Public injunctive relief is relief whose primary purpose and effect is to stop unlawful conduct that threatens future injury to the general public, benefiting the plaintiff who requests it, if at all, only incidentally; it is distinct from private injunctive relief, which mainly resolves the plaintiff's own dispute (McGill, slip op. at 5 to 6).

## What the documents must say

- Where a clause releases, disclaims, or limits liability, state the carve-out plainly rather than leaving it to be read in: the clause does not exempt a party from its own fraud, from willful injury to another's person or property, or from a violation of law, whether willful or negligent (§ 1668).
- Where an arbitration clause in a consumer agreement requires individual (non-class, non-representative) arbitration of claims and bars an arbitrator from awarding relief to anyone but the named parties, state separately that the clause does not require arbitration of, and does not waive in any forum, a request for public injunctive relief under the CLRA, the UCL, or the false advertising law. A clause may still send a consumer's individual damages and other individual claims to arbitration, with that arbitration proceeding unaffected by any separate litigation of the inarbitrable public-injunctive-relief claim, without running into McGill's holding (McGill, slip op. at 2, 20).

## What the documents must not say

- Do not write a release, disclaimer, limitation-of-liability, or waiver clause, however labeled, whose effect is to exempt a party from responsibility for its own fraud, for willful injury to the person or property of another, or for a violation of law, whether willful or negligent; such a clause is against the policy of the law regardless of its object being reached directly or only indirectly (§ 1668).
- Do not rely on a clause's label (release, waiver, disclaimer, limitation of liability) to put it outside § 1668: the statute looks to what the clause does, not what it calls itself (§ 1668).
- Do not include a clause that a court could find unconscionable as a matter of law at the time the contract was made; an unconscionable clause exposes the whole contract to nonenforcement, beyond the clause itself, at the court's choice (§ 1670.5(a)).
- Do not write a clause requiring a California consumer to waive any right or remedy the CLRA gives; such a clause is unenforceable and void regardless of the words used, because the bar reaches the whole CLRA title (§ 1751).
- Do not draft a predispute arbitration clause that purports to waive a consumer's right to seek public injunctive relief under the CLRA, the UCL, or the false advertising law in any forum, arbitration, court, or otherwise. The California Supreme Court holds this unenforceable under state law and holds that the Federal Arbitration Act does not preempt the state rule that invalidates it (McGill, slip op. at 1, 14 to 16).
- Do not assume a classwide-arbitration-procedure waiver and a public-injunctive-relief waiver stand or fall together under federal preemption law. The California Supreme Court in McGill distinguishes the two: a procedural waiver of class arbitration is a matter the Federal Arbitration Act leaves to the parties' agreement, but a waiver of the substantive right to pursue a public-interest statutory remedy at all is not (McGill, slip op. at 18 to 20, discussing AT&T Mobility LLC v. Concepcion, 563 U.S. 333 (2011), and American Express Co. v. Italian Colors Restaurant, 570 U.S. __ (2013)).

## Product behaviour it assumes

- Whenever a limitation-of-liability, release, disclaimer, or similar clause is drafted or reviewed for a California consumer or any other California-governed contract, it is checked against the object-based test of § 1668 rather than read for its label alone (§ 1668).
- Whenever a standard-form clause is reviewed for California enforceability, the review asks whether a court could find it unconscionable at formation, since an adverse finding lets the court strike the whole contract, sever the clause, or narrow it, at the court's choice rather than the drafter's (§ 1670.5(a)).
- Where unconscionability is raised or appears to the court, the parties can present evidence of the clause's commercial setting, purpose, and effect; a drafter keeps records supporting that setting and purpose, since the statute makes that evidentiary opportunity part of the determination, not optional (§ 1670.5(b)).
- Any arbitration clause presented to a California consumer under the CLRA, the UCL, or the false advertising law is checked, as a distinct step from the clause's general enforceability, for whether it purports to foreclose public-injunctive-relief requests in every forum; if it does, that part of the clause is treated as unenforceable under California law independent of any Federal Arbitration Act argument (McGill, slip op. at 14 to 16).
- Where an arbitration clause is updated over time (as Citibank's was, across a 2001, a 2005, and a 2007 version), the clause's severability language is reviewed each time a public-injunctive-relief carve-out is added or changed, since whether the rest of the clause survives striking one part depends on exactly what that severability language says, and differing versions of the same clause have produced different answers (McGill, slip op. at 20 to 21).

## Prescribed wording

None. §§ 1668, 1670.5, and 1751 to 1756 state substantive and remedial rules; they do not fix verbatim words a contract must use.

## Dates

- § 1668 was enacted in 1872 as part of Title 4 (Unlawful Contracts, §§ 1667 to 1670.15) and carries no later amendment in the official text.
- § 1670.5 was added by Stats. 1979, Ch. 819, and is unchanged since.
- § 1750, § 1753, § 1754, and § 1755 were added by Stats. 1970, Ch. 1550, the CLRA's original enactment, as was § 1751 itself. § 1752 was later amended by Stats. 1975, Ch. 615. The CLRA's substantive and procedural provisions apply only to actions filed on or after January 1, 1971 (§ 1756).
- McGill v. Citibank, N.A. was decided April 6, 2017 by the California Supreme Court, No. S224086, and reverses the Court of Appeal's judgment.

## Open points to raise

- Whether a specific limitation-of-liability or release clause in the draft reaches, even indirectly, one of § 1668's three categories (own fraud, willful injury to person or property, violation of law whether willful or negligent); the statute's object-based test cannot be answered from the clause text alone without knowing what the clause is meant to cover in practice.
- Whether a specific standard-form clause carries features a court could find unconscionable at formation under § 1670.5(a); the statute sets the remedy and the evidentiary process but no checklist of unconscionable features, so this needs a case-by-case read against the clause's commercial setting, purpose, and effect.
- Whether the CLRA applies to the product or transaction at hand at all. In McGill, the defendant raised an argument that the CLRA does not apply to credit cards, but raised it too late for the court to decide, so that question remains open rather than settled by this case (McGill, slip op. at 9 to 10, fn. 2).
- Whether the specific relief an arbitration or class-waiver clause would foreclose actually qualifies as public injunctive relief under the CLRA, UCL, or false advertising law, as opposed to private injunctive relief that only resolves an individual dispute; McGill treats this as a merits question that depends on the facts of the claim, not something the clause's wording alone decides.
- Whether a given clause's severability language would, if a public-injunctive-relief waiver inside it were struck, take the rest of the clause down too. McGill expressly leaves this open on facts where differently worded severability clauses were used across time, so a current clause's own severability wording needs its own check rather than reliance on how McGill came out.
- Whether any later California or U.S. Supreme Court decision has narrowed the McGill holding, or any statute enacted since has displaced the underlying CLRA, UCL, or false advertising law remedy for the transaction type being drafted for, since the case itself flags this as a standing check before relying on it for a new clause.
