# California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA) and its regulations

Citation: Cal. Civ. Code 1798.100 to 1798.199.100, as amended; 11 Cal. Code Regs. 7000 to 7304
Version: statute as in effect 2026-01-01 (AB 137 and AB 566 update), compiled by the California
Privacy Protection Agency in December 2025; regulations as in effect 1 January 2026
Official text: https://cppa.ca.gov/regulations/
Law as of: 2026-10-20

## When it applies

- Applies to a "business": a for-profit entity doing business in California that decides the
  purposes and means of processing a consumer's personal information and meets one of three
  thresholds: over $25,000,000 in prior-year gross revenue (adjusted for inflation every
  odd-numbered year from 2025, see Dates); buying, selling, or sharing 100,000 or more
  consumers' or households' personal information a year; or deriving 50% or more of annual
  revenue from selling or sharing personal information (1798.140(d)). A commonly branded
  affiliate sharing data with a qualifying business, certain 40%-plus joint ventures, and a
  voluntary CPPA certifier not otherwise covered also count as a business (1798.140(d)(2) to (4)).
  A product that has not crossed one of these thresholds is outside the CCPA; check the
  thresholds before drafting any CCPA-specific clause.
- "Consumer" means a natural person who is a California resident (1798.140(i)); the CCPA does
  not turn on how the data was collected, electronic or not, and reaches all personal
  information a business collects from consumers by any means (1798.175).
- "Personal information" is information that identifies, relates to, describes, or could
  reasonably be linked with a consumer or household, across eleven listed categories (identifiers,
  certain Section 1798.80(e) information, protected classifications, commercial information,
  biometric information, internet or network activity, geolocation, audio/visual/thermal/
  olfactory information, professional or employment information, education information, and
  inferences), plus sensitive personal information as its own category (1798.140(v)). It excludes
  publicly available information, lawfully obtained truthful information of public concern, and
  deidentified or aggregate consumer information (1798.140(v)(2) to (3)).
- "Sensitive personal information" covers government ID numbers, account log-in or financial
  account credentials, precise geolocation, racial or ethnic origin, citizenship or immigration
  status, religious or philosophical beliefs, union membership, the contents of mail, email, or
  text messages (unless the business is the intended recipient), genetic data, neural data
  (information from measuring nervous-system activity, not inferred from other data), biometric
  information used to uniquely identify a consumer, health-related information, and sex life or
  sexual orientation information (1798.140(ae)(1) to (2)). Publicly available sensitive personal
  information does not count as sensitive, or as personal, information (1798.140(ae)(3)).
- "Sell" and "share" are broad and do not turn on the label a document uses. "Sell" covers
  selling, renting, releasing, disclosing, disseminating, making available, transferring, or
  otherwise communicating personal information to a third party for money or other value
  (1798.140(ad)). "Share" covers the same list of acts for cross-context behavioral advertising,
  whether or not for consideration (1798.140(ah)). Neither applies where the consumer directs
  the disclosure, where an identifier is used solely to flag that the consumer opted out, or in an
  asset transfer within a merger, acquisition, or bankruptcy consistent with the title, with prior
  notice of a material change (1798.140(ad)(2), (ah)(2)). "Cross-context behavioral advertising"
  means targeting ads based on a consumer's activity across businesses or distinctly branded
  sites or apps other than the one the consumer intentionally interacts with (1798.140(k)).
  Mislabeling a cross-context-advertising transfer as a mere "service" does not change its
  classification, and courts and the agency must disregard steps taken to avoid the sell/share
  definition, including stripping out monetary consideration while a party still gets something of
  value (1798.190).
- "Consent" means a freely given, specific, informed, unambiguous indication of wishes, by
  statement or clear affirmative action, for a narrowly defined purpose. Accepting broad,
  bundled terms of use is not consent; hovering, muting, pausing, or closing content is not
  consent; and agreement obtained through a dark pattern is not consent (1798.140(h)). A "dark
  pattern" is a user interface designed or manipulated with the substantial effect of subverting
  or impairing a consumer's autonomy, decisionmaking, or choice (1798.140(l)); a business that
  knows an interface has that effect and does not fix it is not thereby excused (7 CCR 7004(c)).
- "Service provider" and "contractor" each mean a person processing personal information on a
  business's behalf under a written contract that bars selling or sharing it, bars retaining,
  using, or disclosing it outside the contracted purpose or the direct business relationship, bars
  combining it with other data except where the CCPA allows, requires the recipient to certify
  it understands and will comply, and lets the business monitor compliance at least annually
  (1798.140(ag), (j)). A "third party" is anyone who is not the business the consumer
  intentionally interacts with, a service provider, or a contractor (1798.140(ai)). A vendor
  without a qualifying contract in place is not a service provider, contractor, or authorized third
  party, and the disclosure to it may itself be a sale or sharing that triggers opt-out rights
  (7 CCR 7050(e)). A service provider or contractor that is itself a business, and that collects,
  maintains, or sells personal information outside its contracted role, must comply with the
  CCPA directly for that outside-role data (7 CCR 7050(d)).
- The employee-context exemption (job applicant, employee, owner, director, officer, medical
  staff, independent contractor, emergency-contact, and benefits-administration data used in
  that capacity) and the B2B due-diligence or transaction-context exemption both became
  inoperative on 1 January 2023 by their own sunset terms (1798.145(m)(4), (n)(3)); ordinary
  employee and B2B contact data is now within the CCPA's general obligations. While the
  employee exemption was in force it never excluded the notice-at-collection duty (1798.100(a))
  or the security-breach private right of action (1798.150); the B2B exemption carried no
  comparable exception (1798.145(n)).
- Exemptions that still apply, each scoped to what it names: compliance with law, legal process,
  or an emergency government request under a three-part safeguard (reproductive-care access
  requests do not qualify as an emergency) (1798.145(a)(1)); where compliance would violate a
  California evidentiary privilege, for the rights and disclosure sections it lists (1798.145(b));
  HIPAA-governed medical information and covered providers, and Common Rule or FDA clinical
  trial data not sold or shared outside those rules (1798.145(c), 1798.146); Fair Credit Reporting
  Act, Gramm-Leach-Bliley or California Financial Information Privacy Act, Farm Credit Act, and
  Driver's Privacy Protection Act data, each still subject to the security-breach private right of
  action (1798.145(d) to (f)); vehicle or vessel data shared between a dealer and manufacturer
  solely for a warranty repair or safety recall (1798.145(g)); deidentified or aggregate consumer
  information (1798.145(a)(1)(F)); and household data, for the delete, correct, know, and sale/
  share-disclosure rights only, not for the notice-at-collection duty or the security-breach right
  (1798.145(p)). A business relying on an exemption should cite the specific ground; a blanket
  statement that data may be retained as permitted by law does not itself establish which
  exemption applies if challenged.
- An insurance company that meets the CCPA business definition must comply with the CCPA for
  personal information not subject to the Insurance Code and its regulations, for example
  marketing-site visitor data or employee and applicant data; personal information collected as
  part of an insurance transaction, as defined in Insurance Code 791.02, stays outside the CCPA
  (7 CCR 7270(a), 7271(a)).
- Collection, use, retention, and sharing must stay reasonably necessary and proportionate to
  the purpose disclosed at collection, or to another purpose compatible with that context
  (1798.100(c); 7 CCR 7002(a)). Compatibility and proportionality are judged against the
  consumer's relationship with the business, the type and amount of data, its source, the clarity
  of the business's disclosures, and how visible service providers or third parties are in the
  collection (7 CCR 7002(b) to (c)). A business must not collect categories beyond what its
  Notice at Collection discloses, or use data for an incompatible purpose, without a fresh notice
  (1798.100(a); 7 CCR 7002(f)).

## What the documents must say

- At or before collection: the categories of personal information collected and the purposes for
  collecting or using them, whether each category is sold or shared, the same for sensitive
  personal information if collected, and the retention period for each category or the criteria
  for setting it (1798.100(a)(1) to (3)). A third-party business controlling collection on its own
  premises, including in a vehicle, must disclose this clearly and conspicuously at that location,
  or, if collecting through its own website, post it prominently on its homepage (1798.100(b)).
- A written contract whenever a business sells, shares, or discloses personal information to a
  third party, service provider, or contractor for a business purpose: it must name the limited
  and specific purposes, bind the recipient to comply with the CCPA and give the data the same
  level of privacy protection, let the business take reasonable steps to check compliance, require
  the recipient to notify the business if it can no longer meet its obligations, and let the business
  take reasonable steps to stop and remediate unauthorized use on notice (1798.100(d)(1) to (5)).
  The service-provider and contractor contract must also prohibit combining the data with data
  from other sources except as the CCPA allows, require the recipient's certification of
  understanding and compliance, and flow the same terms down to any subcontractor
  (1798.140(ag), (j); 7 CCR 7051(a)). The third-party contract must instead require the third
  party to use the data only for the named purpose, provide the business's own level of privacy
  protection such as honoring a forwarded opt-out, and let the business confirm compliant use
  and stop unauthorized use on notice (7 CCR 7053(a)).
- A privacy policy, updated at least every 12 months: the categories of personal information
  collected in the preceding 12 months (using the statutory category terms), categories of
  sources, the business or commercial purpose of collection, categories sold or shared (or a
  statement that none were) with the third-party recipient categories and the purpose, categories
  disclosed to service providers or contractors for a business purpose (or a statement that none
  were) with the purpose, whether sensitive personal information is used or disclosed outside the
  listed authorized purposes, an explanation of each CCPA right and how to exercise it (including
  links to request forms, the verification process in general terms, how opt-out preference
  signals are handled, and how an authorized agent can act), a contact channel matching how the
  business primarily interacts with consumers, and the date of the policy's last update
  (1798.130(a)(5); 7 CCR 7011(c) to (e)). The policy must be reachable from a conspicuous
  homepage link using the word "privacy," or, for a mobile app, from the app's download page
  and from inside the app, such as its settings menu (7 CCR 7011(c) to (d), 7003(d)).
- At least two designated methods for requests to delete, correct, or know, including at minimum
  a toll-free number, plus a webform if the business has a website; an online-only business
  dealing directly with the consumer need only give an email address (1798.130(a)(1); 7 CCR
  7020(a) to (b)). The same two-or-more-method rule, chosen for how the business interacts with
  consumers, applies to opt-out and limit requests, with at least one method matching the
  business's primary channel; an online business must support at minimum an opt-out
  preference signal plus one more method, such as an interactive form (7 CCR 7026(a); 7025).
  Account creation must never be a condition of exercising any of these rights (1798.130(a)
  (2)(A); 7 CCR 7026(c), 7027(d)).
- A clear and conspicuous homepage link titled "Do Not Sell or Share My Personal Information"
  and one titled "Limit the Use of My Sensitive Personal Information," or, at the business's
  choice, a single combined link; both links must sit in the header or footer and match the
  business's other homepage links in size and prominence (1798.135(a)(1) to (3); 7 CCR 7013(c),
  7014(c)). A business may instead post one Alternative Opt-out Link with the prescribed title
  and icon in place of both (7 CCR 7015(a) to (b); see Prescribed wording). A business need not
  post either the sale/sharing link or the sensitive-information link if it honors a compliant opt-out
  preference signal and processes it frictionlessly instead (1798.135(b); 7 CCR 7025(e) to (g)),
  provided its privacy policy discloses the right, the frictionless processing, how to enable the
  signal, and any other method offered.
- A fresh Notice at Collection whenever collection expands to a new category or an incompatible
  new purpose, given where the consumer encounters the collection: a link on the relevant
  webpage, a notice near a webform's input fields, a link on a mobile app's download page and
  inside the app, paper notice or signage offline, or oral notice for a phone or in-person collection
  (7 CCR 7012(c)). If no such notice is given, the business must not collect the information
  (7 CCR 7012(d)).
- A Notice of Financial Incentive wherever a business offers a financial incentive, a price or
  service difference, or payment tied to data: a succinct summary of the incentive, its material
  terms including the data categories involved and the value of the consumer's data, how to opt
  in, the right to withdraw at any time and how, and an explanation of how the price or service
  difference is reasonably related to that value, including a good-faith estimate and the method
  used (7 CCR 7016(d); 1798.125(b)(1) to (2)).
- A Pre-use Notice before using automated decisionmaking technology (ADMT) for a significant
  decision about a consumer (financial or lending services, housing, education enrollment or
  opportunities, employment or independent-contracting opportunities or compensation, or
  healthcare services, 7 CCR 7001(ddd)): a plain-language, non-generic explanation of the
  ADMT's specific purpose; the right to opt out and how to request it, or, where the business
  relies on the human-appeal exception, how to appeal, or, for another exception, which one;
  the right to access ADMT and how to request it; a non-retaliation statement; and, available at
  least behind a link, how the ADMT processes data to reach the decision, what the output is and
  how it feeds the decision including whether it is the sole factor and what role a human plays,
  and what the alternative process looks like for a consumer who opts out (7 CCR 7220(b) to
  (c)). This detail need not include trade secrets, as defined in Civil Code 3426.1(d), or
  information that would undercut security-incident response, resisting fraud, or physical safety
  (7 CCR 7220(d)).
- For a product scoring, ranking, recommending, or screening consumers with an automated or
  AI-driven feature, including a recommendation engine, a credit or eligibility score, or a risk flag
  that meets the "significant decision" test above, the consumer's access and opt-out rights over
  that use, and an explanation of the decisionmaking logic and likely outcome, belong in the
  document rather than being left unaddressed (7 CCR 7001(ddd), 7220(a), 7221(a), 7222(b)).

## What the documents must not say

- No clause purporting to waive or limit any right, remedy, or enforcement mechanism under the
  CCPA, including a representative-action waiver; see Prescribed wording for the statute's own
  text (1798.192). An arbitration clause or class-action waiver reaching CCPA rights is void on
  this ground.
- No clause describing collection or use purposes so broadly that a later, undisclosed use could
  be called "incompatible" with what was disclosed; a broad catch-all purpose clause risks
  failing both the notice duty and the proportionality test (1798.100(a)(1) to (2), (c)).
- No bundled consent: a single checkbox agreeing to broad terms of use or a privacy policy while
  also covering unrelated processing purposes is not consent under the CCPA, even if it is the
  only mechanism offered (1798.140(h); 7 CCR 7004(a)(4)(B) to (C)).
- No choice architecture that confuses or impairs the consumer's choice: an opt-in flow offering
  only "Yes" and "Ask me later," a banner offering only "Accept All" paired with "More
  Information" instead of a true decline, a toggle or button that states only "on" or "off"
  without clarifying text, or pairing "Do Not Sell or Share My Personal Information" with a bare
  choice of "Yes" or "No" (a prohibited double negative) (7 CCR 7004(a)(2)(B) to (C), (a)(3)(A)
  to (B)). No requiring the consumer to search or scroll through the privacy policy to find the
  opt-out mechanism after clicking the required link, and no circular or broken links or
  unmonitored, heavily filtered contact channels (7 CCR 7004(a)(5)(A) to (B)). Any of these
  failures may make a resulting consent a dark pattern, and consent obtained through a dark
  pattern is treated as never given (1798.140(l); 7 CCR 7004(b) to (c)).
- No describing data as "anonymized" or "deidentified" unless it actually meets all three
  conditions: reasonable measures against re-association, a public commitment not to
  reidentify, and contractual flow-down of that commitment (1798.140(m)). Loosely calling data
  "anonymous" without meeting these conditions keeps it within "personal information."
  Reidentifying, or attempting to reidentify, deidentified patient data is itself barred outside a
  short list of purposes (treatment, payment, health care operations, public health activity,
  Common Rule research, a bounded reidentification-testing engagement with destruction
  required after, or where otherwise required by law) (1798.148(a)).
- No price or service difference tied to exercising a CCPA right unless the difference is
  reasonably related to the value the consumer's data provides to the business, calculated in
  good faith and documented; without that calculation and relationship, the business must not
  offer the difference (1798.125(a)(1) to (2); 7 CCR 7080(a) to (b)). Denying a request for a
  reason the CCPA permits is never, by itself, discrimination (7 CCR 7080(c)).
- No limiting the opt-out or limit-use mechanism to the medium of first consent, no demanding
  account creation or information beyond what identifies the consumer, and no requiring a
  verifiable request to exercise the opt-out, the limit, or the ADMT opt-out (1798.135(c)(1);
  7 CCR 7026(c) to (d), 7027(d) to (e), 7221(e) to (f)). A cookie banner or cookie controls alone
  never satisfy the opt-out-of-sale/sharing, limit, or ADMT-opt-out method requirement, because
  cookies concern collection, not sale, sharing, or ADMT use (7 CCR 7026(a)(4), 7221(c)(4)).
- No retaliating against a consumer, applicant, or independent contractor for exercising a CCPA
  right, and no retaliation specifically for exercising the ADMT opt-out or access right
  (1798.125(a)(1)(E); 7 CCR 7221(l), 7222(k)).
- No contract clause purporting to limit a business's liability protection when it properly
  forwards an opt-out request downstream; such a clause is void by the statute's own terms
  (1798.135(g)).
- No authorizing sale or sharing of a consumer's data where the business has actual knowledge
  the consumer is under 16, without the age-tiered consent chain in the Prescribed wording and
  Product behaviour sections below; willfully disregarding age signals is treated as actual
  knowledge, so not asking is not a shield (1798.120(c)).

## Product behaviour it assumes

- The checkout, account settings, and privacy pages carry two or more designated request
  channels for delete, correct, and know requests (minimum a toll-free number, or an email
  address for a qualifying online-only business) and two or more for opt-out and limit requests,
  with at least one matching the business's primary channel (1798.130(a)(1); 7 CCR 7020(a) to
  (c), 7026(a), 7027(b) to (c)).
- The request pipeline acknowledges a delete, correct, know, ADMT-access, or ADMT-appeal
  request within 10 business days, describing the verification process and expected response
  time unless already granted or denied; the substantive response is due within 45 calendar days
  of receipt, extendable once by 45 more days with notice and reason given within the first
  window (1798.130(a)(2)(A); 7 CCR 7021(a) to (b)).
- Identity verification runs on a tiered standard: no verification at all for opt-out of sale/
  sharing, limit, or ADMT opt-out requests, only non-burdensome information such as a name
  (7 CCR 7060(b)); a reasonable degree of certainty, which may mean matching at least two data
  points, for requests to know categories (7 CCR 7062(b)); and a reasonably high degree of
  certainty, which may mean matching at least three data points plus a signed declaration under
  penalty of perjury, for requests to know specific pieces or to access ADMT (7 CCR 7062(c)).
  The business never charges a verification fee, and must not use a disputed fact, such as a
  disputed address, to verify a request to correct that same fact (7 CCR 7060(e), (h)).
- For a delete, correct, or know request submitted through an authorized agent, the business may
  require the agent to prove the consumer gave signed permission, and may also require the
  consumer either to verify their own identity directly or to confirm directly that they gave the
  agent permission, but must not require the consumer to resubmit the request in their own
  capacity (7 CCR 7063(a)). None of this applies where the consumer gave the agent power of
  attorney under Probate Code 4121 to 4130, and the business must not demand power of
  attorney as a condition of letting a consumer use an authorized agent (7 CCR 7063(b)).
- A deletion request erases the data from active systems, or deidentifies or aggregates it;
  directs service providers and contractors to delete what they hold; and notifies every third
  party the data was sold or shared with, cascading down the chain, unless impossible or
  disproportionate, in which case the consumer gets a detailed, fact-based explanation rather
  than a bare assertion (1798.105(c)(1); 7 CCR 7022(b) to (c)). Archived or backup copies may
  wait until next restored or accessed (7 CCR 7022(d)). If a deletion request is denied and the
  consumer has not already opted out of sale/sharing, the system prompts the opt-out offer
  (7 CCR 7022(g)).
- A correction request is evaluated on the totality of the circumstances and may be denied if the
  business decides the contested information is more likely than not accurate; if granted, the
  correction is fixed against later overwrites, such as a refreshed data-broker feed, and pushed
  to service providers and contractors (7 CCR 7023(b) to (c)).
- Opt-out and limit requests are honored no later than 15 business days after receipt, with notice
  cascaded to every third party already holding the data, directing further compliance down the
  chain (7 CCR 7026(f), 7027(g)); an ADMT opt-out received after processing started is honored
  the same way, within 15 business days, with notice to every service provider, contractor, or
  other recipient of the data used for that ADMT (7 CCR 7221(n)). The system shows the
  consumer the opt-out took effect, and treats the absence of a later signal as never meaning
  consent to opt back in (7 CCR 7025(c)(5) to (6), 7026(g)). A business honors an opt-out, or a
  minor's non-consent, for at least 12 months before asking again (1798.135(c)(4) to (5); 7 CCR
  7026(k), 7027(l), 7221(k)); re-enrollment in sale/sharing, or in a financial incentive program, or
  opting back into ADMT, needs the two-step opt-in-then-confirm process (7 CCR 7028(a)).
- A compliant opt-out preference signal, sent in a commonly recognized format such as an HTTP
  header field or JavaScript object, is applied to the browser or device and any associated
  profile, including pseudonymous profiles, and to the consumer's account where the business
  can connect the signal to a known consumer, without demanding more information than the
  signal itself requires (7 CCR 7025(c)(1) to (2)). The business and the signal-sending platform
  use any data collected while processing the signal only for that purpose (7 CCR 7025(d)).
  Processing a signal frictionlessly means no fee, no change to the consumer's product
  experience, and no pop-up, interstitial, or similar response beyond showing the opt-out status
  (7 CCR 7025(f)).
- Age-tiered consent for selling or sharing a minor's data: for a consumer the business has
  actual knowledge is under 13, a documented, reasonable method confirms a parent or
  guardian consents (a signed form under penalty of perjury, a payment method that notifies the
  account holder of each transaction, a toll-free or video call with trained staff, an in-person
  meeting with trained staff, or checking government ID against a database and deleting it
  promptly after), on top of whatever COPPA already requires, followed by telling the parent or
  guardian about the right to opt the child back out (7 CCR 7070(a) to (b)). For a consumer at
  least 13 and under 16, a reasonable two-step opt-in process applies, followed by telling the
  consumer about the ongoing right to opt out (7 CCR 7071(a) to (b); 1798.120(c)).
- Starting 1 January 2027, a business that develops or maintains a browser builds in
  configurable functionality letting a consumer send an opt-out preference signal, easy for a
  reasonable person to find and configure, and discloses in public materials how the signal
  works and its intended effect (1798.136(a) to (b)); a browser implementing this is not liable for
  a business's violation on receiving the signal (1798.136(d)).
- A business whose processing presents significant risk to consumer security (meeting the
  revenue-plus-data threshold, or the revenue threshold plus processing 250,000 or more
  consumers' or households' personal information, or 50,000 or more consumers' sensitive
  personal information, in the prior year) runs an annual cybersecurity audit by a qualified,
  independent auditor, assessing 18 listed program components (authentication including
  phishing-resistant multi-factor authentication, encryption at rest and in transit, access
  controls, data inventory, secure configuration, vulnerability testing, audit-log management,
  network monitoring, antivirus, network segmentation, port and protocol controls, security
  awareness and training, secure development practices, vendor oversight, retention and secure
  disposal, incident response, and business continuity, among others), and certifies completion
  to the Agency by 1 April following the covered year, phased in by prior-year revenue (see Dates)
  (7 CCR 7120(a) to (b), 7121(a) to (b), 7123(c)(1) to (18), 7124(a) to (b)).
- A business whose processing presents significant risk to consumer privacy (selling or sharing
  personal information; processing sensitive personal information, with a narrow carve-out for
  data used solely to administer employee or contractor compensation, authorization, benefits,
  legally required accommodation, or wage reporting; using ADMT for a significant decision;
  inferring protected traits from systematic observation of an applicant, student, employee, or
  independent contractor, or from a consumer's presence at a sensitive location; or training an
  ADMT for a significant decision, or training facial-recognition, emotion-recognition, or similar
  profiling technology) completes and documents a risk assessment before starting that
  processing, reviews and updates it at least every three years, updates it within 45 calendar
  days of a material change, retains it for as long as the processing continues or until five years
  after the assessment's completion, whichever is later, and submits the required information
  to the Agency on the schedule in Dates (7 CCR 7150(a) to (b), 7152(a), 7155(a) to (c), 7157(a)).
- A business that makes its ADMT available to another business so that recipient business can
  make a significant decision must give the recipient business all facts available to it that the
  recipient needs for its own risk assessment (7 CCR 7153(a)).
- Service providers and contractors cooperate with the business's cybersecurity audit and risk
  assessment by making available all relevant facts in their possession, custody, or control, and
  help answer a verified ADMT-access request by supplying or enabling access to the data they
  hold under contract (7 CCR 7050(h), 7222(i)).
- Request-handling records, including the request date, nature, submission method, response
  date and nature, and the basis for any denial, are kept for at least 24 months with reasonable
  security, used only to review and improve CCPA compliance, and not shared with third parties
  except to meet a legal obligation (7 CCR 7101(a) to (d)). A business that buys, receives, sells,
  or shares 10 million or more consumers' personal information a year, alone or with affiliates,
  also runs a formal staff training policy and compiles and discloses yearly metrics on requests
  received, granted, and denied by category, plus median or mean response times, by 1 July each
  year, within or linked from the privacy policy (7 CCR 7100(a) to (b), 7102(a) to (b)).

## Prescribed wording

- 1798.135(a)(1): the homepage link, where posted, is titled exactly "Do Not Sell or Share My
  Personal Information".
- 1798.135(a)(2): the companion homepage link, where posted, is titled exactly "Limit the Use
  of My Sensitive Personal Information".
- 7 CCR 7015(b): a business choosing the Alternative Opt-out Link instead of the two separate
  links titles it exactly "Your Privacy Choices," or "Your California Privacy Choices," with the
  prescribed opt-out icon shown adjacent to the title, approximately the same size as the
  business's other header or footer icons (colors may be adjusted only to keep the icon visible
  against the page background).
- 1798.150(c): "The cause of action established by this section shall apply only to violations as
  defined in subdivision (a) and shall not be based on violations of any other section of this
  title." This is the statute's own scope limit on the private right of action; a drafter states it
  accurately if referencing the private right of action at all, rather than asserting a broader or
  narrower scope.
- 1798.192: "Any provision of a contract or agreement of any kind, including a representative
  action waiver, that purports to waive or limit in any way rights under this title, including, but
  not limited to, any right to a remedy or means of enforcement, shall be deemed contrary to
  public policy and shall be void and unenforceable." This governs what a terms document must
  not say, above; it does not stop a consumer from declining to exercise a right or later
  authorizing a sale or share after opting out.
- 1798.148(c): a contract for the sale or license of qualifying deidentified patient information,
  where a party resides or does business in California, must include, or substantially similar
  provisions: "(1) A statement that the deidentified information being sold or licensed includes
  deidentified patient information. (2) A statement that reidentification, and attempted
  reidentification, of the deidentified information by the purchaser or licensee of the information
  is prohibited pursuant to this section. (3) A requirement that, unless otherwise required by
  law, the purchaser or licensee of the deidentified information may not further disclose the
  deidentified information to any third party unless the third party is contractually bound by the
  same or stricter restrictions and conditions."
- 7 CCR 7124(d)(4): the yearly cybersecurity-audit certification to the Agency carries this signed
  attestation: "I attest that I meet the requirements of California Code of Regulations, Title 11,

## Dates

- Statute compiled by the CPPA for the 2026-01-01 effective date (AB 137 and AB 566 update);
  regulations effective 1 January 2026.
- The employee-context exemption and the B2B exemption (1798.145(m), (n)) both became
  inoperative 1 January 2023, by their own sunset terms.
- The browser opt-out-preference-signal duty (1798.136) and the ADMT compliance deadline for
  pre-existing significant-decision use (7 CCR 7200(b)) both begin 1 January 2027; new ADMT
  use for a significant decision must be compliant from the moment it starts.
- Cybersecurity audit report deadlines phase in by prior-year revenue: 1 April 2028 for a
  business with 2026 gross revenue over $100,000,000 as of 1 January 2027 (covering 1 January
  2027 to 1 January 2028); 1 April 2029 for revenue between $50,000,000 and $100,000,000 for
  2027 as of 1 January 2028 (covering 1 January 2028 to 1 January 2029); 1 April 2030 for
  revenue under $50,000,000 for 2028 (covering 1 January 2029 to 1 January 2030). After 1 April
  2030, a business meeting the threshold as of 1 January of a year audits the next 12 months and
  files by 1 April of the year after (7 CCR 7121(a) to (b)).
- Risk assessments for processing already under way before 1 January 2026 and continuing
  after it are due by 31 December 2027 (7 CCR 7155(b)). Agency submission of risk-assessment
  information is due 1 April 2028 for assessments done in 2026 and 2027, then 1 April of the
  year following each later assessment year (7 CCR 7157(a)(1) to (2)).
- The deidentified-patient-data contract-provision duty (1798.148(c)) applies to a contract
  entered on or after 1 January 2021.
- Several dollar figures move with inflation: the $25,000,000 revenue threshold (1798.140(d)(1)
  (A)), the $100 to $750 statutory-damages range (1798.150(a)(1)(A)), the $2,500 and $7,500
  administrative-fine and civil-penalty ranges (1798.155(a), 1798.199.90), and the board per
  diem (1798.199.25) are adjusted by the agency every odd-numbered 1 January starting 2025,
  using the California CPI. Check the agency's current posted figures before quoting a number
  from this file; the figures above are the 2026-01-01 compiled ones, not necessarily current.
- 1798.185(d): civil and administrative enforcement of the 2020 statutory amendments began 1
  July 2023 and reaches only violations on or after that date (historical, carried in the current
  compiled text).

## Open points to raise

- Whether the product has crossed any of the three business thresholds (revenue, data volume,
  or revenue share), since every duty in this file depends on that answer (1798.140(d)).
- Whether a given vendor relationship has a written contract meeting the service-provider,
  contractor, or third-party requirements; without one, a data transfer to that vendor may itself
  be a sale or sharing (7 CCR 7050(e), 7051(a), 7053(a)).
- Whether any feature meets the "significant decision" test for ADMT (financial or lending
  services, housing, education enrollment, employment or contracting opportunities or
  compensation, or healthcare services), which triggers the Pre-use Notice, opt-out, access, and
  risk-assessment duties (7 CCR 7001(ddd), 7200(a)).
- Whether the business meets the 10-million-consumer threshold for the formal training and
  yearly metrics duties, counted alone or with affiliates (7 CCR 7100(b), 7102(a)).
- Whether billing runs through a channel, such as an app store, where the business itself
  cannot verify a consumer's age for the under-16 sale/sharing consent chain, and how that
  consent is then obtained and documented (1798.120(c)).
- Which specific 1798.145 exemption, if any, actually applies to a given category of retained or
  undisclosed data, since relying on one that does not apply leaves the underlying duty in force.
