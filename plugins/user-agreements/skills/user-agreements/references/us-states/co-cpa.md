# Colorado Privacy Act

Citation: C.R.S. 6-1-1301 to 6-1-1314, as amended by HB 24-1058, HB 24-1130, SB 24-041, and SB 25-276
Version: Colorado Revised Statutes 2024, title 6, part 13, through the amendments effective October 1, 2025
Official text: https://content.leg.colorado.gov/sites/default/files/images/olls/crs2024-title-06.pdf
Law as of: 2026-10-20

## When it applies

- Applies to a controller that conducts business in Colorado or produces or delivers commercial products or services intentionally targeted to Colorado residents, and that either controls or processes 100,000 or more consumers' personal data in a calendar year, or derives revenue or a price discount from selling personal data and controls or processes 25,000 or more consumers' personal data (§ 6-1-1304(1)(a)(I)).
- A controller that controls or processes any amount of biometric identifiers or biometric data is covered for that processing regardless of volume, even where it does not meet the general threshold; such a controller must comply with the part only for the biometric identifier or data it collects and processes (§ 6-1-1304(1)(a)(II)).
- From October 1, 2025, §§ 6-1-1305.5, 6-1-1308.5 and 6-1-1309.5 to 6-1-1313 apply to any controller conducting business in or targeting Colorado residents, with no volume threshold at all (§ 6-1-1304(1)(b)).
- "Consumer" means a Colorado resident acting only in an individual or household context; it excludes a person acting in a commercial or employment context, as a job applicant, or as a beneficiary of someone acting in an employment context (§ 6-1-1303(6)).
- "Controller" determines, alone or jointly, the purposes and means of processing; "processor" processes on the controller's behalf (§ 6-1-1303(7), (19)). Whether a person is a controller or processor for a specific activity is fact based: a person not limited by, or that fails to follow, a controller's instructions is itself a controller for that processing, and a processor that starts determining purposes and means becomes a controller (§ 6-1-1305(7)).
- "Child" means under thirteen; "minor" means under eighteen; "adult" means eighteen or older (§ 6-1-1303(4), (16.5), (1), all effective October 1, 2025 except "child").
- The part does not apply to protected health information held by a covered entity or business associate; certain health-care, patient-identifying, and human-subjects-research information under named federal regimes; consumer-report data to the extent regulated by the federal Fair Credit Reporting Act; data under the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act, Driver's Privacy Protection Act, Children's Online Privacy Protection Act, or Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act, to the extent compliant with those laws; employment-record data; named air-carrier, securities-association, and public-utility data; and data a state institution of higher education, the state, the judicial department, or a local government holds for noncommercial purposes authorized by law (§ 6-1-1304(2)(a) to (q)).
- The duties imposed on controllers and processors do not restrict compliance with law, cooperation with legal process, pursuit or defense of legal claims, internal research to improve products, repair of technical errors, internal operations aligned with the consumer's existing relationship, providing a requested product or service, protecting vital interests, preventing or responding to security incidents and fraud, or processing for public-health purposes under confidentiality safeguards (§ 6-1-1304(3)(a)). They do not override an evidentiary privilege under Colorado law, do not reach information a third party makes available that the controller reasonably believes is protected speech, and do not reach an individual's purely personal or household processing (§ 6-1-1304(3)(b) to (e)). From October 1, 2025, a controller need not implement an age-verification or age-gating system, and is not liable for an erroneous age estimate from commercially reasonable age estimation (§ 6-1-1304(3)(f)); the part imposes no obligation that adversely affects First Amendment speech or press rights (§ 6-1-1304(3)(g)).
- Personal data processed under a § 6-1-1304 exemption must not be processed for any other purpose, and processing must stay necessary, reasonable, and proportionate to the exempt purpose; a controller invoking an exemption bears the burden of showing it qualifies and stays within these limits (§ 6-1-1304(4), (5)). A controller that cannot make that showing is treated as processing outside any exemption and so is subject to the full part.
- "Sensitive data" means personal data revealing racial or ethnic origin, religious beliefs, a mental or physical health condition or diagnosis, sex life or sexual orientation, or citizenship status; genetic or biometric data processed for unique identification; personal data from a known child; biological data; and, from October 1, 2025, precise geolocation data (§ 6-1-1303(24); § 6-1-1303(24)(e), added by 2025 Colo. Sess. Laws ch. 240 § 19).
- "Precise geolocation data" means information derived from technology that accurately identifies the present or past location of a device that links or is linkable to an individual within a radius of 1,850 feet, including GPS coordinates and other device-derived location data meeting that radius; it excludes the content of communications and data from advanced utility metering infrastructure (§ 6-1-1303(17.4), added by 2025 Colo. Sess. Laws ch. 240 § 18, effective May 23, 2025). An earlier definition at a 1,750-foot radius (§ 6-1-1303(17.5)) was added for an October 1, 2025 effective date but is repealed that same day, so it never independently took effect; a document should use the 1,850-foot radius for any conduct from May 23, 2025 onward.
- "Biometric data" means one or more biometric identifiers used or intended for identification; it excludes a photograph, audio or voice recording, or data generated from one, unless used for identification. "Biometric identifier" includes a fingerprint, voiceprint, eye retina or iris scan, facial map or geometry, or other unique biological, physical, or behavioral pattern (§ 6-1-1303(2.4), (2.5), effective July 1, 2025).
- "Biological data" means data from the technological processing, measurement, or analysis of an individual's biological, genetic, biochemical, physiological, or neural properties or activities, used or intended for identification purposes; it includes neural data (§ 6-1-1303(2.2)).

## What the documents must say

- A privacy notice: reasonably accessible, clear, and meaningful, stating the categories of personal data collected or processed, the purposes of processing, how and where a consumer may exercise the consumer rights below (including contact information and the appeal process), and the categories of personal data and of third parties involved in any sharing (§ 6-1-1308(1)(a)). If the controller sells personal data or processes for targeted advertising, it clearly and conspicuously discloses that fact and the opt-out method, both inside the notice and in a separate, readily accessible location outside it (§ 6-1-1308(1)(b), § 6-1-1306(1)(a)(III)). From October 1, 2025, the sensitive-data category list in the notice must include precise geolocation data where the controller collects it (§ 6-1-1308(1), § 6-1-1303(24)(e)).
- A rights-request section naming the specific request method or methods, chosen to fit how consumers normally interact with the controller, the need for secure and reliable communication, and the controller's ability to authenticate the request; it must not require creation of a new account, though it may require use of an existing one (§ 6-1-1306(1)).
- The five consumer rights: opt out of processing for targeted advertising, sale of personal data, or profiling toward decisions with legal or similarly significant effects; access; correction; deletion; and portability, exercisable up to twice a year and never requiring disclosure of the controller's trade secrets (§ 6-1-1306(1)(a) to (e)).
- A response to any of these requests, without undue delay and within 45 days of receipt, extendable by 45 more days where reasonably necessary given complexity or volume, with notice of the extension and its reasons within the initial 45 days; where the controller does not act, notice within the same window of the reasons and of the appeal process (§ 6-1-1306(2)). Responses are free except that a second or subsequent request within 12 months may carry a fee under § 24-72-205(5)(a).
- An internal appeal process, conspicuous and as easy to use as the request process, available within a reasonable period after a refusal notice, with a response and written reasons within 45 days of the appeal (extendable by 60 more days with notice and reasons given within the initial 45 days), and a statement that the consumer may contact the attorney general about the appeal's outcome (§ 6-1-1306(3)).
- Where the controller sells data or processes for targeted advertising, a clear and conspicuous opt-out method outside the privacy notice itself, directly or through a link whose text gives a clear understanding of its purpose (examples the attorney general's rules accept, verbatim: "Colorado Opt-Out Rights," "Personal Data Use Opt-Out," "Your Opt-Out Rights," "Your Privacy Choices," "Your Colorado Privacy Choices") (§ 6-1-1306(1)(a)(III); Rule 4.03(B)(3)).
- From July 1, 2024, support for a consumer opting out through a user-selected universal opt-out mechanism meeting the attorney general's technical specifications, honoring the signal as an opt-out of targeted advertising, sale, or both as the mechanism indicates, for the associated browser, device, and, if known, consumer, until the consumer consents back in (§ 6-1-1306(1)(a)(IV)(B); Rule 5.08(A)(1)). A controller may still let a consumer consent, through a web page or application, to processing that takes precedence over the signal, but only after clear notice of the choices, the data categories and purposes, and the withdrawal method, and only where revoking that consent is as easy as giving it (§ 6-1-1306(1)(a)(IV)(C)).
- Consent before processing sensitive data, or, for a known child's data, consent from the parent or lawful guardian, meeting the § 6-1-1303(5) definition of consent (§ 6-1-1308(7)). From an amendment approved May 23, 2025, the same consent is also required before selling a consumer's sensitive data (§ 6-1-1308(7), as amended by 2025 Colo. Sess. Laws ch. 240 § 20). A controller need not obtain this consent for sensitive data inferences about a consumer over 13 where the processing purpose would be obvious to a reasonable consumer from the context, the inferences are permanently deleted within 24 hours of collection or of the processing's completion (whichever comes first), they are never transferred, sold, or shared with a processor, affiliate, or third party, and the data and inferences are not processed for any other purpose than the one disclosed; a controller relying on this must describe the practice, including the retention and deletion timeline, in its privacy notice and in its data protection assessment (Rule 6.10(B), (C)).
- For a service the controller knows or willfully disregards is used by a minor, from October 1, 2025: no processing of a minor's personal data for targeted advertising, sale, or decision-affecting profiling; no processing for an undisclosed or incompatible purpose, or longer than reasonably necessary; and no use of a system design feature to significantly increase, sustain, or extend a minor's use of the service, in each case absent the minor's (or, for a child, the parent's or guardian's) consent (§ 6-1-1308.5(2)(a), (b), (3)(a)). Compliance with the federal Children's Online Privacy Protection Act's verifiable-parental-consent requirements satisfies the parental-consent branch (§ 6-1-1308.5(3)(a)(II)(B)).
- Collection of a minor's precise geolocation data only where reasonably necessary to provide the service, retained only as long as necessary, with a signal that collection is occurring made available to the minor throughout the collection, except for a service used under a ski area operator's direction (§ 6-1-1308.5(2)(c)).
- A consent mechanism reachable by a minor that is not designed or manipulated to substantially subvert or impair a minor's autonomy, decision-making, or choice; a direct-messaging feature reachable by a minor with readily accessible, easy-to-use safeguards limiting an unconnected adult's ability to send unsolicited messages, except for email or private one-to-one messaging not posted publicly (§ 6-1-1308.5(3)(b)).
- Before collecting a biometric identifier, from July 1, 2025: satisfaction of every § 6-1-1308 duty, plus a clear and accessible disclosure that collection is occurring, the specific purpose and retention length, and whether and why the identifier will go to a processor (§ 6-1-1314(4)(a)).
- A written biometric retention policy, made public (with narrow exceptions for employee-only or internal-security-incident policies), setting a retention schedule, a security-incident response protocol, and guidelines requiring deletion on or before the earliest of: the date the collection purpose is satisfied; 24 months after the consumer's last interaction with the controller; or a reasonably feasible date no more than 45 days (extendable by up to 45 more days where reasonably necessary) after the controller's at-least-annual necessity review (§ 6-1-1314(2)).
- On request, free disclosure of the category or description of a consumer's biometric data, its source, the purpose of collection or processing, the identity of any third party it was disclosed to and the purpose, and the category or description of what is disclosed to third parties, limited to controllers meeting the volume thresholds, a common-branded affiliate, or a two-business joint venture (§ 6-1-1314(5)).
- An employer's biometric-consent clause conditioned on employment, limited to the four listed purposes: secure physical or electronic access (excluding location or app-usage-time tracking), recording the start and end of a full work day including meal and longer rest breaks, workplace safety or security, and public safety or security during an emergency (§ 6-1-1314(6)(a)). Any other employer use needs a separate consent that is not itself a condition of employment.
- A vendor or processor contract must fix the processing instructions (nature and purpose), the data type and duration of processing, a confidentiality duty on every person processing the data, a subcontractor clause giving the controller a chance to object and binding the subcontractor to the processor's own obligations, an end-of-service deletion-or-return term chosen by the controller unless retention is legally required, the processor's duty to make compliance information available, and audit rights for the controller or its designated auditor, or an independent annual audit at the processor's expense with a report on request (§ 6-1-1305(3) to (5)).
- Where a controller must obtain consent to process a known child's personal data, reasonable efforts at verifiable parental consent, using a method reasonably calculated to confirm the person consenting is the child's parent or lawful guardian; acceptable methods include a signed consent form returned by mail, fax, or electronic scan; a payment-card or other online-payment transaction that notifies the account holder of the discrete transaction; a toll-free call to trained staff; a videoconference with trained staff; or checking a government-issued ID against an identification database, with the ID deleted promptly after verification. Data collected to verify a parent's or guardian's identity may not be used for any other purpose (Rule 7.06).
- Notice of a material change to the privacy notice, given through a channel the controller regularly uses to communicate with consumers; material changes include, without limitation, changes to the categories of personal data processed, the processing purposes, the controller's identity, whether data is shared with third parties, the categories of those third parties, or the methods for exercising data rights. Where a material change amounts to a secondary use of data already collected, the controller obtains fresh consent for that secondary use before applying the change to that data (Rule 6.04).
- A privacy notice must meet a baseline quality standard: understandable and accessible to its target audience with particular attention to children or minors, plain language over jargon, accessible to consumers with disabilities, available in the languages the controller ordinarily uses, available through a readily accessible interface used with the product, readable on every device consumers normally use, communicated through a channel the controller regularly uses, and straightforward, accurate, never unfair, deceptive, false, or misleading (Rule 3.02(A)).
- The privacy notice content required by Rule 6.03(A): a description of the controller's online and offline processing practices by data category (described at a granular level, with worked examples such as "contact information," "government issued identification numbers," "payment information", "Information from Cookies," "data revealing religious affiliation," and "medical data") and processing purpose; a disclosure of whether the data provided for each purpose will be sold or used for targeted advertising or for profiling toward decisions with legal or similarly significant effects; categories of personal data sold or shared with third parties and, for those third parties, a granular category description with worked examples such as "analytics companies," "data brokers," "third-party advertisers," "payment processors," "lenders," "other merchants," and "government agencies"; the data-rights list and request methods, including instructions on how an authorized agent submits an opt-out on the consumer's behalf; the authentication process used; where sensitive data inferences will be deleted within 24 hours, a description of those inferences and their retention and deletion timeline; contact information; appeal instructions; and the date the privacy notice was last updated (Rule 6.03(A)).
- Where the controller processes personal data for profiling toward a decision with legal or similarly significant effects (financial or lending services, housing, insurance, education enrollment or opportunity, criminal justice, employment, health care, or access to essential goods or services), the privacy notice discloses, at a minimum: which decisions are subject to the profiling; the categories of personal data processed for it; a plain-language, non-technical explanation of the profiling logic; a plain-language explanation of how the profiling is used in the decision, including the role of any human involvement; whether the system has been evaluated for accuracy, fairness, or bias (including the impact of sensitive data) and the outcome of that evaluation; the benefits and potential consequences of the profiling-based decision; and how to opt out of the profiling. This disclosure duty does not require exposing the controller's trade secrets (Rule 9.03(A), (B)).

## What the documents must not say

- No clause representing that consumers have a private cause of action for a violation of this part: no private right of action exists under this part or any other law, and the part neither relieves any party of its duties nor alters any independent right consumers have under other law (§ 6-1-1310(1), § 6-1-1311(1)(b)).
- No consent mechanism built on a bundled general terms-of-service and privacy-policy checkbox that also covers unrelated processing; acceptance of a general or broad terms-of-use document bundling data-processing descriptions with unrelated information is expressly excluded from the § 6-1-1303(5) definition of consent, along with hovering, muting, pausing, or closing content, and any agreement obtained through a dark pattern (§ 6-1-1303(5)(a) to (c)).
- No consent request that conditions contract performance, or goods, services, discounts, or promotions, on consent to data processing not necessary for them; that is not freely given and so not valid consent, even if the consumer clicked "accept" (Rule 7.03(C)).
- No pre-ticked boxes, blanket acceptance of general terms, silence, inactivity, or a negative-option construction requiring the consumer to act to prevent agreement; none of these is a clear affirmative action (Rule 7.03(B)(2)).
- No bundled-only consent request for two or more processing purposes that are not reasonably necessary to or compatible with one another; the consumer must be able to consent to each separately, or the controller must offer a granular option alongside the bundle in the same interface (Rule 7.03(D)).
- No choice interface that gives one option greater size, prominence, color, or styling than another; no "accept all" made easier than "reject all"; no extra step, such as a mandatory "Learn More" click, placed only in front of the less privacy-protective path; no pre-selected or default option; no treating silence, closing a pop-up, or continued use as consent (Rule 7.10(A)).
- No describing data as "de-identified" unless the controller takes reasonable measures against re-association, publicly commits not to re-identify it, and contractually obligates recipients to the same; data that fails any of these three conditions remains personal data regardless of the label used (§ 6-1-1303(11)).
- No clause permitting sale, lease, or trade of a biometric identifier with any entity; that prohibition is absolute and cannot be waived by consent (§ 6-1-1314(4)(b)(I)).
- No clause authorizing a sale of sensitive data based only on a privacy-notice disclosure or on the consumer's failure to opt out; a sale of sensitive data needs the consumer's (or, for a known child, the parent's or guardian's) affirmative consent, the same standard already required for processing it (§ 6-1-1308(7), as amended by 2025 Colo. Sess. Laws ch. 240 § 20).
- No requiring a new account to exercise any of the five consumer rights, and no increasing a product's cost or decreasing its availability based solely on a right's exercise and unrelated to feasibility or value; this does not bar a different price or quality tied to voluntary participation in a bona fide loyalty, rewards, or discount program (§ 6-1-1306(1), § 6-1-1308(1)(c), (d)).
- No charging a fee for authenticating a rights request (Rule 4.08(E)).
- No treating the absence of a later universal-opt-out signal as consent to opt back in, and no requiring login or other authentication as a condition of recognizing the signal, beyond what § 6-1-1313(2)(f) permits for residency authentication (Rule 5.08(D), 5.09(B)).
- No local-law compliance clause addressed to Colorado municipal or county privacy ordinances for processing this part covers; this part preempts them (§ 6-1-1312).

## Product behaviour it assumes

- The checkout, account, and privacy pages name the specific request method or methods for the five rights, support authentication without requiring a new account, and route a request into the 45-day (plus possible 45-day extension) response pipeline with a documented appeal path (§ 6-1-1306(1) to (3)).
- The privacy notice, or a separate location outside it, carries a clear and conspicuous opt-out method for targeted advertising and sale, and from July 1, 2024 the controller's systems recognize a universal opt-out mechanism signal and persist the opted-out state per browser, device, and known consumer until the consumer consents back in (§ 6-1-1306(1)(a)(III), (IV)(B); Rule 5.08).
- A documented, before-the-fact data protection assessment exists for any processing presenting a heightened risk of harm: targeted advertising, profiling with a listed risk, sale of personal data, or sensitive-data processing, weighing benefits against risks as mitigated by safeguards; stored for as long as the activity continues and produced to the attorney general on request (§ 6-1-1309; confidential under the Colorado Open Records Act).
- From October 1, 2025, a separate minors data protection assessment exists for any service, product, or feature the controller knows or willfully disregards reaches a minor consumer and that presents a heightened risk of harm to minors, addressing the service's purpose, the minor's data categories processed, the processing purposes, and the foreseeable heightened risk; where the assessment or a later review finds that risk, a mitigation or elimination plan is established and implemented (§ 6-1-1309.5(1), (5)).
- A biometric-identifier collection flow discloses, before collection, that collection is occurring, its specific purpose and retention length, and whether and why the identifier goes to a processor; a published written retention policy exists separately from this collection notice (§ 6-1-1314(2), (4)(a)).
- A loyalty or rewards program's terms are reviewed so that any price or service difference tracks voluntary participation in a bona fide program, not a condition on waiving a consumer right outside that structure (§ 6-1-1308(1)(d); Rule 6.05).
- The controller's systems log and retain records of every consumer data-rights request made under § 6-1-1306 for at least 24 months, recording at minimum the date of the request, the type of right exercised, the date and nature of the controller's response, the basis for any denial, and the existence and resolution of any appeal (Rule 6.11(A)).
- Where a consumer has not interacted with the controller in the prior 24 months, the consent flow runs again in full before the controller continues processing that consumer's sensitive data or continues a secondary use tied to decision-affecting profiling; this refresh is not required where the consumer can update their opt-out preferences at any time through a self-service interface. A separate rule governs refreshing an employee's biometric consent under § 6-1-1314(6)(b) (Rule 7.08(A), (B)).

## Prescribed wording

- Accepted opt-out link text under Rule 4.03(B)(3), any one of, verbatim: "Colorado Opt-Out Rights," "Personal Data Use Opt-Out," "Your Opt-Out Rights," "Your Privacy Choices," "Your Colorado Privacy Choices." These are examples the rule accepts, not an exhaustive list of the only acceptable text.
- Accepted display text for honoring a universal-opt-out signal under Rule 5.08(E): "Opt-Out Preference Signal Honored"
- Operative statutory text of the precise-geolocation-data definition, § 6-1-1303(17.4) (2025 Colo. Sess. Laws ch. 240 § 18; capitals as printed in the session law):

  > "PRECISE GEOLOCATION DATA" MEANS INFORMATION DERIVED FROM TECHNOLOGY THAT ACCURATELY IDENTIFIES THE PRESENT OR PAST LOCATION OF A DEVICE THAT LINKS OR IS LINKABLE TO AN INDIVIDUAL WITHIN A RADIUS OF ONE THOUSAND EIGHT HUNDRED FIFTY FEET.

- Operative statutory text of the sensitive-data duty, § 6-1-1308(7) as amended (2025 Colo. Sess. Laws ch. 240 § 20; capitals in the original session-law text mark the added words):

  > A controller shall not process OR SELL a consumer's sensitive data without first obtaining the consumer's consent or, in the case of the processing of personal data concerning a known child, without first obtaining consent from the child's parent or lawful guardian.

## Dates

- July 1, 2023: the original part 13 (§§ 6-1-1301 to 6-1-1313) takes effect.
- July 1, 2024: the universal-opt-out-mechanism duty begins (§ 6-1-1306(1)(a)(IV)(B)); the prior subsection (1)(a)(IV)(A) is repealed the same date.
- January 1, 2025: the general 60-day cure-notice requirement before enforcement is repealed (§ 6-1-1311(1)(d)(I)).
- July 1, 2025: the biometric data and biometric identifier definitions (§ 6-1-1303(2.2), (2.4), (2.5)) and the full biometric regime at § 6-1-1314 take effect; the applicability threshold's biometric carve-out (§ 6-1-1304(1)(a)(II)) also dates from this point.
- May 23, 2025: 2025 Colo. Sess. Laws ch. 240 (SB 25-276) is approved; its new precise-geolocation-data definition at § 6-1-1303(17.4) (1,850-foot radius) takes effect the same day, as does its amendment adding a sale prohibition to the sensitive-data duty at § 6-1-1308(7); the act states no separate delayed effective date for that amendment.
- October 1, 2025: the minors-specific sections (§§ 6-1-1305.5, 6-1-1308.5, 6-1-1309.5), the minors and "adult" definitions, and the minors-sections applicability carve-out (§ 6-1-1304(1)(b)) take effect; precise geolocation data is added to the sensitive-data definition at § 6-1-1303(24)(e), and the interim 1,750-foot definition at § 6-1-1303(17.5) is repealed the same day it would otherwise have taken effect, so it never independently governs.
- December 31, 2026: the separate cure-notice requirement for enforcement of the minors-specific sections is repealed (§ 6-1-1311(1)(d)(II)).

## Open points to raise

- Whether a specific document's data flow is a "sale" under § 6-1-1303(23) on the facts, since mislabeling a disclosure as a mere "service" does not change which duties attach.
- Whether the controller's processing of biometric identifiers is covered by the general volume thresholds, the biometric carve-out regardless of volume, or both, since a controller below the general thresholds is not automatically exempt from § 6-1-1314 once it touches biometric data.
- Whether the controller actually knows or willfully disregards that a consumer is a minor, a fact question the attorney general's rules weigh on a non-exhaustive, non-dispositive totality of the circumstances (Rule 6.13), and on which this file states the factors but not a bright line for any specific product.
- Whether an existing geolocation clause, drafted against the earlier 1,750-foot figure, has been updated to the operative 1,850-foot radius for conduct from May 23, 2025 onward.
