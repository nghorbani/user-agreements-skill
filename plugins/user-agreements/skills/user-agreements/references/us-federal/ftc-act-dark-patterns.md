# FTC Act section 5: unfair or deceptive acts or practices, and dark patterns

Citation: 15 U.S.C. § 45
Version: current through Pub. L. 119-121 (2026-10-09)
Official text: https://uscode.house.gov/browse/prelim@title15&edition=prelim
Law as of: 2026-10-20

This file covers the statutory unfairness and deception standard in § 45, and how FTC staff guidance
applies that standard to manipulative interface design ("dark patterns") and to online disclosures.
It does not cover the Restore Online Shoppers' Confidence Act or the Negative Option Rule, which
govern cancellation mechanics for recurring charges in their own file, or COPPA, which has its own
file. Where a pattern described here also triggers one of those regimes, this file flags the overlap
and points to the other file.

## When it applies

- Section 45(a)(1) declares two things unlawful: unfair methods of competition, and unfair or
  deceptive acts or practices, each "in or affecting commerce." The Commission enforces this against
  every person, partnership, or corporation except a short exempt list: banks, the savings and loan
  institutions described in 15 U.S.C. § 57a(f)(3), federal credit unions described in 15 U.S.C.
  § 57a(f)(4), common carriers subject to the Acts to regulate commerce, air and foreign air carriers
  under 49 U.S.C. subtitle VII part A, and entities covered by the Packers and Stockyards Act, 1921,
  except as that Act's own § 406(b) provides (§ 45(a)(2)).
- Foreign commerce (other than import commerce) is covered only when it has a direct, substantial,
  and reasonably foreseeable effect either on domestic or import commerce, or on a United States
  person's export commerce, and that same effect gives rise to the claim; when the only hook is the
  export-commerce branch, relief is limited to injury to that export business (§ 45(a)(3)). Separately,
  foreign-commerce conduct causing reasonably foreseeable injury within the United States, or
  involving material conduct occurring within the United States, counts as "unfair or deceptive acts or
  practices," with the full range of Commission remedies available for it, including restitution to
  domestic or foreign victims (§ 45(a)(4)).
- The deception and unfairness prohibitions reach the full interface a consumer experiences, beyond
  the words of a clause alone. FTC staff guidance evaluates the net impression created by a flow or sequence
  of screens the same way deception analysis evaluates an advertisement; a technically accurate
  statement sitting in a terms of service document, a tooltip, or a collapsed menu does not cure a
  misleading interface built around it (FTC Staff Report, Bringing Dark Patterns to Light, Introduction
  and Section II). Manipulative design practices of this kind are commonly called dark patterns: design
  choices that trick or manipulate users into choices they would not otherwise have made and that may
  cause harm (Bringing Dark Patterns to Light, Background).
- The clear and conspicuous disclosure standard applies to every medium a business uses to advertise
  or sell, online or offline, desktop or mobile, with no exemption for space-constrained formats such
  as banner ads or short social media posts (FTC Staff Guidance, .com Disclosures, Section II, Section
  III.C.1.e).
- Patterns often combine and compound each other: a deceptive testimonial, a buried disclaimer, and a
  hard-to-cancel subscription can each make the others more effective and more harmful together than
  any one alone (Bringing Dark Patterns to Light, Introduction).

## What the documents must say

- Every claim conveyed by an advertisement, express or implied, must be truthful, and the advertiser
  must hold substantiation for it before the claim runs; an advertisement must also not be unfair under
  the three-part test below (.com Disclosures, Section III).
- Where a disclosure is needed to keep a claim from being misleading, it must be clear and
  conspicuous, which is measured by how consumers actually perceive and understand it within the whole
  ad or flow, not by whether the information exists somewhere a consumer could find it (.com
  Disclosures, Section III.B). A disclosure can only qualify a true claim; it cannot cure a false one,
  so where a disclosure would contradict the claim it is paired with, the claim itself has to change
  (.com Disclosures, Section III.A).
- If a disclosure is necessary to prevent a claim from being deceptive or unfair and it cannot be made
  clear and conspicuous, the claim must change so the disclosure is not needed, or the ad must not run;
  the same duty applies to the choice of channel, so a platform or ad format that does not let the
  business make clear and conspicuous disclosures must not be used to run a claim that needs one (.com
  Disclosures, Section III.B).
- A disclosure is stronger the closer it sits to the claim it qualifies, ideally on the same screen and
  immediately adjacent to it; where scrolling is unavoidable, the product should use text or visual
  cues that prompt the consumer to scroll, and should make a scrolled-to disclosure unavoidable before
  the consumer can proceed, rather than leaving it optional to view (.com Disclosures, Section
  III.C.1.a).
- A disclosure that is not integral to a claim, or that is too long or complex to place directly next
  to the claim, can be carried by a hyperlink if the hyperlink is visibly clickable, labeled to convey
  the specific subject and importance of what it leads to, placed adjacent to the claim, leads directly
  to a click-through page or screen that itself displays the full disclosure prominently, and is styled
  the same way everywhere on the site or app rather than signaled differently from page to page or
  feature to feature (.com Disclosures, Section III.C.1.b).
- Where a purchase can be completed inside an interactive ad unit itself, every disclosure the purchase
  needs must be included in that ad; a link out to the disclosure elsewhere is not enough (.com
  Disclosures, Section III.C.1.e).
- A disclosure should be at least as large as the claim it qualifies, in a color that contrasts with
  its background, and should not depend on an icon, abbreviation, or symbol unless a significant
  majority of consumers already understand what it means (.com Disclosures, Section III.C.2, Section
  III.C.6).
- Where claims requiring qualification repeat through a site, app, or campaign, the disclosure must
  repeat with them; a long or complex site may also need the same disclosure on more than one page
  because consumers enter and move through it by different paths (.com Disclosures, Section III.C.4).
- Disclosures must match the format of the claim they qualify: a written claim needs a written
  disclosure, not an audio- or video-only one, and a visual disclosure in a video must stay on screen
  long enough for a consumer to notice, read, and understand it (.com Disclosures, Section III.C.5).
- A disclosure needed to prevent deception must appear before the consumer decides to buy, for example
  before clicking an "add to cart" or "order now" control, and where a product can also be bought
  somewhere other than the advertiser's own site, the disclosure must appear in the ad itself before
  the consumer would go elsewhere to buy it (.com Disclosures, Section III.C.1.d).
- A space-constrained ad is not exempt from the disclosure duty: the disclosure must fit in the ad
  itself or link clearly to it, and every instance of the ad that would be misleading on its own must
  carry the disclosure; a business cannot rely on consumers connecting a series of separate posts
  (.com Disclosures, Section III.C.1.e).
- A toggle, binary choice, or accept and reject pair must give both options comparable visual
  prominence and must avoid a double negative that leaves it unclear which position stops which
  outcome (Bringing Dark Patterns to Light, Section IV).
- Where a design choice, claim, or disclosure is aimed at or reasonably likely to reach a specific
  audience, such as older adults, children, non-native speakers of the interface language, or users who
  may share a device with others, the business must consider how that audience will actually perceive
  the design, for example by avoiding placing important information at the edge of the screen or in a
  light color that an older consumer is less likely to notice (Bringing Dark Patterns to Light, Section
  II; see also the coppa.md file for children-specific duties and the negative-option.md file for
  accountholder-consent rules in subscription and shared-device contexts).
- A business collecting leads or referrals on behalf of a named purpose or a named recipient must be
  honest about who is collecting the information and why, and must not share it with a different buyer
  or use it for a different purpose without the consumer's consent (Bringing Dark Patterns to Light,
  Section IV).

## What the documents must not say

- A clause or interface may not rely on wording alone to make an unfair practice fair: a practice is
  unfair under § 45(n) only where it causes or is likely to cause substantial injury to consumers that
  is not reasonably avoidable by consumers themselves and that is not outweighed by countervailing
  benefits to consumers or competition; a drafter cannot write around this test by adding a disclaimer,
  since the test looks at the practice's effect, not at whether it was described somewhere (§ 45(n)).
  Established public policy may be considered as one piece of evidence toward an unfairness finding,
  but a document cannot be built on the assumption that public policy alone, without the three-part
  test, settles the question (§ 45(n)).
- No design may create a false impression of urgency, scarcity, or popularity that is not true: a
  countdown timer that does not actually expire or that resets, a message that inventory is low or
  demand is high when it is not, or a claim that other people are viewing or have recently bought the
  same item when that is not true (Bringing Dark Patterns to Light, Section I).
- Advertising, rankings, or comparison content may not be formatted to look like independent editorial
  content, a neutral comparison, or an unaffiliated recommendation when it is in fact paid for, ranked
  by payment, or otherwise influenced by a relationship the consumer is not told about (Bringing Dark
  Patterns to Light, Section I).
- A business may not advertise a price without including every mandatory charge a consumer would
  reasonably expect to be part of the cost, and may not postpone disclosure of a mandatory fee until
  late in the purchase flow (drip pricing); a business may also not represent a fee as mandatory when
  it is not (Bringing Dark Patterns to Light, Section II). Where the product involves credit, a design
  that varies by race, national origin, or another protected characteristic risks a separate violation
  of the Equal Credit Opportunity Act alongside the FTC Act claim (Bringing Dark Patterns to Light,
  Section II).
- A business may not rely on a general terms of service document, a privacy policy, a hyperlink, a
  pop-up, or a drop-down menu to carry a disclosure that is necessary to prevent deception or
  unfairness; burying the disclosure there does not make it clear and conspicuous, and a consumer's
  click to "agree" to a document does not by itself make an embedded disclosure clear and conspicuous
  (.com Disclosures, Section III.C.2; Bringing Dark Patterns to Light, Section II, Section IV).
- A necessary disclosure may not depend on a pop-up that pop-up-blocking software can keep from
  appearing at all; where a pop-up or interstitial does carry a disclosure, the consumer must take an
  affirmative action, such as choosing between "yes" and "no" with neither one preselected, before being
  able to proceed past it (.com Disclosures, Section III.C.1.c).
- An interaction may not lead to an outcome other than the one its label or icon indicates, such as a
  control that looks like it closes a pop-up but instead installs unwanted software or starts a purchase
  (Bringing Dark Patterns to Light, Section IV).
- A design may not use contrasting visual prominence to steer a consumer toward one option while
  making the other option harder to see, find, or select, whether the choice concerns a purchase, a
  cancellation, or a privacy setting (Bringing Dark Patterns to Light, Section IV).
- A design may not present a privacy or data choice in a way that does not let the consumer
  definitively reject collection or use, that repeatedly re-prompts after a rejection until the
  consumer gives in, that hides the choice behind multiple tabs or sub-tabs, or that uses confusing
  toggle language such as a double negative (Bringing Dark Patterns to Light, Section IV).
- A design may not add an item to a shopping cart, enroll a consumer in a plan, or otherwise take an
  action with a cost consequence without the consumer's prior, unambiguous action to choose it; a
  pre-checked box or a default selection that benefits the business rather than the consumer does not
  supply that choice (Bringing Dark Patterns to Light, Section III).
- A business representing that it collects personal information for one stated purpose or one named
  recipient may not then share that information with a different buyer or use it for a different
  purpose without the consumer's consent; this applies to lead generators as much as to a business
  collecting information directly, and a false claim of affiliation with another organization to induce
  a consumer to submit information is itself a separate violation (Bringing Dark Patterns to Light,
  Section IV).

## Product behaviour it assumes

- Every checkout, signup, upgrade, or data-sharing flow is reviewed as a whole sequence of screens for
  its net impression on a reasonable consumer, not clause by clause, before it ships, and again after a
  material redesign.
- Pricing displays carry every mandatory charge in the price shown at the point a consumer first sees
  the price, and any genuinely optional or complex charge that cannot fit there sits behind a hyperlink
  that is visibly clickable, specifically labeled, and placed immediately next to the price it
  qualifies; the full charge detail displays on the very next screen the link opens, without requiring
  further navigation.
- Any countdown timer, stock indicator, or activity message a product surfaces (such as a count of
  other shoppers currently viewing an item) is backed by a live, accurate value at the moment it
  displays, and is removed or corrected rather than looping or resetting when the underlying condition
  changes.
- Disclosures that a legal or compliance review marks as necessary to prevent deception are placed on
  the same screen as the claim they qualify, are reviewed for size and color contrast against their
  background, and are excluded from the general terms of service or privacy policy as the place they
  can live instead.
- A hyperlinked disclosure's click-through rate and the time consumers spend on the linked page are
  monitored, and a disclosure that consumers are not following or reading is moved to a different
  delivery method rather than left as a link that the data shows is not working.
- A/B testing or other design-experimentation tooling used on claims, defaults, or disclosures logs
  which variant a legal or compliance reviewer has cleared, so that a variant found to increase
  conversion by creating a false or misleading impression is not shipped on that basis alone.
- Toggle, accept and reject, and other binary choice components in design system or component-library
  code enforce equal visual prominence (size, color, position) between the two options as a shared,
  reusable rule, rather than leaving prominence to each feature team's own styling, and the same
  components reject double-negative labels in copy review.
- Consent flows that bundle an unrelated add-on product, trial, or subscription alongside a primary
  purchase require an affirmative, non-preselected acknowledgment of the add-on specifically, separate
  from and in addition to acceptance of the primary purchase; that acknowledgment is captured early in
  the flow, before the primary item is actually added to the cart, not folded into a later checkout step.
- Lead-generation, referral, and data-sharing features record the stated collection purpose and the
  named recipient at the point of collection, and block a downstream share to a different buyer or use
  for a different purpose unless a fresh consumer consent for that different purpose or recipient is on
  file; a business using third-party lead generators monitors what those generators are doing on its
  behalf, and a business buying sensitive personal data takes steps to vet a prospective buyer before the
  sale.
- Account and subscription deletion, and any step a consumer takes to limit data collection, are
  monitored for the number of screens, clicks, or required actions compared to the signup or
  data-collection flow for the same feature, so that a review can catch a cancellation or
  opt-out path that has grown harder than the path that created the obligation in the first place.

## Prescribed wording

None. Neither § 45 nor FTC staff guidance prescribes exact sentences for a disclosure, a toggle label,
or a consent prompt; the standard is functional (clear and conspicuous, equal prominence, an
affirmative and unambiguous act), and compliance is judged by the net impression the design creates.

## Open points to raise

- Neither staff guidance document is itself a Commission rule, so neither carries the force and
  binding effect of a regulation; a drafter relying on either for a specific design choice should treat
  it as the Commission's stated enforcement theory under § 45, confirmed case by case through current
  FTC enforcement actions, rather than as a safe harbor.
- Whether a specific design choice crosses from a legitimate business practice into an unfair or
  deceptive one under the three-part test in § 45(n) is a fact-specific question about the actual
  consumer experience; it is not settled by this file alone, and a drafter should raise a borderline
  design with counsel before shipping it.
- The Bringing Dark Patterns to Light source material recovered for this file is missing its endnotes
  and a second appendix the report calls Appendix B; no statement in this file depends on either, but a
  drafter who needs a specific U.S. Code pinpoint cite behind one of the report's examples, beyond the
  report's own section references used here, will not find it in this file.
