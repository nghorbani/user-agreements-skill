# Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act

Citation: 15 U.S.C. 2301 et seq. (chapter 50), implementing rules at 16 CFR parts 700, 701, 702, 703
Version: current through Pub. L. 119-121 (2026-10-09); eCFR as of 2026-10-16
Official text: https://uscode.house.gov/browse/prelim@title15&edition=prelim
Law as of: 2026-10-20

This file covers the Act's warranty-disclosure, warranty-content, and dispute-mechanism rules and
their four implementing rules in 16 CFR parts 700 to 703. It does not cover the FTC Act unfairness
and deception standard beyond the points the Act and its rules cross-reference, and it does not
cover state implied-warranty or UCC law except where the Act expressly limits a supplier's power to
disclaim it.

## When it applies

- The Act reaches written warranties on a "consumer product": tangible personal property
  distributed in commerce and normally used for personal, family, or household purposes, including
  property meant to be attached to or installed in real property whether or not it ends up attached
  or installed (15 U.S.C. 2301(1)). A product counts if that type of product is not uncommonly used
  this way; the share of sales or any individual buyer's actual use does not decide the question,
  and an unclear case is resolved in favor of coverage (16 C.F.R. 700.1(a)). Products bought solely
  for commercial or industrial use are excluded from parts 701 and 702, but not from the Act itself
  or from part 700 (16 C.F.R. 701.1(b), 702.1(b)).
- Farm machinery and other agricultural implements used in the business of farming fall outside the
  Act where personal or household use is uncommon; equipment normally used for personal or household
  gardening is still a consumer product (16 C.F.R. 700.1(b)). Separate items of equipment attached
  to real property, such as air conditioners, furnaces, and water heaters, are covered even though
  state law may treat them as fixtures; the wiring, plumbing, and ducts that are integral parts of
  the structure are not (16 C.F.R. 700.1(c), (d)). Building materials sold over the counter or
  contracted for as part of a home repair or improvement are consumer products; once such materials
  are integrated into the structure at the time of sale, or a consumer contracts with a builder to
  construct a home or an addition using them, they are not, because they cannot practically be
  told apart from the realty (16 C.F.R. 700.1(e), (f)).
- A written warranty is a written affirmation of fact or written promise, made in connection with a
  sale to a buyer, that the material or workmanship is defect-free or will meet a specified
  performance level over a specified time, or a written undertaking to refund, repair, replace, or
  take other remedial action if the product fails to meet the specifications stated, and it must
  become part of the basis of the bargain between a supplier and a buyer other than for resale
  (15 U.S.C. 2301(6)). A statement or disclosure, such as an energy-efficiency rating or care label,
  that does not tie to a specified time period is not a written warranty under the Act, even if it
  is an express warranty under the UCC (16 C.F.R. 700.3(a)). A seller's unconditional right to
  revoke acceptance within a stated number of days, or a trade-in or return-for-credit policy, taken
  alone is not a written warranty and should not be called one; it should be labeled and stated
  separately, for example as a "free trial period" (16 C.F.R. 700.3(b)). A warranty a component
  supplier gives only to a product manufacturer, never conveyed to or brought to the consumer's
  attention, is not a written warranty under the Act; one a component supplier gives directly to the
  consumer, even through an assembler, is (16 C.F.R. 700.3(c)).
- Only the supplier who actually makes a written affirmation, promise, or undertaking is the
  warrantor liable under the Act; a supplier who merely distributes or sells a product under
  another person's warranty, naming that person as warrantor, is not liable for that warranty's
  compliance unless state law treats it as having adopted the warranty, or its own conduct and
  representations create separate obligations (15 U.S.C. 2310(f); 16 C.F.R. 700.4).
- "Consumer" reaches a buyer other than for resale, anyone the product is transferred to during the
  warranty or service contract term, and anyone else entitled under the warranty's terms or
  applicable state law to enforce it; part 701 adds that a buyer for commercial or industrial use,
  or use in the ordinary course of the buyer's business, is excluded from its own "consumer"
  definition (15 U.S.C. 2301(3); 16 C.F.R. 701.1(i)). Part 702 has no separate "consumer"
  definition; its own commercial/industrial carve-out sits instead in its "Seller" definition, which
  reaches only a sale other than for resale or use in the ordinary course of the buyer's business
  (16 C.F.R. 702.1(e)).
- Statements of general policy on customer satisfaction, not tied to a specific product and not
  subject to any specific limitation, do not have to be designated full or limited and are exempt
  from the disclosure, designation, and minimum-standards duties, though FTC Act section 5 and the
  Act's own enforcement provision still reach them (15 U.S.C. 2303(b); 16 C.F.R. 700.5(a)). A policy
  tied to specific products the supplier sells, or carrying an express duration or refund-amount
  limit, does not qualify; a refusal to honor an old policy because the consumer used the product
  for years without raising a complaint is not treated as a disqualifying specific limitation
  (16 C.F.R. 700.5(b)).
- The Act's disclosure duty in 15 U.S.C. 2302 applies only to warranties on products actually
  costing the consumer more than $5 (15 U.S.C. 2302(e)). The designation duty in 15 U.S.C. 2303(a)
  and the Commission's exemption authority in 2303(c) apply only to warranties on products costing
  the consumer more than $10 that are not designated full (statement of duration) warranties; the
  pre-sale disclosure rules in parts 701 and 702 apply above $15 (15 U.S.C. 2303(d); 16 C.F.R.
  700.1(g), 701.2, 702.3). The $10 and $15 thresholds exclude tax and are applied to the bundled
  package price, not a per-unit price, so a package of a dozen items priced at $12 is covered even
  if a smaller package of the same items would sell under the threshold (16 C.F.R. 700.1(g)).
- The Act applies only to products manufactured after July 4, 1975, generally, and only to products
  manufactured after December 31, 1976 for the pre-sale disclosure duties in parts 701 and 702
  (15 U.S.C. 2312(a); 16 C.F.R. 700.2, 700.12). For a repaired product, the date of manufacture of
  the replacement parts controls, not the date of the original product; for a rebuilt or
  exchange-basis product, the date the rebuild was completed controls (16 C.F.R. 700.2).
- The Act generally does not reach products exported to a foreign jurisdiction, as a matter of
  enforcement policy, though products exported for sale at military post exchanges stay subject to
  the same standards as domestic sales (16 C.F.R. 700.1(i)).
- A warranty is distinct from a service contract, which involves either added consideration beyond
  the purchase price or a start date after the sale, and from an agreement regulated as insurance
  under state law, such as automobile breakdown coverage; the McCarran-Ferguson Act, 15 U.S.C. 1011
  et seq., keeps the Act's service-contract rules from invalidating, impairing, or superseding a
  state insurance-regulation law (16 C.F.R. 700.11; 701.1(c), (d); 702.1(c)).
- "On the face of the warranty", the location several disclosures below must use, means the page on
  which the warranty text begins, whether the warranty is a single sheet, printed on both sides, made
  of more than one sheet, or included in a larger document such as a use-and-care manual; where the
  warranty is on a website or displayed electronically, it means close proximity to where the
  warranty text begins (16 C.F.R. 701.1(j); 703.1(h)).

## What the documents must say

- A written warranty must fully and conspicuously disclose, in simple and readily understood
  language, its terms and conditions to the extent FTC rules require; the items the Commission may
  require include the warrantors' names and addresses, who the warranty is extended to, what
  products or parts are covered, what the warrantor will do and at whose expense and for how long,
  what the consumer must do and what it will cost, exceptions and exclusions, the step-by-step claim
  procedure, information on any informal dispute mechanism, a brief description of legal remedies,
  and when the warrantor will perform (15 U.S.C. 2302(a)(1) to (13)).
- Part 701 fixes the disclosure duty for a product actually costing the consumer more than $15: a
  single document, clear, conspicuous, and in simple language, stating who the warranty is extended
  to if coverage is limited to the original purchaser or similarly restricted; a clear description
  of what is covered and, where needed for clarity, excluded; what the warrantor will do for a
  defect, malfunction, or nonconformity, including what it will and will not pay for; when the
  warranty term starts if different from the purchase date, and its duration; a step-by-step claim
  procedure naming who may perform warranty obligations, with a mailing address, or a responsible
  employee's or department's name or title and address, or a toll-free phone number; information on
  any informal dispute mechanism elected under part 703; any limitation on implied warranty duration
  disclosed on the face of the warranty under 15 U.S.C. 2308; any exclusion or limitation of
  incidental or consequential damages; and a statement that the warranty gives specific legal rights
  that may vary by state (16 C.F.R. 701.3(a)(1) to (9)). These nine items do not apply to general
  policy statements on third-party emblems, seals, or insignias that carry no representation about
  product quality or performance, as long as the required disclosures are published in each issue
  of a general-circulation publication by the third party and given free on written request
  (16 C.F.R. 701.3(b)).
- If return of an owner-registration card or similar card is a condition precedent to coverage, the
  warranty must say so; if returning such a card could reasonably appear to be a condition precedent
  but is not, the warranty must disclose that too (16 C.F.R. 701.4). A full warranty may suggest use
  of a registration card only as one possible way to prove the purchase date, and any such
  suggestion must tell the consumer that failure to return the card will not affect warranty rights
  so long as the purchase date can be shown reasonably another way; a full warranty may not make
  card return a condition of coverage (16 C.F.R. 700.7).
- A full warranty whose stated duration runs only for as long as the first purchaser owns the
  product, for example a battery or muffler warranty designated "full warranty for as long as you
  own your car.", may rely on that duration measure; because the consumer is led to believe proof of
  purchase is unneeded while they own the product, the warrantor may not reasonably impose a
  documentary-proof duty, and bears the burden of showing a claimant is not the original purchaser,
  though it may ask the consumer to state or affirm first-purchaser status (16 C.F.R. 700.6(b)).
- A seller of a warranted product must make the warranty text readily available to a prospective
  buyer before sale, either by displaying it near the product (including electronically, if the
  warrantor uses the website option) or by furnishing it on request and posting signs that tell
  buyers warranties are available on request (16 C.F.R. 702.3(a)). The warrantor must supply sellers
  with the materials to do this: a copy with every product, a tag, sign, sticker, label, or decal
  carrying the full text, printed text on a display package accompanied by a copy with the product,
  or a notice, sign, or poster accompanied by a copy with each product (16 C.F.R. 702.3(b)(1)).
- As an alternative, a warrantor may post the warranty terms on its website if it tells the consumer,
  clearly and conspicuously on the product, packaging, or manual, the website address and a phone
  number or postal address to request a copy; provides a free hard copy promptly on request; keeps
  the terms posted and accessible; and gives enough information to let the consumer identify which
  posted terms apply to the specific product (16 C.F.R. 702.3(b)(2)). Where the pre-sale disclosure
  duty under 15 U.S.C. 2302(b) is at stake rather than part 702's own duty, the same digital-display
  option is available, paired with on-product or on-packaging information about the website and a
  phone number, postal address, or other reasonable non-internet contact method, except that for a
  retail, catalog, or door-to-door sale the seller must still make the terms available at the point
  of sale before purchase (15 U.S.C. 2302(b)(4)(A), (B)).
- For catalog or mail-order sales, the seller must disclose, in close conjunction with the product
  description or in a clearly referenced information section of the catalog, either the full warranty
  text or the warrantor's website address plus a statement that the warranty is available free on
  request and where to request it, and must then promptly provide a requested copy (16 C.F.R.
  702.3(c)). For door-to-door sales, before the sale closes the seller must orally disclose, and
  include in any written sales materials shown to the buyer, that the sales representative holds
  copies of the warranties that the buyer may inspect at any time during the presentation
  (16 C.F.R. 702.3(d)).
- A warranty meeting the Act's minimum standards must be conspicuously designated a "full (statement
  of duration) warranty". A warranty not meeting them has a different designation, see PW-US-FED-4
  below. These are the only two designations the Act permits absent a specific exemption by rule
  (15 U.S.C. 2303(a)(1), (2); 16 C.F.R. 700.6(a)). The designation must appear as a caption or
  prominent title, clearly set apart from the warranty text (16 C.F.R. 700.6(a)). A seller may offer
  a product with both full and limited warranties if the two are clearly and conspicuously
  differentiated (15 U.S.C. 2305).
- To meet the federal minimum standards for a full warranty, the warrantor must remedy the product
  within a reasonable time and without charge for a defect, malfunction, or nonconformity; may not
  limit the duration of any implied warranty; may not exclude or limit consequential damages for
  breach of a written or implied warranty unless the exclusion or limitation conspicuously appears
  on the warranty's face; and, if a reasonable number of repair attempts fails to cure the defect,
  must let the consumer elect either a refund or a free replacement (15 U.S.C. 2304(a)(1) to (4)). A
  replacement component must be installed in the product without charge (15 U.S.C. 2304(a)(4)). The
  warrantor's duties under this minimum standard are waived if it can show the defect, malfunction,
  or nonconformity was caused by damage in the consumer's possession that did not result from a
  defect or malfunction, or by unreasonable use, including a failure to provide reasonable and
  necessary maintenance (15 U.S.C. 2304(c)).
- Labeling a warranty "full (statement of duration)" is not only a captioning choice: if a supplier
  so designates it, the warranty is deemed in any action under 15 U.S.C. 2310(d) or under state law
  to incorporate at least the minimum requirements of 15 U.S.C. 2304 regardless of what the
  warranty's own drafted text says, so a drafter who uses the full designation without actually
  meeting those minimum standards creates a document that a court will read as if it met them
  anyway (15 U.S.C. 2304(e)).
- Under a full warranty, the warrantor may not impose any duty on the consumer besides notification
  of the defect as a condition of remedy, unless the warrantor can show the added duty is reasonable
  in a rulemaking, an enforcement proceeding, or an informal dispute proceeding; it may, however,
  require that the product be made available free and clear of liens and encumbrances as a condition
  of replacement or refund, except where the Commission has ruled that impracticable (15 U.S.C.
  2304(b)(1), (2)). If the warranted product has utility only when installed, the no-charge remedy
  duty includes free installation, though the warrantor may impose a reasonable duty to remove,
  return, or reinstall the product (16 C.F.R. 700.9). A provision requiring the consumer to return a
  warranty registration card as a condition of full-warranty coverage is an unreasonable duty, and
  may not appear or be implied in a full warranty (16 C.F.R. 700.7(a), (b)).
- The term "without charge" means the warrantor may not assess the consumer for its own or its
  representatives' costs of the required remedy; it need not compensate incidental expenses, but if
  those expenses arise because the remedy was not timely or because the warrantor imposed an
  unreasonable duty as a condition of remedy, the consumer may recover reasonable incidental
  expenses in an action against the warrantor (15 U.S.C. 2304(d)).
- A warrantor may designate representatives to perform warranty duties, provided it arranges
  reasonable compensation for them; doing so does not relieve the warrantor of its direct
  responsibility to the consumer or make the representative a co-warrantor (15 U.S.C. 2307).
- The Commission may by rule extend the period a written warranty or service contract stays in
  effect to match any period beyond a reasonable minimum of 10 days that the consumer was deprived
  of use of the product because the product did not conform to the warranty or because the
  warrantor or service contractor failed to perform within the time the warranty or service
  contract specifies; a drafter stating warranty duration should account for this possible
  extension where repairs are delayed (15 U.S.C. 2302(b)(3)).
- "Remedy" means repair, replacement, or refund, as the warrantor elects, but the warrantor may not
  elect refund unless it is unable to provide replacement and repair is not commercially
  practicable or cannot be made in a timely way, or unless the consumer is willing to accept a
  refund; "replacement" means furnishing a new consumer product identical or reasonably equivalent
  to the warranted product; "refund" means refunding the actual purchase price, less reasonable
  depreciation for actual use where Commission rules permit such a deduction (15 U.S.C. 2301(10) to
  (12)).
- A warranty or service contract incorporating an informal dispute settlement mechanism must
  disclose, clearly and conspicuously on the warranty's face, that the mechanism is available; the
  mechanism's name and address, or name and a toll-free phone number; whether resort to the
  mechanism is a precondition to exercising rights under the Act, together with a statement that
  resort is not required for remedies arising outside the Act; and where fuller information on the
  mechanism can be found if it sits in accompanying materials (16 C.F.R. 703.2(b)(1) to (4)). Those
  accompanying materials, or the warranty itself, must give either a form addressed to the mechanism
  or a toll-free phone number, the mechanism's name and address, a brief description of its
  procedures, its time limits, and the kinds of information it may require (16 C.F.R. 703.2(c)(1) to
  (5)).
- A warrantor using such a mechanism must take steps reasonably calculated to make consumers aware
  of it when a dispute arises, respond fully and promptly to the mechanism's reasonable information
  requests, notify the mechanism whether and to what extent it will abide by a decision, perform any
  obligations it agrees to, act in good faith in deciding whether to abide by a decision, and comply
  with the mechanism's reasonable requirements for fair and expeditious resolution (16 C.F.R.
  703.2(d), (f), (g), (h)). When a dispute is submitted directly to the warrantor rather than the
  mechanism, the warrantor must decide within a reasonable time whether and to what extent it will
  satisfy the consumer, tell the consumer of its decision, and include the mechanism-availability
  information with that notice (16 C.F.R. 703.2(e)).
- The mechanism itself must be funded and staffed enough to resolve disputes fairly and
  expeditiously, charge consumers no fee, and be insulated from the warrantor's and sponsor's
  influence through advance funding commitments, merit-based staffing, and no conflicting duties for
  mechanism staff (16 C.F.R. 703.3). No member deciding a dispute may be a party to it, an employee
  or agent of a party except for deciding disputes, or a person who is or may become a party to
  related litigation including a class action; where one or two members decide a dispute, all must
  have no direct involvement in the manufacture, distribution, sale, or service of any product, and
  where three or more decide it, at least two-thirds must meet that standard (16 C.F.R. 703.4(a),
  (b)). Members must be interested in the fair and expeditious settlement of consumer disputes
  (16 C.F.R. 703.4(c)).
- The mechanism must have written operating procedures, available to any person on request, and
  must notify both sides promptly on receiving a dispute, investigate and gather the information
  needed for a fair decision, disclose any contradictory information it relies on to both sides with
  an opportunity to respond, and require no information not reasonably needed to decide the dispute
  (16 C.F.R. 703.5(a) to (c)). Absent settlement, it must decide the dispute within 40 days of
  notification except for two narrow delay grounds (a consumer's failure to supply basic case
  information, or a 7-day allowance where the consumer never first sought redress from the
  warrantor), stating a reasonable time for performance, and must disclose its decision and reasons
  to the warrantor, learn whether the warrantor will comply, and disclose the decision, reasons,
  warrantor's intended action, and further-recourse information to the consumer (16 C.F.R. 703.5(d),
  (e), (g)). An oral presentation by a party may occur only with both parties' express agreement,
  after specified disclosures to the consumer, and each party has the right to attend the other's
  presentation (16 C.F.R. 703.5(f)). Where the warrantor agrees to perform, the mechanism must check
  with the consumer within 10 working days of the performance date whether performance occurred
  (16 C.F.R. 703.5(h)). A mechanism decision is not legally binding on anyone, but is admissible in
  evidence in a related civil action (16 C.F.R. 703.5(j); 15 U.S.C. 2310(a)(3)).
- A requirement that a consumer resort to the mechanism before suing under 15 U.S.C. 2310(d) is
  satisfied 40 days after notifying the mechanism, or when the mechanism finishes its duties,
  whichever comes first, extended by any permitted delay (16 C.F.R. 703.5(i)).
- The mechanism must keep detailed records of each dispute for at least 4 years after final
  disposition, maintain indexes by warrantor and by brand and model, and compile semiannual
  statistics across the outcome categories the rule lists; it must have an independent audit
  conducted at least annually covering consumer-awareness efforts, the required indexes, and a
  random sample of handled disputes, with the audit report filed with the Commission and available
  to any person at reasonable cost (16 C.F.R. 703.6; 703.7). Statistical summaries are open to public
  inspection and copying, meetings to decide disputes are open to observers on reasonable terms
  without disclosing party or product identity, and a party to a dispute may access and copy the
  records relating to it (16 C.F.R. 703.8).
- A warrantor may not condition warranty coverage on the consumer's use of a branded article or
  service unless the Commission has granted a waiver on a showing that the product will function
  properly only with that article or service and that the waiver serves the public interest
  (15 U.S.C. 2302(c)). Under a limited warranty covering only parts and not labor, the warrantor may
  not require the consumer to use only warrantor-identified service to install the replacement parts
  (16 C.F.R. 700.10(b)). A warrantor may expressly exclude liability for defects or damage caused by
  unauthorized parts or service, or deny liability where it can show the defect was so caused, which
  is different from conditioning the warranty's validity on exclusive use of authorized parts or
  service in the first place (16 C.F.R. 700.10(c)).
- Congress's declared policy is to encourage warrantors to set up informal dispute settlement
  mechanisms so consumer disputes are fairly and expeditiously settled without litigation
  (15 U.S.C. 2310(a)(1)).
- A state labeling or disclosure requirement on written warranties or their performance that falls
  within the scope of 15 U.S.C. 2302, 2303, or 2304 (or a rule under those sections) and is not
  identical to that section or rule does not apply to a written warranty that complies with those
  sections or rules (15 U.S.C. 2311(c)(1)). As an exception to that baseline preemption rule, a
  State requirement affording consumers greater protection than the Act, which does not unduly
  burden interstate commerce, can remain applicable alongside the Act's own disclosure and
  minimum-standards provisions if the Commission so determines on a state agency's application
  (15 U.S.C. 2311(c)(2)). The Act does not invalidate or restrict any consumer right or remedy under
  other state or federal law, and, apart from the implied-warranty-duration and consequential-damage
  provisions, does not affect personal-injury liability or supersede state law on consequential
  damages for personal or other injury (15 U.S.C. 2311(b)).

## What the documents must not say

- A warrantor may not indicate, directly or indirectly, in a written warranty or service contract
  that its own decision, a service contractor's decision, or a designated third party's decision is
  final or binding in a warranty or service-contract dispute, and may not state that it alone will
  determine what counts as a defect; such statements are deceptive because state and federal courts
  have jurisdiction over warranty and service-contract suits (16 C.F.R. 700.8; 15 U.S.C. 2310(d)).
- A warranty may not be voided, or made to appear voided, for use of unauthorized repair service or
  replacement parts outside what the warranty actually covers; language such as requiring use of an
  "authorized" dealer or "genuine" parts for non-warranty service and maintenance is prohibited
  both as an unlawful tying arrangement and as deceptive, since a warrantor cannot as a matter of
  law avoid liability for a defect unrelated to the consumer's use of unauthorized articles or
  service (16 C.F.R. 700.10(a), (c)).
- A deceptive warranty is one that contains an affirmation, promise, description, or representation
  that is false or fraudulent, or that would mislead a reasonable individual exercising due care in
  light of all the circumstances, or that fails to contain information necessary to keep it from
  misleading such an individual; a warranty using terms like "guaranty" or "warranty" whose actual
  terms and conditions so limit its scope as to deceive a reasonable individual is likewise deceptive
  (15 U.S.C. 2310(c)(2)(A), (B)). Failing to comply with any requirement the Act or its rules impose,
  or violating any prohibition they contain, is itself a violation of FTC Act section 5(a)(1)
  (15 U.S.C. 2310(b)).
- A warranty may not require return of a warranty registration card as a condition precedent to
  coverage, and may not imply such a condition either (16 C.F.R. 700.7(b)).
- A full warranty measured by first-purchaser ownership may not be drafted to cut off a subsequent
  transferee's rights during the warranty's stated duration, since by definition that duration
  expires at transfer and so no rights are "cut off" mid-term; apart from that distinction, a full
  warranty may not expressly restrict a transferee's rights during its stated term (16 C.F.R.
  700.6(b); 15 U.S.C. 2304(b)(4)).
- Suits under 15 U.S.C. 2310(d)(1)(B), the federal-court route, are not available for an individual
  claim worth less than $25, for total claims worth less than $50,000 exclusive of interest and
  costs, or for a class action with fewer than 100 named plaintiffs (15 U.S.C. 2310(d)(3)(A) to (C)).
  These dollar and class-size floors apply only to the federal-court route; a state-court suit under
  15 U.S.C. 2310(d)(1)(A) is not subject to them.
- No individual action for breach of a written or implied warranty or service contract may proceed
  under 15 U.S.C. 2310(d) unless the warrantor or service contractor is first given a reasonable
  opportunity to cure, unless the action is a class action or concerns a warranty using an informal
  dispute settlement mechanism a warrantor has incorporated under 15 U.S.C. 2310(a)(3). A class
  action (other than one concerning a warranty using such a mechanism) must still afford that cure
  opportunity, which the named plaintiffs provide by notifying the defendant at that time that they
  are acting on behalf of the class (15 U.S.C. 2310(e)).

## Product behaviour it assumes

- A warranty or returns workflow classifies each written assurance as a written warranty, a general
  policy statement, a free-trial or return-for-credit term, or a service contract before it is
  published, since each category carries different disclosure duties and different labels are
  appropriate to each.
- Checkout and product pages for any consumer product carrying a written warranty display or link
  the full warranty text before purchase, consistent with whichever pre-sale option (physical
  display, on-request with signage, or the website option with on-product contact information) the
  business has chosen, and keep that link live and current after the sale.
- Warranty text in the system is tagged full or limited, carries that designation as a conspicuous
  caption separate from the body text, and is generated or reviewed against the minimum-standards
  checklist (remedy without charge, no cap on implied-warranty duration, consequential-damage
  exclusion on the face of the document, and refund-or-replacement after a reasonable number of
  failed repair attempts) before it can be labeled full.
- Any process that asks a customer to register a product tracks and discloses whether registration
  is a true condition of coverage or merely a convenience, and the public-facing warranty text
  states explicitly that failure to register will not affect coverage when it is not a condition.
- Support, returns, and warranty-claim scripts and macros never state that a company representative,
  a repair partner, or an internal review panel has final or binding say over a warranty dispute,
  and never state that the company alone decides what counts as a defect.
- Where the business uses, or plans to use, an informal dispute settlement mechanism, the product
  and support flow surface the part 703 disclosures (availability, contact channel, whether resort
  is a precondition, and where fuller detail sits) at both the point of sale and the point a dispute
  is raised, and route disputes to the mechanism with the recordkeeping and time limits it imposes.
- Any workflow that would condition warranty validity on using a particular repair vendor or branded
  replacement part is flagged for legal review before publication, since this duty applies by
  default and only a Commission waiver on the statutory showing removes it.

## Prescribed wording

- Where an implied-warranty duration limit is disclosed on the face of the warranty under 15 U.S.C.
  2308, it must be accompanied by the state-variation statement at PW-US-FED-1 (16 C.F.R.
  701.3(a)(7)).
- Where a warranty excludes or limits incidental or consequential damages, it must be accompanied by
  the state-variation statement at PW-US-FED-2, which may be combined with PW-US-FED-1 (16 C.F.R.
  701.3(a)(8)).
- Every written warranty covered by part 701 must carry the legal-rights statement at PW-US-FED-3
  (16 C.F.R. 701.3(a)(9)).
- The only two permitted warranty designations are "full (statement of duration) warranty" and
  "limited warranty"; see PW-US-FED-4 (15 U.S.C. 2303(a)(1), (2)).

## Dates

- The Act took effect 6 months after January 4, 1975, and does not apply to consumer products
  manufactured before that effective date (15 U.S.C. 2312(a)).
- Section 2302(a)'s disclosure duty takes effect 6 months after final publication of the
  Commission's implementing rules under that section, extendable to one year for good cause to let
  designated supplier classes come into compliance (15 U.S.C. 2312(b)).
- The Commission was required to promulgate initial implementing rules as soon as possible after
  January 4, 1975, and no later than one year after that date (15 U.S.C. 2312(c)).
- Parts 701 and 702 apply only to written warranties on products manufactured after December 31,
  1976, one year after the December 31, 1975 promulgation of those rules (16 C.F.R. 700.12).
- The text of the statute in this file is current through Pub. L. 119-121 (2026-10-09); the text of
  parts 700 to 703 is current as of the eCFR edition of 2026-10-16.

## Open points to raise

- Whether the FTC has since defined, by rule, what counts as a "reasonable number of attempts" to
  remedy a defect for purposes of the refund-or-replacement duty is not settled by this file; the
  statute only authorizes such a rule, it does not state a number or a test (15 U.S.C. 2304(a)(4)).
- Whether a specific duty a warrantor wants to impose as a condition of remedy, beyond notification,
  has been found reasonable in a Commission rulemaking or enforcement proceeding is not settled by
  this file and needs a current check of Commission rules and decisions (15 U.S.C. 2304(b)(1)).
- Whether the Commission has granted any waiver under 15 U.S.C. 2302(c) allowing a warranty to be
  conditioned on a particular branded article or service is not settled by this file; a drafter
  relying on such a waiver needs the Federal Register notice granting it.
