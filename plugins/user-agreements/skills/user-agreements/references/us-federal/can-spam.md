# CAN-SPAM Act

Citation: 15 U.S.C. 7701 et seq. (chapter 103)
Version: current through Pub. L. 119-121 (2026-10-09)
Official text: https://uscode.house.gov/browse/prelim@title15&edition=prelim
Law as of: 2026-10-20

This file covers the whole chapter as codified, § 7701 to § 7713. It does not reproduce the FTC's
implementing regulations issued under § 7711, including the primary-purpose criteria the
Commission was told to define and any current Do-Not-E-Mail or sexually oriented material mark
text: those sit in the Code of Federal Regulations and the Federal Register, not in the statute
itself, and a drafter needs the current regulatory text for those points. The administrative-report
sections, § 7708 to § 7710, set one-time reporting deadlines that ran in 2004 and 2005 and are
covered below only for completeness.

## When it applies

- The chapter governs "commercial electronic mail messages": a message whose primary purpose is
  the commercial advertisement or promotion of a commercial product or service, including content
  on a website run for a commercial purpose (§ 7702(2)(A)). Including a reference to a commercial
  entity or a link to its website does not by itself make a message commercial; the test looks at
  the message's contents and circumstances as a whole (§ 7702(2)(D)).
- It excludes "transactional or relationship" messages, which carry none of the duties below. A
  message is transactional or relationship if its primary purpose is one of five things: completing
  or confirming a transaction the recipient already agreed to; giving warranty, product recall,
  safety, or security information about a product or service the recipient uses or bought; giving
  notice of a change in the terms of, a change in the recipient's status under, or a periodic
  account statement for, an ongoing relationship such as a subscription, membership, account, or
  loan; giving information tied to an employment relationship or benefit plan the recipient is in;
  or delivering goods or services, including updates or upgrades, the recipient is entitled to
  under a prior transaction (§ 7702(17)(A)(i) to (v)). The Commission may change this list by
  regulation to fit changes in email technology or practice (§ 7702(17)(B)).
- The duties run to whoever "initiates" a message: originates or transmits it, or procures its
  origination or transmission; more than one person can be an initiator of the same message
  (§ 7702(9)). "Procure" means intentionally paying, giving other consideration, or inducing
  someone else to initiate a message on one's behalf (§ 7702(12)). Routine conveyance, the
  automatic technical transmission, routing, relaying, handling, or storage of a message for
  someone else who picked the recipients or supplied the addresses, is not initiating
  (§ 7702(9), (15)).
- The "sender" is the person who initiates the message and whose product, service, or website it
  advertises or promotes. If a company operates through separately branded lines of business or
  divisions and holds itself out to the recipient as that particular line or division rather than
  as the parent entity, the line or division is the sender, not the parent (§ 7702(16)).
- The core duties in § 7704(a) and § 7704(b) run to messages sent to a "protected computer," a term
  that takes its meaning from 18 U.S.C. § 1030(e)(2)(B) (§ 7702(13)).
- § 7712(b) applies in addition where a message is a "mobile service commercial message": a
  commercial email message sent directly to a wireless device used by a subscriber of commercial
  mobile service, as defined at 47 U.S.C. § 332(d), in connection with that service (§ 7712(d)). A
  message to a device a subscriber happens to use, but unconnected to the mobile service itself,
  falls outside this narrower definition even if § 7704's general duties still reach it.

## What the documents must say

- Every commercial email message must carry a working return email address or other
  internet-based opt-out mechanism, clearly and conspicuously displayed, that a recipient can use
  to ask not to receive further commercial email from that sender at the address the message
  reached, and that stays able to receive such requests for at least 30 days after the message was
  sent (§ 7704(a)(3)(A)). A sender can meet this with a list or menu of message types the recipient
  can choose to get or not, as long as the list includes an option to decline every commercial
  message from the sender (§ 7704(a)(3)(B)). A temporary, unexpected technical failure of the
  mechanism, beyond the sender's control and fixed within a reasonable time, does not break this
  duty (§ 7704(a)(3)(C)).
- Every commercial email message to a protected computer must give clear and conspicuous
  identification that it is an advertisement or solicitation, clear and conspicuous notice of the
  opt-out opportunity, and a valid physical postal address of the sender (§ 7704(a)(5)(A)(i) to
  (iii)). The identification duty alone drops out where the recipient already gave prior
  affirmative consent to receiving the message; the opt-out notice and the postal address still
  apply (§ 7704(a)(5)(B)).
- "Affirmative consent" means the recipient expressly consented to receive the message, either
  answering a clear and conspicuous consent request or acting on their own initiative, and, where
  the sender is not the party the recipient originally consented to, the recipient got clear and
  conspicuous notice, at the time of consenting, that the address could be passed to that other
  party for commercial email (§ 7702(1)(A), (B)).
- A terms document for a company that pays others to promote it by email should make the promoted
  business responsible for watching for false or misleading header information in messages that
  promote it, and for either stopping the sending or detecting it and reporting it to the
  Commission; knowing of a violation, expecting a benefit from it, and doing nothing is itself the
  statutory trigger for liability (§ 7705(a)(1) to (3)). Only the Commission may enforce this
  liability; a State or an internet access service provider cannot bring its own action for it
  (§ 7705(c)).
- A document for a company that sends or facilitates commercial email should describe the
  enforcement exposure beyond the FTC's default authority (§ 7706(a)): an internet access service
  provider adversely affected may itself sue for an injunction or damages over a violation of
  § 7704(a)(1), (b), or (d), or a pattern or practice violating § 7704(a)(2) to (5), with statutory
  damages up to $100 per violation for a § 7704(a)(1) violation or up to $25 per violation for any
  other, capped at $1,000,000 and triple on a showing of willful and knowing conduct
  (§ 7706(g)(1), (3)). A State attorney general or official may likewise sue as parens patriae on
  behalf of State residents for the same kinds of violations, with statutory damages up to $250 per
  violation, capped at $2,000,000 and likewise tripled (§ 7706(f)(1), (3)). For a violation of
  § 7704(a)(1)(C), (a)(2), (a)(4)(A)(ii) to (iv), (b)(1)(A), or (b)(3), none of these enforcers has
  to prove the sender's state of mind: not the Commission or the FCC seeking a cease-and-desist
  order or an injunction (§ 7706(e)), not a State seeking an injunction over the same listed
  violations (§ 7706(f)(2)), and not a State seeking damages over them, where proof of actual or
  fairly implied knowledge is otherwise that State's burden (§ 7706(f)(9)).
- A document covering email sent to US recipients generally can state that CAN-SPAM is the federal
  floor and that it displaces State statutes that expressly regulate commercial email, apart from
  their anti-falsity or anti-deception rules (§ 7707(b)(1)).

## What the documents must not say

- No message, commercial or transactional, may carry header information that is materially false
  or materially misleading (§ 7704(a)(1)). Header information that is technically accurate but
  whose access was obtained by false or fraudulent pretenses is materially misleading
  (§ 7704(a)(1)(A)); a "from" line that accurately identifies any person who initiated the message
  is not materially false or misleading (§ 7704(a)(1)(B)); and header information is materially
  misleading if it fails to accurately identify a protected computer because the sender knowingly
  relays through another protected computer to disguise where the message came from
  (§ 7704(a)(1)(C)). "Materially" here reaches alteration or concealment that would impair an
  internet access service's, a complainant's, or law enforcement's ability to identify, locate, or
  respond to the initiator, or the recipient's own ability to respond (§ 7704(a)(6)).
- No commercial message may carry a subject heading the sender has actual knowledge, or knowledge
  fairly implied from objective circumstances, would likely mislead a reasonable recipient about a
  material fact regarding the message's content or subject (§ 7704(a)(2)).
- Once a recipient opts out through the § 7704(a)(3) mechanism, the sender, and anyone acting on
  the sender's behalf, may not send a message within the scope of that request more than 10
  business days after the request arrives, may not help send one with actual or fairly implied
  knowledge that it would break this rule, and may not sell, lease, exchange, or otherwise transfer
  or release that recipient's address for any purpose other than complying with this chapter or
  other law (§ 7704(a)(4)(A)(i) to (iv)). The Commission may change the 10-business-day period by
  regulation (§ 7704(c)(1)). The opt-out does not expire on its own and does not reset because time
  has passed; it only stops applying if the recipient later gives a fresh affirmative consent after
  the opt-out request (§ 7704(a)(4)(B)).
- No message may be sent, or assisted in sending through providing or picking addresses, if it is
  already unlawful under § 7704(a) and the sender had actual or fairly implied knowledge that the
  recipient's address was harvested from a website or online service that, at the time of
  harvesting, posted a notice saying it would not transfer addresses to others for sending email,
  or that the address was generated by combining names, letters, or numbers into permutations
  (§ 7704(b)(1)(A)(i), (ii)). This paragraph creates no ownership interest in such addresses
  (§ 7704(b)(1)(B)). No person may use scripts or other automated means to register for multiple
  email or online user accounts to send, or help someone else send, a message that is already
  unlawful under § 7704(a) (§ 7704(b)(2)). No person may knowingly relay or retransmit such a
  message from a protected computer or network they accessed without authorization
  (§ 7704(b)(3)). Each of these three requires an underlying § 7704(a) violation; none is a
  freestanding prohibition.
- No commercial message containing sexually oriented material (material depicting sexually
  explicit conduct as defined at 18 U.S.C. § 2256, unless the depiction is a small and
  insignificant part of a whole not primarily devoted to sexual matters, § 7704(d)(4)) may go to a
  protected computer unless its subject heading carries the marks or notices the Commission has
  prescribed for this purpose, and unless the content initially viewable on opening the message,
  without further action by the recipient, is limited to those marks or notices, the § 7704(a)(5)
  disclosures, and instructions or a mechanism for reaching the sexually oriented material
  (§ 7704(d)(1)(A), (B)). This duty drops out where the recipient already gave prior affirmative
  consent (§ 7704(d)(2)). Knowingly violating it is a crime, fined under title 18 or punished by up
  to 5 years in prison, or both (§ 7704(d)(5)).
- A third party that only supplies goods, products, property, or services to a business that
  violates § 7705(a) is not liable for that violation, unless the third party owns, or holds more
  than a 50 percent ownership or economic interest in, the violator's business, or has actual
  knowledge of the unlawful promotion and expects an economic benefit from it (§ 7705(b)(1), (2)(A),
  (B)).
- A document may not claim that CAN-SPAM displaces State trespass, contract, or tort law, or other
  State fraud or computer-crime law: those survive the preemption in § 7707(b)(1) untouched
  (§ 7707(b)(2)(A), (B)). It may not claim that CAN-SPAM limits enforcement of 47 U.S.C. §§ 223 or
  231, the federal obscenity statute (18 U.S.C. chapter 71) or the federal child sexual
  exploitation statute (18 U.S.C. chapter 110), any other federal criminal statute, or the
  Commission's existing FTC Act authority over materially false or deceptive representations or
  unfair practices in commercial email (§ 7707(a)(1), (2)). It may not claim CAN-SPAM affects the
  lawfulness, under other law, of an internet access service's policy of declining to carry certain
  kinds of email (§ 7707(c)). For a mobile service commercial message, it may not claim that
  § 7712(b)'s FCC rules replace rather than add to the general § 7704 duties, or that this chapter
  displaces 47 U.S.C. § 227 (the Telephone Consumer Protection Act) or the Commission's § 6102
  rules (§ 7712(a)).
- A compliance document may not claim the FTC can require specific words, characters, marks, or
  labels for the general advertisement or solicitation identification duty in § 7704(a)(5)(A), or
  dictate where in the message that identification must sit, for instance the subject line or the
  body (§ 7711(b)). The one place the statute does let the Commission fix exact mark text is the
  sexually oriented material subject-line notice under § 7704(d)(3), a separate and narrower
  mechanism.

## Product behaviour it assumes

- Email-sending features classify every outbound template as commercial or transactional against
  the § 7702(2)/(17) test before it ships, and re-run that classification when a template's content
  changes, since a message that mixes transactional content with a promotional element still needs
  the primary-purpose analysis.
- The opt-out mechanism on commercial mail is live and monitored from the moment a message sends,
  stays able to accept requests for at least 30 days, and is wired to a process that stops sending
  within the scope of a request inside 10 business days of receiving it, with no manual step that
  could miss the deadline.
- Once an address is marked opted out, the system blocks it from commercial sends within the scope
  of that request indefinitely, and blocks any sale, lease, exchange, or other transfer of that
  address for anything other than a compliance purpose, until a fresh affirmative consent, dated
  after the opt-out, is recorded for that address.
- List-acquisition workflows (purchased lists, scraped lists, partner-supplied lists) record where
  each address came from and screen for the two § 7704(b)(1) markers, a no-transfer-notice source
  and signs of generated or permutated addresses, before the list is used for commercial sends.
- Every commercial template carries, in the body, an ad or solicitation label unless the
  recipient's prior affirmative consent is on file, the opt-out notice, and the sender's current
  valid physical postal address; none of these three is left to be added later.
- Where a marketing or affiliate program pays others to promote the business by email, a monitoring
  or reporting channel exists for the promoted business to detect and report § 7704(a)(1)
  violations in messages promoting it to the Commission.
- Any sexually oriented commercial content is gated behind the current FTC-prescribed subject-line
  mark and a first-screen view limited to that mark, the standard disclosures, and access
  instructions, for every recipient who has not given prior affirmative consent.
- A platform or email-sending service whose customers compose and target their own messages keeps
  its own technical role within automatic transmission, routing, relaying, handling, or storage for
  messages whose recipients the customer selected, so that the platform itself is not an
  initiator; if platform staff or systems also pick recipients or write subject lines for a
  customer's message, that activity is tracked separately since it can make the platform an
  initiator regardless of what the terms say.

## Prescribed wording

None. CAN-SPAM fixes functional requirements, a working opt-out mechanism, a 30-day minimum, a
10-business-day deadline, clear and conspicuous identification, a valid postal address, rather than
mandating specific sentences (§ 7704(a), § 7711(b)). The one exact-wording mechanism in this
chapter is the sexually oriented material mark the Commission prescribes under § 7704(d)(3); its
current text lives in the Federal Register, not in the statute, so it is not reproduced here.

## Dates

- Enacted Pub. L. 108-187, Dec. 16, 2003. Most sections, including § 7701 to § 7707 and § 7711 to
  § 7713, took effect January 1, 2004 (Pub. L. 108-187 § 16).
- The Commission's deadline to prescribe the sexually oriented material marks under § 7704(d)(3)
  ran 120 days from December 16, 2003.
- § 7708 (the Do-Not-E-Mail registry study) was excluded from the January 1, 2004 effective-date
  clause. It required an FTC report within 6 months of December 16, 2003, and let the Commission
  implement a registry plan no earlier than 9 months after that date (§ 7708(a), (b)).
- § 7709 required a 24-month effectiveness study, due within 24 months of December 16, 2003.
- § 7710 required a rewards-system report within 9 months of December 16, 2003, and a subject-line
  identifiability plan report within 18 months of that date.
- § 7712(b) gave the FCC 270 days to issue its mobile-messaging rules once this section took
  effect.
- The text in this file is current through Pub. L. 119-121 (2026-10-09).

## Open points to raise

- Whether the FTC has since issued the § 7702(2)(C) primary-purpose regulation, or modified the
  five transactional categories under § 7702(17)(B), is not settled by this file: the statute only
  requires the regulation, it does not state the Commission's criteria, and a drafter relying on
  edge cases in the commercial/transactional line should check current FTC rules under § 7711.
- The current text of the FTC-prescribed sexually oriented material marks under § 7704(d)(3) is
  not in this file; a drafter building the subject-line and first-screen gating needs that text
  from the Federal Register.
- Whether the Do-Not-E-Mail registry contemplated by § 7708 was ever implemented, and in what form,
  is not settled by this file; the statute only required a report and authorized implementation no
  earlier than 9 months after enactment, it did not itself create an ongoing registry.
