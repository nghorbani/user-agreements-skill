# Communications Decency Act section 230

Citation: 47 U.S.C. § 230
Version: current through Pub. L. 119-121 (2026-10-09)
Official text: https://uscode.house.gov/browse/prelim@title47&edition=prelim
Law as of: 2026-10-20

This file covers the whole section: the Good Samaritan immunity in § 230(c), the parental-control notice
duty in § 230(d), the carve-outs in § 230(e), and the definitions in § 230(f). The findings and policy in
§ 230(a) and (b) state Congress's reasons for the section and create no duty or defense on their own, so
they are not restated as rules below.

## When it applies

- The immunity in § 230(c) runs to a "provider or user of an interactive computer service": any
  information service, system, or access software provider that provides or enables computer access by
  multiple users to a computer server, including a service or system that provides Internet access, and
  systems or services offered by libraries or educational institutions (§ 230(f)(2)).
- It covers only information provided by another "information content provider": a person or entity
  responsible, in whole or in part, for creating or developing the information at issue (§ 230(f)(3)). A
  platform that itself creates or develops the specific content at issue is an information content
  provider as to that content, and does not get the publisher or speaker immunity for it (§ 230(c)(1),
  (f)(3)).
- The notice duty in § 230(d) applies to a provider of interactive computer service at the time it enters
  an agreement with a customer for that service.
- The carve-outs in § 230(e) each state their own scope; read them one at a time rather than assuming the
  shield stops at one boundary for every purpose (§ 230(e)(1) to (5)).

## What the documents must say

- A terms document may state that the platform is not treated as the publisher or speaker of information
  provided by another information content provider, and that reviewing, filtering, labeling, restricting,
  or removing content does not make it one (§ 230(c)(1)).
- A provider of interactive computer service must, when entering an agreement with a customer for the
  service and in a manner it deems appropriate, notify the customer that parental control protections
  (such as computer hardware, software, or filtering services) are commercially available that may help
  limit a minor's access to harmful material, and identify, or give the customer access to information
  identifying, current providers of such protections (§ 230(d)). See Product behaviour below for what this
  means for the signup flow.
- A document may accurately state that an inconsistent state or local law cannot be the basis of liability
  under § 230(e)(3); it may not state that § 230 displaces a state or local law that is consistent with it,
  since that law stays enforceable (§ 230(e)(3)).
- A document may describe the platform's right, voluntarily and in good faith, to restrict access to or
  availability of material it considers obscene, lewd, lascivious, filthy, excessively violent, harassing, or
  otherwise objectionable, whether or not that material is constitutionally protected, and to give users or
  other information content providers the technical means to do the same, as a voluntary exercise of
  discretion rather than a promised or guaranteed review (§ 230(c)(2)(A), (c)(2)(B)).

## What the documents must not say

- No clause may claim the § 230(c) shield for content the platform itself created or developed, in whole
  or in part; as to that content the platform is an information content provider, not merely a provider or
  user of the service carrying someone else's content (§ 230(f)(3)).
- No clause may state or imply that § 230 limits the enforcement of federal criminal law: the obscenity and
  child-exploitation provisions of chapters 71 and 110 of title 18, 47 U.S.C. §§ 223 or 231, or any other
  federal criminal statute (§ 230(e)(1)).
- No clause may state or imply that § 230 expands or limits any law on intellectual property (§ 230(e)(2)).
- No clause may state or imply that § 230 limits the Electronic Communications Privacy Act of 1986, any
  amendment made by that Act, or a similar state law (§ 230(e)(4)).
- No clause may claim the § 230(c)(1) publisher/speaker immunity or the § 230(c)(2)(B) technical-means
  immunity as a defense to a civil sex-trafficking claim under 18 U.S.C. § 1595 tied to a violation of 18
  U.S.C. § 1591, or to a state criminal prosecution tied to a violation of 18 U.S.C. § 1591 or § 2421A: only
  the § 230(c)(2)(A) good-faith-moderation immunity remains available there, and for the § 2421A prong only
  where promotion or facilitation of prostitution is illegal in the jurisdiction the conduct targeted
  (§ 230(e)(5)(A) to (C)).
- No clause may promise full or guaranteed review of user content, or otherwise vouch for specific user
  content; that kind of promise can support an argument that the platform co-developed the content it
  reviewed, which risks the information-content-provider carve-out above rather than the wording of any one
  clause (§ 230(f)(3)).

## Product behaviour it assumes

- The signup or onboarding flow for an interactive computer service delivers the parental-control notice at
  the point the customer enters the agreement, rather than placing it somewhere in a terms document the
  customer may never open, and the notice identifies current providers of parental control protections or
  links to a source that does (§ 230(d)).
- Content-moderation tooling (removal, restriction, filtering, demotion, ranking, labeling) is built and
  operated as a voluntary, discretionary function the platform exercises in good faith, not as a promised or
  guaranteed review of every item, since the immunity in § 230(c)(2)(A) turns on good faith and
  voluntariness rather than on completeness (§ 230(c)(2)(A)).
- Workflows that let the platform's own staff or systems originate, substantially edit, or curate specific
  user-facing content are tracked separately from workflows that only carry, moderate, or technically
  process content other users or information content providers supply, since the first kind of content
  falls outside the § 230(c)(1) shield (§ 230(f)(3)).

## Prescribed wording

None. § 230 does not prescribe exact wording for a notice, disclosure, or clause; the parental-control
notice duty in § 230(d) leaves the manner and format to the provider.

## Dates

- § 230 was added in 1996 (Pub. L. 104-104, title V, § 509) and later amended in 1998 (Pub. L. 105-277, div.
  C, title XIV, § 1404(a)) and in 2018 (Pub. L. 115-164, § 4(a)).
- The text in this file is current through Pub. L. 119-121 (2026-10-09).

## Open points to raise

- Whether a platform's specific review, editing, or curation workflow for a given content type crosses from
  carrying another party's content into creating or developing it, so that § 230(f)(3) takes that content
  outside the § 230(c)(1) shield, is a fact-specific question about how that workflow runs; it is not settled
  by the clause wording alone and a drafter should raise it with counsel for any workflow where platform
  staff substantially edit or curate user-facing content before publication.
