# Copyright Act: computer program copies and online service provider safe harbor

Citation: 17 U.S.C. §§ 117, 512
Version: current through Pub. L. 119-121 (2026-10-09)
Official text: https://uscode.house.gov/browse/prelim@title17&edition=prelim
Law as of: 2026-10-20

This file covers two unrelated limitations on copyright liability. Section 117 lets the owner of a
copy of a computer program make the copies needed to run or archive it. Section 512 is the DMCA
safe harbor: it shields a qualifying online service provider from monetary liability, and narrows
the injunctive relief a court can order against it, for four separate categories of user conduct.
Each category stands on its own conditions; partial compliance with one does not borrow from
another (§ 512(n)).

## When it applies

- Section 117 applies whenever a terms document or license addresses what a licensee or purchaser
  may do with their own copy of a computer program: running it, archiving it, transferring it, or
  having it serviced (§ 117(a) to (d)).
- Section 512 applies to any product that stores, transmits, caches, or links to material at a
  user's direction: hosting, file sharing, user-generated-content platforms, search and directory
  services, caching proxies, and network transit (§ 512(a) to (d)).
- A product seeking § 512 protection must meet two conditions that apply across every category
  before any category-specific condition is reached: a reasonably implemented and communicated
  policy for terminating repeat infringers in appropriate circumstances, and non-interference with
  standard technical measures that copyright owners and service providers develop by broad,
  open, multi-industry consensus, that are available on reasonable and nondiscriminatory terms, and
  that do not impose substantial costs or burdens on the provider (§ 512(i)(1), (i)(2)).
- A public or other nonprofit institution of higher education gets a separate, narrower limitation
  when the service provider conduct at issue is a faculty member's or graduate student's teaching
  or research activity, rather than the general § 512(a) to (d) categories (§ 512(e)).

## What the documents must say

- A license or terms document may state that the owner of a copy of a program may make or
  authorize one new copy or adaptation of it without infringing, where that copy is either an
  essential step to run the program with a machine and used in no other way, or purely archival and
  destroyed once possession of the program stops being rightful (§ 117(a)(1), (a)(2)).
- A license may state that such copies travel only together with the original copy and only as part
  of a transfer of all rights in the program, and that adaptations may be transferred only with the
  copyright owner's authorization (§ 117(b)).
- A license may state that the owner or lessee of a machine may let a copy of a program be made
  solely because activating the machine triggers it, for maintenance or repair only, used in no
  other manner and destroyed immediately after the work is done, and that a part of the program not
  needed to activate the machine must not be accessed beyond that incidental copy (§ 117(c)(1),
  (c)(2)). "Maintenance" means servicing the machine to make it work to its original specifications
  and any authorized changes to them; "repair" means restoring it to that state (§ 117(d)(1),
  (d)(2)).
- Terms for a hosting, user-generated-content, caching, or linking product may describe the four
  § 512 safe harbors and condition them on the same facts the statute conditions them on:
  - Transitory network communications: the provider is not liable for routing or transmitting
    material at a user's direction if it does not select the content or the recipients, keeps no
    copy ordinarily accessible beyond what the transmission needs, and passes the material through
    unmodified (§ 512(a)(1) to (5)).
  - System caching: the provider is not liable for automatic, temporary caching of material a third
    party made available online if the cached copy is unmodified, is refreshed under rules the
    originating site sets under a generally accepted protocol, does not defeat hit-count technology
    meeting specific conditions, respects any access condition the originating site imposes (a fee
    or password), and is expeditiously updated or removed once the provider is notified, in a
    notice confirming it, that the original was taken down or a court ordered it down (§ 512(b)(1),
    (b)(2)(A) to (E)).
  - User-stored material: the provider is not liable for infringing material a user stored on its
    system if the provider lacks actual knowledge of infringement and, absent that knowledge, is
    not aware of facts or circumstances from which infringing activity is apparent (or acts
    expeditiously once it gets either), gets no financial benefit directly attributable to the
    infringing activity where it has the right and ability to control that activity, and takes the
    material down expeditiously once it receives a notification of claimed infringement meeting
    § 512(c)(3) (§ 512(c)(1)(A) to (C)).
  - Information location tools (directories, indexes, hyperlinks): the same actual-knowledge,
    financial-benefit, and expeditious-takedown conditions as user-stored material, applied to
    referring or linking users to infringing material rather than hosting it (§ 512(d)(1) to (3)).
- Terms may state that a provider who qualifies for a § 512 safe harbor is not free of injunctions
  altogether, and that the forms a court may order differ by category. For conduct in the system-
  caching, user-stored-material, or information-location-tools categories, the court may order
  blocking access to the infringing material or activity at a particular online site, termination of
  an identified subscriber's or account holder's accounts, or other relief that is the least
  burdensome among comparably effective forms (§ 512(j)(1)(A)). For conduct in the transitory-
  communications category, the court may only order termination of an identified subscriber's or
  account holder's accounts, or an order to block access, by reasonable steps the order specifies, to
  a specific, identified online location outside the United States (§ 512(j)(1)(B)). Either way, the
  court considers the burden on the provider, the harm to the copyright owner, technical feasibility
  and effectiveness, and less burdensome alternatives (§ 512(j)(2)), and the injunction issues only
  after notice to the provider and an opportunity for the provider to appear, except for orders
  preserving evidence or orders with no material adverse effect on the provider's network
  (§ 512(j)(3)).
- A document may state that designating an agent to receive notices of claimed infringement under
  § 512(c)(2) requires two separate steps: publishing the agent's name, address, phone number, and
  email address through the service, including on the public part of its website, and filing the
  same information with the Copyright Office (§ 512(c)(2)(A), (B)).
- A document may state what a compliant notice of claimed infringement must substantially contain:
  a signature of a person authorized to act for the owner of the allegedly infringed right;
  identification of the copyrighted work, or a representative list where one notice covers multiple
  works at one site; identification of the allegedly infringing material with information
  reasonably sufficient to locate it; contact information for the complaining party; a statement of
  a good faith belief that the use is not authorized; and a statement, under penalty of perjury,
  that the information is accurate and that the complaining party is authorized to act for the
  rights owner (§ 512(c)(3)(A)(i) to (vi)).
- A document may state that a notice substantially complying with the identification and contact
  elements, but missing others, still counts toward the provider's knowledge once the provider
  promptly tries to reach the notifier or takes other reasonable steps to get a fully compliant
  notice (§ 512(c)(3)(B)(ii)).
- A takedown or counter-notice process may state what a compliant counter notification must
  substantially contain: the subscriber's signature; identification of the removed material and
  where it appeared before removal; a statement under penalty of perjury of a good faith belief
  that removal was a mistake or misidentification; and the subscriber's name, address, and phone
  number, with consent to federal jurisdiction and acceptance of service of process (§ 512(g)(3)(A)
  to (D)).
- A document may state that, absent notice that the original notifier has filed suit, the provider
  restores material removed under a user notice between 10 and 14 business days after receiving a
  valid counter notice, after first notifying the subscriber of the removal and forwarding the
  counter notice to the original notifier with a statement that restoration follows in 10 business
  days (§ 512(g)(2)(A) to (C)).
- A document may state that a provider acting in good faith to remove or disable access to material
  it reasonably believes is infringing is not liable to anyone for that action, whether or not the
  material turns out to be infringing, subject to the replacement duties above where the material
  was stored at a subscriber's direction (§ 512(g)(1), (g)(2)).
- A document may state that a service provider must expeditiously disclose, in response to a court
  clerk's subpoena obtained on the statutory showing, information sufficient to identify an alleged
  infringer, to the extent the information is available to the provider (§ 512(h)(1) to (5)).

## What the documents must not say

- No clause may promise a payout structure, revenue share, or premium placement that pays out
  against specific user uploads the platform can contractually control, where that payout is
  directly attributable to infringing activity: that risks the financial benefit that forfeits the
  § 512(c) and § 512(d) safe harbors (§ 512(c)(1)(B), § 512(d)(2)).
- No clause may claim the § 512(c) safe harbor where the provider has designated an agent only by
  publishing contact details on its own service and has not also filed those details with the
  Copyright Office, or the reverse: both steps are required (§ 512(c)(2)).
- No clause may condition the § 512(a) through (d) safe harbors on the provider monitoring its
  service or affirmatively seeking facts indicating infringing activity, except to the extent a
  standard technical measure under § 512(i) requires it, and no clause may condition them on the
  provider gaining access to, removing, or disabling access to material where doing so is otherwise
  prohibited by law (§ 512(m)(1), (m)(2)).
- No clause may state that qualifying for, or failing to qualify for, the safe harbor in one of
  § 512(a) to (d) affects whether the provider qualifies for the safe harbor in another of those
  four subsections: each is judged solely on its own criteria (§ 512(n)).
- No clause may state that failing to qualify for the § 512 limitation counts against any other
  defense the provider has, including a defense that the conduct is not infringing at all
  (§ 512(l)).
- No clause may state that a counter notice restores the material immediately or on a shorter
  timeline than the statutory window, or that the provider may skip forwarding the counter notice to
  the original notifier (§ 512(g)(2)(B), (C)).
- No notice-and-takedown clause may describe "service provider" with a single definition for every
  purpose: § 512(a) uses a narrower definition (an entity offering transmission, routing, or
  connections for digital communications between points a user specifies, without modifying the
  content) than the rest of the section, which uses a broader definition covering any provider of
  online services or network access, or the operator of the facilities for them (§ 512(k)(1)(A),
  (B)).
- No clause may state that a service provider who knowingly and materially misrepresents that
  material is infringing, or that a takedown was a mistake or misidentification, has no liability
  for that misrepresentation: § 512(f) makes that person liable for the resulting damages, costs,
  and fees (§ 512(f)).

## Product behaviour it assumes

- Software license terms that rely on § 117 track its own conditions: an archival copy is destroyed
  once possession stops being rightful, a copy made under this section is transferred only together
  with the original and only as part of a transfer of all rights, and a machine-repair copy is used
  in no other way and destroyed immediately after the work finishes (§ 117(a)(2), (b), (c)(1)).
- A product that wants the § 512(c) or § 512(d) safe harbor publishes a designated agent's name,
  address, phone number, and email address on the public part of the service, and separately files
  the same details with the Copyright Office; both records are kept current (§ 512(c)(2)(A), (B)).
  The filed information becomes part of a public directory of designated agents that the Register of
  Copyrights maintains for inspection, including through the Internet, and may charge service
  providers a fee to maintain (§ 512(c)(2)).
- The notice-and-takedown workflow accepts a notice that substantially (not necessarily exactly)
  contains the six § 512(c)(3)(A) elements, and when a notice is missing some but substantially
  contains identification of the material and contact information, the workflow attempts to reach
  the notifier or takes other reasonable steps before treating the gap as fatal to the provider's
  knowledge (§ 512(c)(3)(B)).
- The takedown workflow acts expeditiously once it has actual knowledge or becomes aware of facts
  from which infringing activity is apparent, and keeps its monetization and promotion mechanics
  platform-wide rather than tied to specific uploads, to avoid the directly-attributable financial
  benefit that forfeits the safe harbor (§ 512(c)(1)(A), (c)(1)(B)).
- The counter-notice workflow notifies the affected subscriber promptly after a takedown under a
  user notice, forwards any compliant counter notice to the original notifier with the 10-business-
  day restoration statement, and restores the material between 10 and 14 business days later unless
  the original notifier shows it has sued (§ 512(g)(2)(A) to (C)).
- The product maintains and enforces a repeat-infringer termination policy, communicated to
  subscribers and account holders, and its technical measures (DRM, fingerprinting, filtering) do
  not interfere with standard technical measures that meet the § 512(i)(2) consensus, availability,
  and no-substantial-burden conditions (§ 512(i)(1), (i)(2)).
- The product's subpoena-response process discloses to a copyright owner, on receipt of a properly
  issued subpoena tied to a compliant notification, the identifying information it has available for
  the alleged infringer, regardless of whether the provider has otherwise responded to the notice
  (§ 512(h)(3), (h)(5)).

## Prescribed wording

None. Section 512 lists the substantive elements a notice, counter notice, and designated-agent
filing must contain; it does not hand down a sentence to reproduce verbatim. Build the
notice-and-takedown and counter-notice forms from the element lists in What the documents must
say above.

## Open points to raise

- Whether a specific revenue-share, bounty, or premium-placement design counts as a financial
  benefit "directly attributable" to infringing activity under § 512(c)(1)(B) and § 512(d)(2) turns
  on how that particular payout is structured; it is not settled by the statute's wording alone and
  a drafter should raise any upload-linked payout design with counsel before relying on the safe
  harbor for it.
- Whether a particular filtering, fingerprinting, or moderation technology counts as a "standard
  technical measure" under § 512(i)(2), so that accommodating it is a condition of every safe
  harbor, depends on whether that measure in fact reflects a broad, open, multi-industry consensus;
  a drafter cannot settle that from the statute's definition alone and should raise which measures
  the product must accommodate with counsel.
