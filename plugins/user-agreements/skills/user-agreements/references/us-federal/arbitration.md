# Federal Arbitration Act

Citation: 9 U.S.C. §§ 1-16, 401-402 (chapter 1 and chapter 4, title 9)
Version: current through Pub. L. 119-121 (2026-10-09)
Official text: https://uscode.house.gov/browse/prelim@title09&edition=prelim
Law as of: 2026-10-20

## When it applies

- The Act covers a written arbitration provision in a maritime transaction or in a contract
  evidencing a transaction involving commerce, where commerce means interstate, foreign,
  territorial or District of Columbia commerce, read broadly (§ 1, § 2).
- The Act carves out, entirely, contracts of employment of seamen, railroad employees, or any
  other class of workers engaged in foreign or interstate commerce; those contracts are outside
  the Act regardless of what the clause says (§ 1).
- The Act does not apply to contracts made before January 1, 1926 (§ 14).
- Chapter 4 (§§ 401, 402) layers a separate, narrower carve-out on top of general coverage: at the
  election of the person alleging conduct that is a sexual assault dispute or a sexual harassment
  dispute, or of the named representative of a class or collective action alleging such conduct, no
  predispute arbitration agreement or predispute joint-action waiver is valid or enforceable for a
  case relating to that dispute (§ 402(a)). This applies to disputes or claims arising or accruing
  on or after March 3, 2022 (effective-date note, § 401).
- A predispute joint-action waiver under chapter 4 is defined broadly enough to reach a waiver that
  sits outside an arbitration clause entirely: it covers any agreement that would prohibit, or waive
  the right to, participation in a joint, class, or collective action in a judicial, arbitral,
  administrative, or other forum (§ 401(2)). A standalone class-action waiver in a court-only terms
  document, with no arbitration clause attached, is reached the same way for a sexual assault or
  harassment dispute.
- Whether chapter 4 applies to a dispute, and the validity and enforceability of an agreement it
  applies to, is decided by a court, never an arbitrator, regardless of whether the challenge targets
  the arbitration agreement specifically or the contract as a whole, and regardless of any clause
  purporting to delegate that decision to an arbitrator (§ 402(b)).
- Federal preemption under § 2's saving clause interacts with state contract law in three distinct
  ways a drafter needs to keep separate. A state rule that specifically disfavors arbitration, even
  while dressed in neutral unconscionability language, is preempted: this is why a mandatory
  individual-arbitration clause with a class-action waiver is enforceable against a state rule that
  would strike such waivers down as unconscionable in consumer contracts (AT&T Mobility LLC v.
  Concepcion, 563 U.S. 333 (2011)). A state's own generally applicable contract-defense law, applied
  without targeting arbitration, is not preempted: this is why a clause can still be struck down as
  unconscionable under ordinary state doctrine when its arbitration mechanism itself (not the choice
  of arbitration) is substantively and procedurally unfair, and why a scheme built around mandatory
  claim-batching and bellwether precedent can fall outside the Act's protection entirely because it
  is too far removed from the bilateral, individualized model Congress had in mind in 1925 (Heckman
  v. Live Nation Entertainment, Inc., 120 F.4th 670 (9th Cir. 2024)). A state rule voiding the waiver
  of a substantive statutory remedy, as opposed to a procedural mechanism like class treatment, is
  also not preempted, because it does not target arbitration and does not stop the parties' agreed
  claims from being arbitrated (McGill v. Citibank, N.A., 2 Cal.5th 945 (2017)).

## What the documents must say

- An arbitration clause may require individual, bilateral arbitration and bar classwide,
  consolidated, or representative proceedings; a court may not refuse to enforce that bar merely
  because it forecloses class treatment (Concepcion, 563 U.S. at 352).
- A clause that provides its own method for naming an arbitrator or umpire has that method control;
  if no method is stated, or the stated method lapses or fails, the court appoints on a party's
  application, and the default, absent a contrary agreement, is a single arbitrator (§ 5).
- An agreement may name the court where judgment on the award is to be entered; naming no court
  routes the confirmation application to the district court for the district where the award was
  made (§ 9).
- Once a court is satisfied an issue is arbitrable under a written agreement, it must stay
  litigation of that issue, unless the party seeking the stay is itself in default of arbitrating
  (§ 3). A party aggrieved by another's refusal to arbitrate may petition the district court to
  compel arbitration; the making of the agreement or the fact of default, if genuinely disputed, is
  tried summarily or, outside admiralty, by jury on demand (§ 4).
- An award becomes enforceable as a court judgment through confirmation: on an application made
  within one year, the court must confirm the award unless it is vacated, modified, or corrected
  (§ 9). Vacatur is available only where the award was procured by corruption, fraud, or undue
  means; where there was evident partiality or corruption in an arbitrator; where arbitrator
  misconduct prejudiced a party; or where the arbitrators exceeded or so imperfectly executed their
  powers that no mutual, final, definite award was made (§ 10(a)(1) to (4)). Modification or
  correction is narrower: an evident material miscalculation or misdescription, an award on a
  matter not submitted (unless it does not affect the merits), or a purely formal imperfection
  (§ 11(a) to (c)). A motion to vacate, modify, or correct must be served within three months of the
  award (§ 12).
- A delegation clause (one that sends questions about the arbitration agreement's own validity to
  the arbitrator) can still be challenged in court by showing how the rest of the agreement makes
  that delegation itself unconscionable; a court, not the arbitrator, decides that challenge
  (Heckman, 120 F.4th 670).
- If a clause lets one claimant's arbitration bind other claimants (a batching or bellwether
  design), it needs notice, participation and an opt-out or comparable protection for the bound
  claimants, and that protection has to reach them as well as the drafting party; an asymmetry
  where only the drafting party gets notice of and participates in every bellwether proceeding is a
  substantive unconscionability problem, not a neutral efficiency measure (Heckman, 120 F.4th 670).
- Any appeal right built into a private arbitration scheme needs to be checked for real-world
  symmetry, since facial neutrality alone is not enough; an appeal limited to one category of
  outcome functions as a one-way right if only one side typically produces that outcome (Heckman,
  120 F.4th 670).
- Procedural limits on discovery, page counts, or briefing length have to stay workable for
  threshold arbitrability disputes, as well as for the merits claim they were designed around
  (Heckman, 120 F.4th 670).
- A consumer's statutory right to seek public injunctive relief, meaning relief whose primary
  purpose and effect is to stop unlawful conduct that threatens future injury to the general public
  rather than to resolve the plaintiff's own dispute, can be excluded from the scope of arbitration
  and routed to court instead, without running into the rule below, so long as the clause does not
  purport to eliminate that right in every forum (McGill, 2 Cal.5th at 955, 965 to 966).
- An arbitration or class-waiver clause may still bind the parties to individual arbitration of
  damages and other individually held claims even where it excludes public-injunctive-relief
  requests from its scope (McGill, 2 Cal.5th at 966).
- A state may require a class-action-waiver provision in an adhesive consumer agreement to be
  highlighted so a consumer notices it, so long as that requirement does not conflict with the Act
  or frustrate its enforcement purpose (Concepcion, 563 U.S. at 347 n.6).

## What the documents must not say

- No clause may purport to make a predispute arbitration agreement or predispute joint-action
  waiver valid or enforceable, at the election of the person alleging it, against a sexual assault
  dispute or sexual harassment dispute arising or accruing on or after March 3, 2022, whether that
  waiver sits inside an arbitration clause or stands alone in a court-only document (§§ 401(2),
  402(a)).
- No delegation clause may be drafted on the assumption that it removes a court's power to decide
  whether chapter 4 applies, or whether the arbitration agreement itself is valid and enforceable,
  for a sexual assault or sexual harassment dispute; that question is for a court regardless of what
  the delegation clause says (§ 402(b)).
- No arbitration agreement may purport to waive, in any forum, a consumer's right to seek public
  injunctive relief under a state consumer-protection statute that makes such relief a core
  statutory remedy; a clause drafted this way is unenforceable as to that waiver under state
  contract-defense law the Act does not preempt (McGill, 2 Cal.5th at 965 to 966, 971 to 973).
- No mass-arbitration or claim-aggregation design may let the arbitration provider alone decide how
  to batch claims, select bellwether cases binding on non-participating claimants, and withhold
  notice or a timely opportunity to be heard from those bound claimants, while giving the drafting
  party notice of and participation in every bellwether proceeding (Heckman, 120 F.4th 670).
- No clause may let the arbitration provider override a claimant's own selected arbitrator, assign
  disqualification rights unevenly by side rather than by party, or let one arbitrator preside over
  multiple claimants' cases at once, where the forum's own arbitration statute does not permit that
  (Heckman, 120 F.4th 670).
- No clause may state that continued use of a site or app, by a mere visitor rather than a
  transacting party, binds that visitor to arbitration-rule changes made without notice, or apply a
  changed arbitration or forum clause retroactively to a dispute that already accrued before the
  change (Heckman, 120 F.4th 670).
- No clause may promise "individual arbitration" while an incorporated rules document actually
  allows claims to be grouped with, or bound to the result of, another claimant's case; that mismatch
  is itself evidence of unconscionability (Heckman, 120 F.4th 670).
- No severability clause can be assumed to rescue an arbitration agreement where unconscionable
  terms permeate the agreement as a whole rather than sitting in one isolated provision; a court may
  decline to sever on that ground even where a global severability clause and a backup
  arbitrator-selection provision exist (Heckman, 120 F.4th 670).

## Product behaviour it assumes

- A sexual assault or sexual harassment claim is never routed into mandatory arbitration or blocked
  from a joint, class, or collective action by the standard dispute-resolution flow; the product
  gives the claimant (or, for a class or collective claim, the named representative) the election
  chapter 4 reserves to them, and any internal process for recognizing that election treats the
  question of chapter 4's applicability as one for a court, not an internal reviewer standing in for
  an arbitrator (§§ 401, 402).
- Any claim-routing logic that would send a request for an injunction to arbitration checks first
  whether a California consumer is asking for relief that would, if granted, primarily restrain
  future unlawful conduct affecting the general public rather than resolve the claimant's own
  dispute; if so, that request is capable of proceeding in court even where the rest of the
  claimant's dispute goes to arbitration (McGill, 2 Cal.5th at 955, 965 to 966).
- A mass-arbitration or batching feature, if the product supports one, gives every bound claimant
  timely notice of, and a genuine opportunity to participate in or be heard on, any bellwether or
  representative proceeding whose outcome will bind that claimant's own case (Heckman, 120 F.4th
  670).
- Any rule-change mechanism for an arbitration clause applies prospectively only, to disputes that
  accrue after the change, and does not reach a person who has only browsed the product without
  transacting (Heckman, 120 F.4th 670).
- The arbitrator-selection and disqualification logic for any arbitration feature does not let the
  arbitration provider override a party's own selected neutral or assign disqualification rights
  unevenly between the two sides (Heckman, 120 F.4th 670).

## Prescribed wording

The Act and the three cases above do not prescribe mandatory wording for an arbitration clause, a
class-action waiver, or a public-injunctive-relief carve-out. The Act leaves the agreement's content
to the parties subject to the rules above, and the three cases decide the enforceability of clauses
the parties drafted rather than supplying model text. For wording that is prescribed elsewhere in
this skill, see `references/prescribed-wording.md`; for a drafted arbitration clause built around
the rules in this file, see `references/clauses/dispute-resolution.md`.

## Dates

- The Act's general provisions are current through Pub. L. 119-121 (2026-10-09).
- Chapter 4 (§§ 401, 402) applies to a dispute or claim arising or accruing on or after March 3,
  2022 (effective-date note, § 401).
- Concepcion was decided April 27, 2011. Heckman was filed for publication October 28, 2024. McGill
  was decided April 6, 2017.

## Open points to raise

- Whether a later Supreme Court decision has narrowed Concepcion's preemption analysis, and whether
  any federal statute since 2011 has added a predispute-arbitration carve-out for the transaction
  type a given document covers, beyond the chapter 4 carve-out already stated above, is not covered
  here; treat it as an open point for counsel before relying on Concepcion as current law.
- Whether Heckman has since been amended or superseded by a later order in the same docket (No.
  23-55770) is not covered here; confirm against the case docket before relying on this opinion as
  final.
- Whether a later California or U.S. Supreme Court decision has narrowed McGill's holding, or
  whether a statute enacted since 2017 has displaced the CLRA, UCL, or false-advertising-law remedy
  for a given transaction type, is not covered here; treat it as an open point for counsel. For the
  CLRA remedy itself, see `references/us-states/ca-civil-code.md`.
- McGill leaves open, on remand, whether the rest of an arbitration clause survives once a
  public-injunctive-relief waiver is struck from it; that depends on the clause's own severability
  wording, which a drafter controls and should set deliberately rather than leave to an unbriefed
  question (McGill, 2 Cal.5th at 974). See `references/clauses/severability-and-entire-agreement.md`
  for the severability clause itself.
- Heckman's concurrence raises, without deciding, whether a structural conflict of interest exists
  where an arbitrator is employed by a provider whose revenue and procedural rules depend heavily on
  one repeat-player client; this file does not resolve that question, and a drafter choosing an
  arbitration provider with a concentrated client base should weigh it as an unresolved risk (Heckman,
  120 F.4th 670, VanDyke, J., concurring).
