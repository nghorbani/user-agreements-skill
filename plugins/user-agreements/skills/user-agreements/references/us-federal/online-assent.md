# Online contract formation and electronic records

Citation: 15 U.S.C. 7001 et seq. (ESIGN, chapter 96); case law on contract formation on the internet
Version: ESIGN current through Pub. L. 119-121 (2026-10-09)
Official text: https://uscode.house.gov/browse/prelim@title15&edition=prelim
Law as of: 2026-10-20

This file covers two different things together because a drafter needs both at once: the federal statute
that stops a contract or disclosure from being denied effect only because it is electronic (ESIGN), and
the body of case law that decides whether a website or app ever formed a contract with a user in the
first place. ESIGN assumes a contract exists and asks whether its electronic form defeats it. The case
law asks the prior question: did clicking a button, or merely using a site, create a contract at all. A
terms document, a checkout flow or a sign-up screen has to clear both.

## When it applies

- ESIGN applies to a signature, contract or other record relating to a transaction in or affecting
  interstate or foreign commerce (15 U.S.C. § 7001(a)). A "transaction" covers the sale, lease, exchange,
  licensing or other disposition of personal property, services or an interest in real property between
  two or more persons (§ 7006(13)).
- ESIGN never forces anyone to accept electronic records or signatures. The one actor required to accept
  them is a governmental agency, and only for a record other than a contract to which it is a party. A
  business can still insist on paper; ESIGN only stops the automatic objection that a record is invalid
  solely for being electronic (§ 7001(b)(2)).
- Nine categories are excepted from ESIGN's validity guarantee regardless of consent or technology used:
  wills, codicils and testamentary trusts; state family law matters such as adoption or divorce; the UCC
  other than §§ 1-107, 1-206 and Articles 2 and 2A; court orders, notices and official court documents;
  utility cancellation notices; default, foreclosure or eviction notices tied to a primary residence;
  health or life insurance (not annuity) cancellation notices; product recall or safety-failure notices;
  and hazardous-materials transport documents (§ 7003(a), (b)). A terms document touching any of these
  needs to check other law for its form requirements.
- The case law applies whenever a business wants a user's click, tap or continued use of a site or app to
  count as agreement to a document the user did not read line by line, most often an arbitration clause,
  a class-action waiver or a forum-selection clause. The leading opinions are federal appellate decisions
  applying California law (Second Circuit: Specht v. Netscape Commc'ns Corp., 306 F.3d 17 (2d Cir. 2002);
  Meyer v. Uber Techs., Inc., 868 F.3d 66 (2d Cir. 2017). Ninth Circuit: Nguyen v. Barnes & Noble Inc.,
  763 F.3d 1171 (9th Cir. 2014); Berman v. Freedom Fin. Network, LLC, 30 F.4th 849 (9th Cir. 2022);
  Chabolla v. ClassPass, Inc., 129 F.4th 1147 (9th Cir. 2025); Godun v. JustAnswer LLC, 135 F.4th 699
  (9th Cir. 2025)). New York and California apply substantially similar rules for whether a party assented
  to a contract term (Meyer, 868 F.3d 66, slip op. at 15).
- The two-part inquiry-notice test below (conspicuous notice plus unambiguous assent) is a substitute for
  actual knowledge, and applies only where the business cannot show that the consumer had actual knowledge
  of the agreement; a business able to show actual knowledge (for example from a prior express agreement
  with the same consumer) does not need to satisfy the two-part test at all (Berman, 30 F.4th 849, slip
  op. at 3, 12).

## What the documents must say

- Formation is a two-part test, stated first by the Second Circuit and later adopted in the same form by
  the Ninth Circuit: a contract forms only if the site gives reasonably conspicuous notice of the existence
  of contract terms, and the user takes an action that unambiguously shows assent to those terms.
  Neither element substitutes for the other (Specht, 306 F.3d 17, op. at ¶44; Berman, 30 F.4th 849, slip
  op. at 3).
- Four recognized ways a site can solicit assent, each with a different default outcome: a browsewrap
  (terms sit behind a footer hyperlink, assent is said to follow from mere use of the site) is routinely
  refused enforcement; a clickwrap (the user must click an explicit "I agree" after being shown the terms)
  is routinely enforced; a scrollwrap (like clickwrap, but the user must scroll through the displayed terms
  before the "I agree" control activates) is routinely enforced and gives the strongest notice; a sign-in
  wrap (no explicit click on the terms, but text states that a specific action, such as creating an
  account, will itself constitute agreement to linked terms) sits in between and needs both parts of the
  test proven (Nguyen, 763 F.3d 1171, slip op. at 9 to 10; Meyer, 868 F.3d 66, slip op. at 18 to 19; Berman,
  30 F.4th 849, slip op. at 29, Baker, J., concurring, quoting Sellers v. JustAnswer LLC, 289 Cal. Rptr. 3d
  1, 15 (Ct. App. 2021)). Labeling an interface does not by itself answer whether notice was adequate
  (Meyer, 868 F.3d 66, slip op. at 20).
- Conspicuous notice turns on whether a reasonably prudent user would have seen it, judged by font size,
  color contrast, placement and the overall design of the page, not by what an expert user could find
  (Berman, 30 F.4th 849, slip op. at 12 to 13; Nguyen, 763 F.3d 1171, slip op. at 15 to 16). A notice in a
  font considerably smaller than the surrounding text, competing with larger headlines or bright graphics,
  fails this test even where it sits close to the action button (Berman, 30 F.4th 849, slip op. at 12).
- A hyperlink must be recognizable as a hyperlink to a reasonably prudent user, not merely to someone who
  knows to look for it. Underlining a phrase in the same color as plain text is often not enough; a
  contrasting color (the courts note blue as typical) and capitalization are customary markers that a
  reasonably prudent user would recognize (Berman, 30 F.4th 849, slip op. at 14, citing Sellers, 289 Cal.
  Rptr. 3d at 29). Users cannot be required to hover over plain-looking text or otherwise ferret out
  hyperlinks (Nguyen, 763 F.3d 1171, slip op. at 15 to 16; Berman, 30 F.4th 849, slip op. at 14).
- Proximity of a hyperlink to the button a user must click is necessary but never sufficient on its own.
  Even a well-placed, color-contrasting link, present on every page next to every checkout button, does
  not by itself give rise to constructive notice if nothing else ties that specific click to acceptance of
  terms (Nguyen, 763 F.3d 1171, slip op. at 16).
- Clicking a button does not, by itself, communicate assent to anything. A click becomes an unambiguous
  manifestation of assent only if the user is explicitly told, before clicking, that the act of clicking
  will constitute agreement to the terms (Berman, 30 F.4th 849, slip op. at 14, citing Specht, 306 F.3d 17,
  op. at ¶32). "A user's click of a button can be construed as an unambiguous manifestation of
  assent only if the user is explicitly advised that the act of clicking will constitute assent to the
  terms and conditions of an agreement" (Berman, 30 F.4th 849, slip op. at 14). The notice must explicitly
  name the legal significance of the action the user is about to take, not merely describe that terms exist
  somewhere (Berman, 30 F.4th 849, slip op. at 14; Godun, 135 F.4th 699, slip op. at 17 to 18, calling this
  requirement "step two").
- The notice sentence's wording has to name the actual action the actual button performs. A notice reading
  "By signing up you agree" fails if the only control on the page is a button labeled "Continue," because
  the words do not match the button; a notice naming the button by its real label passes (Chabolla, 129
  F.4th 1147, slip op. at 19; Godun, 135 F.4th 699, slip op. at 17 to 18, citing Chabolla). An unrelated
  prompt or question placed between the notice and the action button (for example an irrelevant gift-card
  question just above a "Redeem now" button) can itself make the notice ambiguous, since the user may
  reasonably read the button as referring to that unrelated prompt instead of to the terms (Chabolla, 129
  F.4th 1147, slip op. at 19 to 20).
- Conspicuousness and assent-linkage are judged together but are separate requirements, and the context of
  the transaction (a one-off purchase versus signing up for an ongoing account) is relevant only to
  whether notice was conspicuous, never to whether the assent-linkage wording was explicit enough; a
  business cannot argue that an obviously contractual, ongoing relationship substitutes for the missing
  "By clicking . . ." sentence (Godun, 135 F.4th 699, slip op. at 21 to 22, citing Sellers, 289 Cal. Rptr.
  3d at 26). Transactions that a typical consumer would expect to create an ongoing relationship get a
  lower conspicuousness bar than one-off transactions, which are held to stricter scrutiny (Berman, 30
  F.4th 849, slip op. at 32, Baker, J., concurring, citing Sellers, 289 Cal. Rptr. 3d at 25 to 26, 29 to
  30).
- Where a flow spans several screens, notice and assent must coincide on the one screen where the user
  takes the action that is argued to form the contract. A court will not add up partial notice from one
  screen and a partial assent-linkage statement from a different screen to manufacture a contract that no
  single screen alone would support: "three faulty notices do not equal a proper one" (Chabolla, 129 F.4th
  1147, slip op. at 21).
- Spatial and temporal coupling both help show unambiguous assent: the notice sentence sitting directly
  next to the action button (spatial), presented at the exact moment of signing up or completing a
  purchase rather than before or after (temporal), together let a reasonable user connect the click to the
  terms (Meyer, 868 F.3d 66, slip op. at 25, quoting Schnabel v. Trilegiant Corp., 697 F.3d 110, 127 (2d
  Cir. 2012)). A notice crowded among many other links, several font sizes and colors, and advertisements,
  with the notice not adjacent to the order button, fails where an uncluttered page with the notice
  directly below the button succeeds (Meyer, 868 F.3d 66, slip op. at 24, contrasting Nicosia v.
  Amazon.com, Inc., 834 F.3d 220 (2d Cir. 2016)).
- A pre-checked assent box does not help a business show assent. Had the user instead been required to
  affirmatively check an unchecked box or click an "I accept" button, that would have created a clickwrap
  agreement and been presumptively enforceable (Godun, 135 F.4th 699, slip op. at 21, n.6, citing B.D. v.
  Blizzard Ent., Inc., 76 Cal. App. 5th 931, 944 to 945 (2022)).
- Messages, texts, emails or chat disclaimers sent after a user has already signed up rarely supply the
  missing notice, because they are not presented at the moment the user takes the action argued to form
  the contract and are not obviously contractual (Godun, 135 F.4th 699, slip op. at 23 to 24, n.7).
- A terms document may state that a contract, signature or other record will not be denied legal effect,
  validity or enforceability solely because it is in electronic form, or because electronic signature or
  an electronic record was used to form it (§ 7001(a)(1), (a)(2)).
- A business that must give a consumer a legally required written disclosure may substitute an electronic
  record for that writing only by clearing a four-part gate, all four parts required together: the
  consumer affirmatively consented and has not withdrawn that consent; before consenting, the consumer saw
  a clear and conspicuous statement covering the paper option, the withdrawal right and any fees or
  conditions on withdrawal, whether consent covers one transaction or a category of records, the
  withdrawal procedure and the procedure to update the consumer's contact information, and the paper-copy
  procedure and any fee; before consenting, the consumer saw a hardware and software statement and then
  consented electronically in a way that reasonably demonstrates the consumer can access that electronic
  form; and, if hardware or software requirements later change in a way that creates a material risk the
  consumer cannot access a later record, the business repeats the statement and the access-demonstration
  step (§ 7001(c)(1)(A) to (D)). Failing only the consent-confirmation step of this gate does not by
  itself void the contract, but a flow that skips the rest of the gate cannot rely on § 7001(c) to satisfy
  a writing requirement (§ 7001(c)(3)).
- Withdrawal of a consumer's consent to electronic records is prospective only: it does not undo the
  validity of electronic records already provided before the withdrawal took effect, and the withdrawal
  itself takes effect within a reasonable time after the business receives it. If the business fails to
  repeat the hardware-and-software statement and the access-demonstration step required when requirements
  change materially (§ 7001(c)(1)(D)), the consumer may elect to treat that failure itself as a withdrawal
  of consent (§ 7001(c)(4)).
- Where another statute requires a signature or record to be notarized, acknowledged, verified or made
  under oath, that requirement is satisfied by the electronic signature of the person authorized to
  notarize or acknowledge it, together with whatever other information that other statute requires to
  accompany the signature or record (§ 7001(g)).
- ESIGN does not change the proximity any other statute, regulation or rule of law requires for a warning,
  notice, disclosure or other record that must be posted, displayed or publicly affixed; a placement
  requirement from that other law survives alongside the electronic-form permission ESIGN itself gives
  (§ 7001(f)).
- A required retained record is satisfied electronically only if it accurately reflects the original
  information and stays accessible, for the required period, to everyone entitled to it, in a form that
  can be accurately reproduced later (§ 7001(d)(1)). This does not reach information whose sole purpose is
  enabling transmission (§ 7001(d)(1), (2)).
- Even after that, a writing requirement can still be denied effect if the electronic record is not in a
  form capable of being retained and accurately reproduced later by everyone entitled to it: retrievability
  is the operative test, not merely that something was stored electronically (§ 7001(e)).
- An automated step in forming, creating or delivering a contract (an automated acceptance trigger, an
  automated renewal confirmation) does not by itself deny the contract's validity, so long as that
  automated action is legally attributable to the person to be bound (§ 7001(h)).

## What the documents must not say

- No clause may claim that clicking a button or continuing to use a site, standing alone and without any
  notice tying that specific act to acceptance, created a binding agreement; courts call this an
  unenforceable browsewrap and the burden of designing an adequate notice falls on the website owner, not
  on the user to ferret one out (Nguyen, 763 F.3d 1171, slip op. at 16).
- No clause may rely on an assent sentence that only says "I agree" or something like "I understand and
  agree to the Terms & Conditions," with no explanatory clause naming the action the user must take, as
  proof of assent; courts have repeatedly held that wording insufficient regardless of how prominent or
  how close to the button it sits (Berman, 30 F.4th 849, slip op. at 13 to 14; Godun, 135 F.4th 699, slip
  op. at 20 to 21, calling this failure "step two").
- No clause may treat a state's electronic-transactions rule as displacing § 7001 unless that state law
  either enacts the 1999 Uniform Electronic Transactions Act, or adopts an independent, technology-neutral
  alternative consistent with § 7001 that (if enacted after June 30, 2000) names this chapter; any other
  state rule narrowing § 7001 is preempted, and a state cannot use nonelectronic-delivery requirements to
  route around that result (§ 7002(a), (c)).
- No clause may claim ESIGN validity for a record in the nine excepted categories listed above (wills,
  family law, most of the UCC, court documents, utility or primary-residence default notices, health or
  life insurance cancellation, product recall or safety notices, hazardous-materials transport documents)
  without checking the other law that actually governs that record's form (§ 7003(a), (b)).
- No clause may rely on a one-size-fits-all arbitration or forum-selection clause presented only through a
  footer link as binding a consumer who had no actual or constructive notice of it; the dispute-resolution
  clause fails with the rest of the terms if the terms themselves were never accepted (Specht, 306 F.3d 17,
  op. at ¶¶1, 44; Berman, 30 F.4th 849, slip op. at 2).

## Product behaviour it assumes

- The checkout, registration or sign-up screen that is meant to form the contract displays the assent
  notice on that same screen, in a font size and color comparably prominent to the surrounding text, placed
  directly next to (not merely somewhere on the same page as) the control that completes the action, and
  visible without the user having to scroll to find it (Meyer, 868 F.3d 66, slip op. at 24 to 25; Berman,
  30 F.4th 849, slip op. at 12 to 13).
- Any hyperlink to the terms is rendered in a color that contrasts with surrounding plain text and is
  distinguishable as a clickable link, not merely underlined in the same shade as the rest of the sentence
  (Berman, 30 F.4th 849, slip op. at 13; Godun, 135 F.4th 699, slip op. at 18 to 19, n.4, treating
  formatting as one factor among several rather than a fixed checklist).
- The assent sentence itself names the exact control label the user is about to activate, worded along the
  lines of "By clicking the Continue >> button, you agree to the Terms & Conditions" (see Prescribed
  wording below), and is updated whenever that control's label changes, so the sentence and the button
  never drift out of sync across a release (Chabolla, 129 F.4th 1147, slip op. at 19).
- A multi-screen sign-up flow carries its own notice-and-assent pairing on the one screen where the
  binding action happens, rather than assuming notice shown on an earlier screen carries forward to a
  later one (Chabolla, 129 F.4th 1147, slip op. at 21).
- No unrelated question, offer or control (a gift-card prompt, an upsell, an account-linking option) is
  placed between the assent notice and the button it governs (Chabolla, 129 F.4th 1147, slip op. at 19 to
  20).
- Where terms are reached only by hyperlink rather than displayed in full, the hyperlink itself is
  reasonably conspicuous and the sentence next to it is a clear prompt directing the user to read the
  terms; a user who proceeds without reading them is still on inquiry notice, so the flow does not have to
  force the user to open or scroll through the terms before completing the action (Meyer, 868 F.3d 66,
  slip op. at 25 to 26).
- A consumer consent flow for substituting electronic disclosures for required paper ones delivers, before
  the consumer consents, the full four-part statement in § 7001(c)(1)(B) and (C) (paper option; withdrawal
  right, its conditions, fees and procedure; scope of consent; procedure to update contact information;
  paper-copy procedure and fee; hardware and software requirements) and captures the consumer's electronic
  consent in a way that demonstrates access to that electronic form, with a process to repeat the notice
  and demonstration if hardware or software requirements later change materially (§ 7001(c)(1)).
- Records a business is legally required to retain are kept in a format that stays accurately reproducible
  and accessible, for as long as the law requires, to everyone entitled to see them, not merely archived in
  a form only the business itself can read (§ 7001(d)(1), (e)).
- An automated renewal, acceptance or confirmation step in the product is built so that its action is
  legally attributable to the account holder (for example, tied to an authenticated session or a payment
  method the account holder controls), since ESIGN's shield for electronic-agent actions depends on that
  attribution (§ 7001(h)).

## Prescribed wording

No statute or case in these references prescribes mandatory wording for an assent notice. ESIGN leaves the manner
and format of its consumer-consent statement to the discloser (§ 7001(c)(1)(B)). The case law offers
illustrative sentences that courts have found sufficient on their facts, none of them required text:
"By clicking the Continue >> button, you agree to the Terms & Conditions" (Berman, 30 F.4th 849, slip op.
at 14); "By creating an Uber account, you agree to the TERMS OF SERVICE & PRIVACY POLICY" (Meyer, 868 F.3d
66, slip op. at 19 to 20); "By tapping `Play' I agree to the Terms of Service" (quoted in Godun, 135 F.4th
699, slip op. at 18 to 19). Match whatever sentence is used to the real label of the real button, as the
rule above requires.

## Dates

- ESIGN was enacted June 30, 2000 (Pub. L. 106-229) and took effect October 1, 2000, except that the
  record-retention requirement took effect March 1, 2001 (or, where a federal or state regulatory agency
  had an unfinished rulemaking proceeding on retention as of March 1, 2001, June 1, 2001), and except for
  certain government-guaranteed or student loan transactions on their own later effective dates
  (Pub. L. 106-229, § 107). The transferable-records provisions of § 7021 took effect 90 days after
  enactment (Pub. L. 106-229, § 202).
- The ESIGN text in this file is current through Pub. L. 119-121 (2026-10-09).
- Specht was decided October 1, 2002; Nguyen, August 18, 2014; Meyer, August 17, 2017; Berman, April 5,
  2022; Chabolla, February 27, 2025; Godun, April 15, 2025.
- A concurring judge in Godun argued that Berman's rule requiring an explicit "By clicking . . ." style
  sentence (as opposed to allowing assent to be inferred more broadly from context) is wrong and should be
  revisited, pointing to the Second Circuit's different approach in Edmundson v. Klarna, Inc. (2d Cir.
  2023); as of the Godun panel's own opinion, this is persuasive commentary only and does not change the
  binding rule (Godun, 135 F.4th 699, slip op. at 24 to 29, R. Nelson, J., concurring).

## Open points to raise

- Whether a specific design (a particular font size, a particular color pairing, a particular placement
  distance from a button) is conspicuous enough is a fact-intensive question the courts decide case by
  case; there is no bright-line numeric threshold in the statutes or cases, so a close call on an actual
  design should be checked against current counsel's judgment rather than assumed safe from this file
  alone (Chabolla, 129 F.4th 1147, slip op. at 12, describing the inquiry as always "context- and
  fact-specific").
- Whether a California Court of Appeal decision the Ninth Circuit's case law leans on for the four-type
  classification and the one-off-versus-ongoing-relationship distinction (Sellers v. JustAnswer LLC) has
  since been reviewed or revised by a higher California court is not settled by any source declared for
  this file; confirm its current status before relying on the classification in a jurisdiction outside the
  Ninth and Second Circuits.
- Whether the Second Circuit's narrower, more context-driven approach to inferring assent (as a concurring
  judge in Godun describes the Second Circuit's Edmundson decision) will spread to other circuits, or
  whether the Ninth Circuit's stricter explicit-advisement rule will instead be revisited, is unresolved in
  the cases covered here; a drafter operating in both circuits should design to the stricter
  Ninth Circuit rule until one line prevails.
- These opinions apply California law (and, where addressed, treat New York law as reaching the same
  result); a flow operating under a different state's contract law should confirm whether that state's
  courts have adopted the same two-part test before relying on this file's specific holdings as directly
  controlling outside California and New York.
