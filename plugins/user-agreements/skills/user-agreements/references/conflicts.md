# Conflicts between US and EU drafting

Law as of: 2026-10-20

## C-1 Mandatory individual arbitration with a class-action waiver

US practice: a clause requiring every dispute into individual, bilateral arbitration, barring class, collective or representative proceedings, and often routing the delegation question (whether the clause itself is valid) to the arbitrator too. US courts enforce this against a state rule that specifically disfavors arbitration, including a rule striking down class-action waivers in consumer contracts as unconscionable (Concepcion, 563 U.S. 333).

EU and German rule: a jurisdiction, arbitration or ADR clause must not route consumer disputes to a forum materially distant from the consumer's residence where this deters appearance, must not exclude or hinder the consumer's access to courts or to effective judicial review of unfair terms, and an ADR-submission agreement concluded before a dispute arose does not bind the consumer if it would deprive them of the right to go to court instead (references/eu/uctd.md, Art. 10 Directive 2013/11/EU). Annex point (q) of the Unfair Contract Terms Directive lists, as a suspect clause, excluding or hindering the consumer's right to take legal action, in particular by routing disputes exclusively to an arbitration scheme outside the law. A national court must assess such a term's unfairness of its own motion, even where the consumer never raised it, and a court's duty to review disputed terms survives to the enforcement stage as a last resort (references/eu/uctd.md). This EU floor leaves no room for a US-style delegation clause either: the consumer's entitlement to have a court assess an unfair term is a matter of public policy the parties cannot modify by agreement (references/eu/uctd.md).

Decision: EU module replaces the US text. For an EU consumer module, drop mandatory pre-dispute arbitration and any class-action or collective-redress waiver entirely; keep the US module's individual arbitration and class-waiver language only for the US consumer and US business modules, where the Federal Arbitration Act's preemption of state anti-arbitration rules controls (references/us-federal/arbitration.md).

Basis: references/eu/uctd.md; references/us-federal/arbitration.md.

## C-2 Delegating validity questions to the arbitrator

US practice: a delegation clause sends the question of the arbitration agreement's own validity, including unconscionability, to the arbitrator rather than a court.

EU and German rule: whether a term is unfair, including an arbitration or forum clause, is for a court to decide, and this duty operates independently of the consumer raising the point and cannot be waived or routed around by agreement (references/eu/uctd.md). German civil procedure gives no basis for a private tribunal to clear its own validity question outside judicial review either, since the same ex officio control the Unfair Contract Terms Directive requires binds German courts directly (references/de/bgb-agb.md on the related content-control regime; references/eu/uctd.md).

Decision: drop for EU consumers. A delegation clause has no EU consumer module; keep it only in the US consumer or US business modules, and even there flag that a court, not the arbitrator, decides questions chapter 4 of the Federal Arbitration Act reserves to it (sexual assault or harassment disputes) regardless of delegation wording (references/us-federal/arbitration.md).

Basis: references/eu/uctd.md; references/us-federal/arbitration.md.

## C-3 Governing-law clause naming the trader's home state with no mandatory-protection carve-out

US practice: a single governing-law clause naming the company's home state (commonly Delaware or California) for every user worldwide, with no mention of any other jurisdiction's law.

EU and German rule: a choice-of-law clause naming the trader's home member state is unfair under Art. 3(1) UCTD unless it unambiguously tells the consumer that the mandatory consumer-protection rules of their own country of habitual residence still apply (Rome I Art. 6(2); references/eu/uctd.md; references/eu/jurisdiction-and-law.md, citing Case C-191/15, VKI v Amazon). Absent a valid choice, a consumer contract is in any event governed by the consumer's habitual-residence law wherever the trader directs commercial activity there (Rome I Art. 6(1); references/eu/jurisdiction-and-law.md). A clause creating the impression that only the named law governs, by what it says or by what it omits, fails this standard (references/eu/jurisdiction-and-law.md).

Decision: split the document. Keep the US-home-state choice-of-law clause for the US modules; for the EU consumer module, add the mandatory-protection disclosure the Court of Justice requires, or, more simply, name the consumer's own member state law as controlling for EU consumers and drop the home-state choice for that module entirely.

Basis: references/eu/uctd.md; references/eu/jurisdiction-and-law.md.

## C-4 Forum-selection clause fixing one court for all claims

US practice: a clause naming a single court (often the company's home county or state) as the exclusive forum for every dispute, enforced against a consumer who clicked through a browsewrap or clickwrap flow (references/us-federal/online-assent.md).

EU and German rule: within the Brussels Ia consumer-contract section, a consumer may sue the trader in the trader's own member state of domicile or, at the consumer's choice, in the courts of the consumer's own domicile, while the trader may sue the consumer only in the consumer's own member state of domicile (references/eu/jurisdiction-and-law.md, Brussels Ia Art. 18). A clause fixing the consumer's forum to one named court in place of those two options cannot narrow the consumer's choice, and can be displaced only by an agreement reached after the dispute arose, one giving the consumer an additional forum, or one between parties who already shared a member state domicile at contracting (references/eu/jurisdiction-and-law.md, Brussels Ia Art. 19). A clause materially distant from the consumer's residence is independently exposed as an unfair term under the Unfair Contract Terms Directive (references/eu/uctd.md).

Decision: EU module replaces the US text. For EU consumers, state the Brussels Ia Art. 18 dual forum instead of a single named court; keep the single-forum clause only for US and non-EU business modules.

Basis: references/eu/jurisdiction-and-law.md; references/eu/uctd.md.

## C-5 Broad as-is disclaimer and liability cap for goods, digital content and services

US practice: a conspicuous as-is or as-available disclaimer of all implied warranties, paired with a liability cap (often the greater of a small dollar figure or fees paid in a defined period) and a disclaimer of consequential and incidental damages, standard in a US terms-of-service or EULA.

EU and German rule: EU law gives no opening for a blanket disclaimer of this kind. For digital content and digital services, no clause may exclude, derogate from or vary, to the consumer's detriment, the effect of the national measures transposing the Digital Content Directive before the consumer has brought a failure to supply or a lack of conformity to the trader's attention (references/eu/dcd.md, Art. 22(1)). The parallel rule for goods bars the same before the consumer has brought the lack of conformity to the seller's attention (references/eu/sgd.md, Art. 21(1)). A deviation from the objective conformity standard is valid only through a specific, named disclosure plus the consumer's separate, express acceptance of that exact deviation, never through a general disclaimer or a blanket terms-acceptance checkbox (references/eu/dcd.md, Art. 8(5); references/eu/sgd.md, Art. 7(5); references/de/bgb-kauf.md, § 434 Abs. 3 Satz 1, § 476 Abs. 1 Satz 2 BGB). German standard-terms law separately bars excluding or limiting liability for death, personal injury or health harm from a negligent breach, with no carve-out for any contract type, and bars excluding liability for other damage from a grossly negligent breach (references/de/bgb-agb.md, § 309 Nr. 7 Buchst. a, b BGB). The Unfair Contract Terms Directive's Annex flags unduly excluding or limiting the consumer's legal rights for non-performance or defective performance as a suspect clause pattern in its own right (references/eu/uctd.md, Annex point (b)), and excluding liability for death or personal injury from the trader's own act or omission is listed separately (Annex point (a)).

Decision: EU module replaces the US text. For EU consumers, state the subjective and objective conformity standards in full, name only the exceptions EU and German law actually allow, and keep liability exclusions to the narrow band German law permits (slight negligence, non-essential duties); keep the broad US as-is and damages-cap language for the US modules only.

Basis: references/eu/dcd.md; references/eu/sgd.md; references/eu/uctd.md; references/de/bgb-agb.md; references/de/bgb-kauf.md.

## C-6 Unilateral right to change the terms or the service at any time

US practice: a clause reserving the right to modify the terms, the service, pricing or features at any time, often taking effect on posting or on the user's continued use, with no substantive limit on what can change.

EU and German rule: the Unfair Contract Terms Directive's Annex flags letting the trader alter contract terms unilaterally without a valid reason specified in the contract (point (j)) and letting the trader unilaterally alter a characteristic of the product or service without valid reason (point (k)); a price-amendment term specifically must state the reason for, and the method of, any price variation, with the consumer given a termination right (references/eu/uctd.md, citing Case C-472/10 Invitel). German standard-terms law requires a modification reservation to state the limits of that right so the change, weighed against the trader's own interests, stays reasonable for the other party (references/de/bgb-agb.md, § 308 Nr. 4 BGB), and the German Federal Court of Justice has struck down a deemed-consent mechanism for changing continuing-obligation terms, including price, through the customer's mere silence, with no substantive cap on the change and regardless of a separate statute permitting deemed consent for the same change (references/de/bgb-agb.md, BGH, judgment of 27 April 2021, XI ZR 26/20). For digital content and services specifically, a modification beyond what conformity requires is valid only where the contract itself states a valid reason, the change is free to the consumer, the consumer is clearly informed, and, where the change is more than minor and negative, the consumer gets advance durable-medium notice and a termination right or a genuine unmodified opt-out (references/eu/dcd.md, Art. 19; references/de/bgb-digital.md, § 327r BGB).

Decision: EU module replaces the US text. For EU and German consumers, state the contractual reason for each category of change, cap unilateral price or feature changes to what the UCTD Annex and § 308 Nr. 4, § 309 Nr. 9 BGB allow, and build in the Art. 19 DCD and § 327r BGB notice-and-termination mechanics; keep the open-ended, unlimited US change-of-terms language for the US modules.

Basis: references/eu/uctd.md; references/de/bgb-agb.md; references/eu/dcd.md; references/de/bgb-digital.md.

## C-7 Auto-renewal and cancellation mechanics

US practice: negative-option enrollment disclosed at or near the point of sale, cancellation available by phone or a dedicated flow that need not mirror the sign-up channel under federal law (ROSCA sets disclosure, consent and a "simple mechanism" to cancel but no exact-symmetry rule), with state auto-renewal laws (California, New York, and others in the state matrix) adding their own notice windows, a "click to cancel" control, and same-medium cancellation duties (references/us-federal/negative-option.md; references/us-states/ca-arl.md; references/us-states/ny-gbl-527a.md; references/us-states/state-matrix.md).

EU and German rule: EU law layers two separate, mandatory mechanics on top of disclosure. First, the Consumer Rights Directive requires a standing, persistent withdrawal function on the online interface, labelled with fixed wording, reachable at all times during the withdrawal period, with a second confirmation step and a durable-medium acknowledgment of receipt (references/eu/crd.md, Art. 11a; references/de/bgb-widerruf.md, § 356a BGB, PW-DE-4, PW-DE-5). Second, and separately, German law requires a permanently available Kündigungsschaltfläche (cancellation button) for any continuing paid contract concludable online, leading straight to a confirmation page whose content is exhaustively fixed by statute, with no retention offer, pause option or other content on that page, and no login gate pending a Bundesgerichtshof ruling on the point (references/de/bgb-fernabsatz.md, § 312k BGB, PW-DE-2, PW-DE-3). Missing or non-compliant buttons let the consumer cancel the contract at any time with no notice period at all (references/de/bgb-fernabsatz.md, § 312k Abs. 6 BGB). The Unfair Commercial Practices Directive separately treats added friction on a cancellation flow, compared with the sign-up flow, as a potential aggressive practice (references/eu/ucpd.md, Art. 9(d)).

Decision: EU module replaces the US text. The US model (phone or support-ticket cancellation, a retention offer before completing cancellation) does not satisfy German or EU law at all: an EU and German module needs its own standing withdrawal function, its own cancellation button and confirmation page built to the fixed statutory content, and no retention-offer step inserted into either flow. Keep the US negative-option disclosure-and-consent language, and any US state same-medium cancellation rule, for the US modules.

A file touching § 312j, § 312k or § 356a BGB, or Art. 11a CRD, needs the full set of five prescribed labels checked together, not only the cancellation and withdrawal labels this entry discusses: the order-submission button (zahlungspflichtig bestellen, PW-DE-1), the cancellation button (Verträge hier kündigen, PW-DE-2), the cancellation confirmation button (jetzt kündigen, PW-DE-3), the withdrawal function label (Vertrag widerrufen, PW-DE-4) and the confirmation function label (Widerruf bestätigen, PW-DE-5).

Basis: references/eu/crd.md; references/de/bgb-widerruf.md; references/de/bgb-fernabsatz.md; references/eu/ucpd.md; references/us-federal/negative-option.md; references/us-states/ca-arl.md; references/us-states/ny-gbl-527a.md.

## C-8 Retention offers and save flows inserted before cancellation

US practice: California's Automatic Renewal Law explicitly allows showing a discount, retention benefit or cancellation-effect information together with the required cancellation trigger, never instead of it (references/us-states/ca-arl.md); the FTC has only asked, without deciding, whether offering such a retention incentive before honoring a cancellation request is itself unfair, and its 2021 guidance treats a single offer as acceptable while repeated pitches risk an unreasonable-delay finding (references/us-federal/negative-option.md); New York bars presenting a retention offer as a condition the consumer must sit through, but a single offer after a cancellation request is not itself barred (references/us-states/ny-gbl-527a.md).

EU and German rule: the German cancellation confirmation page is statutorily exhaustive and carries no further fields, offers or information, including an offer to pause the contract instead of cancelling (references/de/bgb-fernabsatz.md, § 312k Abs. 2 Satz 3 BGB; BGH, judgment of 16 July 2026, I ZR 200/25). Separately, the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive treats a cancellation flow using emotional, loss-framed messaging to make the consumer reconsider, without a reasoned, substantive change in terms, as a risk of undue influence under Art. 8, alongside any added-friction exposure under Art. 9(d) (references/eu/ucpd.md).

Decision: drop for EU consumers. No retention offer, discount pitch or pause option belongs on an EU or German cancellation flow at the point the statutory confirmation page is shown; keep a single, non-repeated retention offer only for the US modules, consistent with the FTC's current guidance and the California and New York statutes.

Basis: references/de/bgb-fernabsatz.md; references/eu/ucpd.md; references/us-federal/negative-option.md; references/us-states/ca-arl.md; references/us-states/ny-gbl-527a.md.

## C-9 Perpetual, broad licence to user-generated content with no export or deletion path

US practice: a terms-of-service clause granting the platform a perpetual, irrevocable, worldwide, royalty-free, sublicensable licence to host, use, reproduce and display user content, surviving termination, with no contractual export or deletion right stated.

EU and German rule: on termination of a digital content or digital service contract, the trader must let the consumer retrieve content they provided or created, free of charge, without hindrance, within a reasonable time and in a commonly used, machine-readable format, except for four narrowly defined categories where the trader may keep using the content without exporting it (references/eu/dcd.md, Art. 16(3), (4); references/de/bgb-digital.md, § 327p BGB). A retention clause claiming the trader keeps using all consumer-provided content after termination, beyond those four categories, misstates the law (references/de/bgb-digital.md). Separately, under the GDPR the data subject holds portability and erasure rights over their own personal data that a licence clause cannot contract around (references/eu/gdpr-rights-liability.md, Art. 17, 20), and the Data Act gives the user of a connected product or service its own export rights over product and related-service data (references/eu/data-act.md, Art. 4).

Decision: EU module replaces the US text. Keep the broad perpetual licence for non-personal, non-exportable content categories the US module already carves out, but add the Art. 16(3)-(4) DCD export duty and the four-category limit for the EU and German consumer modules, and cross-reference the separate GDPR portability and erasure rights rather than treating the licence as covering personal data at all.

Basis: references/eu/dcd.md; references/de/bgb-digital.md; references/eu/gdpr-rights-liability.md; references/eu/data-act.md.

## C-10 Consent architecture for cookies and behavioural data: opt-out versus opt-in

US practice: outside a handful of states, US law runs on an opt-out model for sale, sharing and targeted advertising: California, Virginia (opt-in only for sensitive data), Texas and most of the state-matrix laws let a business process and share ordinary personal data by default, subject to an opt-out or a universal opt-out signal, with sensitive data alone needing opt-in consent in most of those states (references/us-states/ca-ccpa.md; references/us-states/va-vcdpa.md; references/us-states/tx-tdpsa.md; references/us-states/state-matrix.md).

EU and German rule: the ePrivacy rule, as both the Directive and its German transposition state it, requires prior opt-in consent before storing or reading anything on a user's device for a purpose that is not strictly necessary, with every non-essential control starting unticked and no pre-ticked box, continued browsing or silence ever constituting consent (references/eu/eprivacy-cookies.md, Planet49; references/de/tdddg.md, § 25 TDDDG). The GDPR separately requires a specific legal basis, most often consent meeting the Art. 4(11) and Art. 7 standard, for the processing that follows, and bars treating acceptance of a bundled terms-of-use document as that consent (references/eu/gdpr-legal-bases.md).

Decision: EU module replaces the US text. A single cookie banner or consent flow cannot serve both legal architectures: the EU and German module needs an opt-in, per-purpose consent flow with a genuine reject option of equal prominence and a working withdrawal mechanism, while the US module can keep an opt-out-plus-universal-signal design for the states that allow it, adding opt-in only where a specific state requires it for sensitive data (sensitive data in most states; Maryland goes further still, barring non-essential sensitive-data processing outright and barring its sale altogether, stricter than an opt-in gate, but stated only for sensitive data, not for ordinary personal data generally) (references/us-states/state-matrix.md).

Basis: references/eu/eprivacy-cookies.md; references/de/tdddg.md; references/eu/gdpr-legal-bases.md; references/us-states/ca-ccpa.md; references/us-states/va-vcdpa.md; references/us-states/state-matrix.md.

## C-11 Waiver of statutory privacy-law rights or remedies

US practice: a general arbitration and class-waiver clause drafted to cover every dispute the user might bring, with no carve-out for a specific statutory claim.

EU and German rule: this is also a live issue on the US side of the Atlantic once a specific state privacy statute is in view. California's CCPA voids any contract provision, including a representative-action waiver, that purports to waive or limit a right, remedy or enforcement mechanism under the statute (references/us-states/ca-ccpa.md, Cal. Civ. Code 1798.192); Texas's TDPSA and Virginia's VCDPA each void a clause waiving or limiting the chapter's consumer rights in the same way (references/us-states/tx-tdpsa.md, Sec. 541.054; references/us-states/va-vcdpa.md, § 59.1-578(B)). On the EU side, the Unfair Contract Terms Directive bars a clause purporting to waive, in advance, the consumer's Art. 6(1) protection, whatever form the waiver takes, and that bar is treated as a matter of public policy (references/eu/uctd.md).

Decision: keep with a carve-out on both sides, but differently scoped. A US arbitration and class-waiver clause needs an explicit exception for a claim under a state privacy statute that voids such waivers (CCPA, TDPSA, VCDPA and the matching provision in each state-matrix row that states one); an EU and German module needs the broader UCTD carve-out covered already in C-1 and C-2 above, since EU consumer law voids the underlying arbitration clause for consumers rather than only a privacy-specific slice of it.

Basis: references/us-states/ca-ccpa.md; references/us-states/tx-tdpsa.md; references/us-states/va-vcdpa.md; references/eu/uctd.md.

## C-12 Public-injunctive-relief and representative-action carve-outs inside arbitration clauses

US practice: a US arbitration clause commonly bars every form of representative or class proceeding without exception.

EU and German rule: California's own McGill rule (not itself EU law, but a second US-side complication worth flagging alongside the EU conflicts above, since both point the same direction: a blanket bar on representative relief is unsafe) holds that a predispute arbitration clause cannot waive, in any forum, a consumer's statutory right to seek public injunctive relief under California's consumer-protection statutes (references/us-federal/arbitration.md, McGill v. Citibank, N.A., 2 Cal.5th 945). On the EU side, a collective injunction against an unfair term binds that trader's future use of the same term in every consumer contract, and a rule automatically suspending an individual consumer's own action pending a parallel collective one is precluded (references/eu/uctd.md, citing Case C-472/10 Invitel and Joined Cases C-381/14 and C-385/14 Sales Sinués and Drame Ba).

Decision: keep with a carve-out. The US module's arbitration and class-waiver clause needs an express exception for public injunctive relief under California law (and any other state that recognises the same doctrine), consistent with C-1; the EU module, having dropped mandatory arbitration and class or representative-action waivers entirely under C-1, needs no separate carve-out, since the underlying bar does not exist there.

Basis: references/us-federal/arbitration.md; references/eu/uctd.md.

## C-13 Intermediary and platform liability shield language

US practice: a US terms document commonly states, in sweeping terms, that the platform bears no responsibility for user-generated content and is immune from claims over it, often citing Section 230 as if it were an unconditional shield.

EU and German rule: the Digital Services Act's conditional liability exemptions apply only while their stated conditions hold, and specifically do not reach a case where the platform's own presentation of an item or its own enablement of a transaction would lead an average consumer to believe the platform itself, or someone under its authority or control, is the one supplying the content, product or service (references/eu/dsa.md, Art. 6(3)). Separately, no clause may suggest the DSA requires, or permits inferring, blanket pre-screening or general monitoring, since Art. 8 bars imposing a general monitoring obligation, while Art. 7 lets a provider run voluntary, good-faith content investigations without losing the exemptions (references/eu/dsa.md).

Decision: keep with a carve-out. Keep the Section 230 language for the US module exactly as US law states it (conditional on the platform not itself having created or developed the content at issue), and build a parallel, separately worded EU module tracking the DSA's own conditions (Art. 4 to 6 exemptions, the Art. 6(3) marketplace-presentation exception, and the Art. 7 to 8 monitoring rules) rather than importing the US no-responsibility framing wholesale.

Basis: references/eu/dsa.md; references/us-federal/section-230.md.

## C-14 Resale and transfer restrictions on a purchased software licence

US practice: a US EULA commonly states a software licence is personal, non-transferable and non-sublicensable, with no resale right, even where the licence was sold for a one-off fee with no time limit.

EU and German rule: once a software copy is first sold in the EU or EEA with the rightholder's consent, under a licence combining a one-off fee with an unlimited-duration right to use the copy, the EU distribution right for that copy exhausts regardless of a contrary contract label, and a clause prohibiting further transfer does not block the resale that exhaustion permits (references/eu/software-directive.md, Case C-128/11, UsedSoft v Oracle). The German transposition reaches the same result directly in its own text: once a copy is put into circulation by sale with the rightholder's consent inside the EU or EEA, the distribution right exhausts for that copy, and the rental right is the only part of that bundle that never exhausts (references/de/urhg-software.md, § 69c Nr. 3 UrhG).

Decision: EU module replaces the US text. For an EU or German consumer or business licence sold as a one-off fee with unlimited duration, drop the blanket non-transferable language and instead state the exhaustion-compliant resale mechanics (the reselling party must render its own copy unusable, group licences stay indivisible below their contracted unit); keep the US personal, non-transferable licence language for the US module, or restructure the US offering itself as a genuinely time-limited subscription if a non-transferable grant is the real commercial intent everywhere.

Basis: references/eu/software-directive.md; references/de/urhg-software.md.

## C-15 Generic, basis-free processing clauses

US practice: a privacy section stating broadly that personal information is used as permitted by applicable law or for the business's general purposes, without naming a specific legal basis, which is adequate disclosure practice under most US state privacy statutes once the required category-and-purpose list is otherwise given (references/us-states/ca-ccpa.md; references/us-states/va-vcdpa.md).

EU and German rule: the GDPR requires naming the specific Art. 6(1) basis relied on for each processing purpose; a clause that only says data is processed as permitted by law without naming a point (a) to (f) ground meets neither the purpose-limitation nor the transparency arm of Art. 5(1)(a), (b) (references/eu/gdpr-legal-bases.md). The privacy-notice content list is itself more granular than the US model: it requires the specific legitimate interests pursued where that basis is used, the recipients or categories of recipients named specifically where possible, and a storage period or the criteria for setting one, not a bare statement that data is kept as long as necessary (references/eu/gdpr-privacy-notice.md).

Decision: EU module replaces the US text. Build the EU and German privacy notice around the Art. 13/14 GDPR content list and a named legal basis per purpose; keep the shorter US category-and-purpose model, which several state statutes already treat as sufficient, for the US-facing notice.

Basis: references/eu/gdpr-legal-bases.md; references/eu/gdpr-privacy-notice.md; references/us-states/ca-ccpa.md; references/us-states/va-vcdpa.md.

## C-16 Age-gating and parental consent for minors

US practice: COPPA requires verifiable parental consent only for a site or service directed to children under 13, or on actual knowledge a user is under 13, with a mixed-audience product running a neutral age screen instead (references/us-federal/coppa.md); several state privacy statutes layer a second, higher age band (13 to 16, or under 18 for some duties) requiring either consent or an opt-out for targeted advertising, sale or profiling (references/us-states/state-matrix.md; references/us-states/va-vcdpa.md; references/us-states/co-cpa.md).

EU and German rule: the GDPR's own child-consent floor for an information-society service runs from 13 to 16 depending on the member state, decided by that state's own law, and does not reach a contract-formation question, which is left to national contract law (references/eu/gdpr-legal-bases.md, Art. 8). The EU AI Act separately bars inferring emotions of a person in an education institution outright, with a narrow medical or safety exception (references/eu/ai-act.md, Art. 5(1)(f)), a prohibition with no COPPA equivalent. The Digital Services Act requires proportionate privacy, safety and security measures for minors and bars profiling-based advertising to a minor regardless of consent (references/eu/dsa.md, Art. 28).

Decision: keep with a carve-out, built around jurisdiction rather than a single global age gate. A single hard-coded age of 13 (the COPPA floor) does not clear the GDPR's own 13-to-16 range for every member state, nor the DSA's flat profiling-to-minors bar; the product needs a jurisdiction-aware age gate rather than one global threshold, with the EU module also carrying the DSA's unconditional profiling-to-minors restriction that the US module does not need in the same form.

Basis: references/us-federal/coppa.md; references/eu/gdpr-legal-bases.md; references/eu/ai-act.md; references/eu/dsa.md; references/us-states/state-matrix.md.

## C-17 Deceptive design and manipulative interface patterns

US practice: enforced under FTC Act section 5's unfairness and deception standard, read through the Commission's own dark-patterns staff guidance, itself non-binding and enforced case by case (references/us-federal/ftc-act-dark-patterns.md).

EU and German rule: the same category of conduct is reached by at least three overlapping EU instruments at once: the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive's aggressive-practices test and its Annex I per se list (references/eu/ucpd.md); the Digital Services Act's Art. 25 bar on deceptive or manipulative online-interface design for an online platform (references/eu/dsa.md); and the GDPR's own dark-pattern-void consent rule, under which agreement obtained through a dark pattern is not valid consent regardless of what the interface discloses (references/eu/gdpr-legal-bases.md, read with the EDPB guidance cited in references/eu/eprivacy-cookies.md). A design pattern that is merely an FTC enforcement risk in the US module (no private right of action, Commission-only enforcement, guidance rather than binding rule) can independently void consent, invalidate a term, or trigger direct regulatory liability under the EU module.

Decision: EU module replaces the US text where the two diverge on consequence, not on substance: both regimes converge on avoiding the same patterns (pre-ticked boxes, asymmetric friction, false urgency, bundled consent), so the underlying design guidance in Product behaviour sections across these files should be followed everywhere; the EU module's drafting, however, needs to state the EU-specific consequence (void consent, an unfair term, a DSA Art. 25 breach) rather than only the US enforcement-risk framing.

Basis: references/us-federal/ftc-act-dark-patterns.md; references/eu/ucpd.md; references/eu/dsa.md; references/eu/gdpr-legal-bases.md.

## C-18 Pricing, payment terms and delivery terms geofenced by customer location

US practice: a US terms document commonly geofences pricing, SKUs, payment methods or delivery by country, with no compliance review, and nothing in US federal or state law restricts pricing or access by a customer's location beyond export-control and tax concerns.

EU and German rule: a trader must not apply different general conditions of access, including different net sale prices, for reasons tied to a customer's nationality, place of residence or place of establishment, in three situations: delivering goods to, or offering collection in, a member state the trader's own general conditions already cover; supplying an electronically supplied service (other than one whose main feature is access to copyright-protected works); or supplying a non-electronic service at a physical location in a member state where the trader operates (references/eu/geoblocking.md, Regulation (EU) 2018/302, Art. 4(1)). A trader must also not block or limit a customer's access to its online interface, or redirect a customer to a different locally targeted version of it, for reasons tied to nationality, residence or establishment, without the customer's explicit consent to the redirection (references/eu/geoblocking.md, Art. 3(1), (2)). A reseller or franchise clause that would force a trader to geo-discriminate against customers in this way, in breach of Art. 3 or 4, is void (references/eu/geoblocking.md, Art. 6(2)). None of this requires the trader to deal with any given customer, or to deliver or bear delivery costs beyond what it already offers; it bars only the location-based differentiation itself, not a refusal to sell taken for other reasons (references/eu/geoblocking.md, recital 18, recital 23).

Decision: EU module replaces the US text. For EU-facing terms, drop nationality, residence or establishment-based blocking, redirection or price, payment or delivery differentiation in the three Art. 4(1) situations, and void any reseller or franchise clause that would require it; keep the US geofencing practice for the US modules, or add an EU carve-out stating the Art. 4(1) exceptions (a binding Union or member state legal requirement, or, for books, a member state's resale-price-maintenance law) where one of them genuinely applies.

Basis: references/eu/geoblocking.md.

## C-19 Unilateral, no-notice change or suspension of a business user's marketplace account

US practice: a marketplace or platform terms document commonly reserves the right to change its terms, or to suspend or terminate a business user's seller account, at will and without notice, with no stated reason and no federal or state counterpart requiring otherwise.

EU and German rule: a provider of online intermediation services must notify a business user, on a durable medium, of any proposed change to its terms and conditions, giving a notice period that is reasonable and proportionate to the change and runs at least 15 days from the notice, longer where the business user needs it for technical or commercial adaptation, and must give the business user a right to terminate the contract within that period (references/eu/p2b.md, Regulation (EU) 2019/1150, Art. 3(2)). Restricting or suspending the service to a business user for individual goods or services requires a statement of reasons on a durable medium no later than when the measure takes effect; terminating the whole of the service to a business user requires a statement of reasons at least 30 days before termination, subject to narrow exceptions for a legal obligation, an imperative reason under national law, or the business user's own repeated infringement (references/eu/p2b.md, Art. 4(1), (2), (4)). The business user must in every case be able to clarify the facts through the provider's internal complaint-handling process, and a revoked restriction, suspension or termination must be reversed without undue delay, including restoring any data access lost through it (references/eu/p2b.md, Art. 4(3)).

Decision: EU module replaces the US text, or split the document. For an EU-facing B2B marketplace terms document, state the Art. 3(2) 15-day minimum change-notice period with its termination right, and the Art. 4 statement-of-reasons duty before restricting, suspending or terminating a business user's access; keep the at-will, no-notice US marketplace terms language only for the US modules.

Basis: references/eu/p2b.md.

## Open points to raise

- Whether a specific US-module clause claiming a severability fallback for an unconscionable or unfair term provision in fact works under German law, since a court finding a term unfair under the Unfair Contract Terms Directive has no power to revise or reduce it and may decline to sever at all where the offending content permeates the clause rather than sitting in one isolated stipulation (references/eu/uctd.md); the equivalent US question (whether an arbitration clause's severability language rescues the rest of the clause once one provision is struck) is itself unsettled on the facts a US court has actually decided (references/us-federal/arbitration.md, Heckman).
- Whether any product sold into both the EU and US markets in fact structures its software licensing as a one-off fee with unlimited duration (triggering the UsedSoft exhaustion conflict in C-14) or as a genuine subscription (which raises no such conflict); this is a product-specific commercial-structure question the reference files do not resolve in the abstract.
- Whether a specific jurisdiction's court has tested the McGill public-injunctive-relief carve-out (C-12) against a non-California state's own consumer-protection statute; the reference files confirm the doctrine for California only.
- Whether the business in fact operates distinct EU and US modules of its terms and privacy documents, or a single global document; every conflict above assumes a split-module approach is available, and a single-document drafting strategy would need its own review against each conflict instead of the split recommended here.
