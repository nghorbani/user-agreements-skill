# TDDDG: German cookie and tracking consent

Citation: Telekommunikation-Digitale-Dienste-Datenschutz-Gesetz (TDDDG), §§ 25 to 26
Version: zuletzt geaendert durch Art. 3 G v. 10.3.2026 I Nr. 64
Official text: https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/ttdsg/
Law as of: 2026-10-20

The TDDDG is the renamed successor to the Telekommunikation-Telemedien-Datenschutz-Gesetz (TTDSG); the act still sits under the URL slug "ttdsg" on gesetze-im-internet.de even though its current title is TDDDG. §§ 25 and 26 are Germany's transposition of Art. 5(3) of the ePrivacy Directive; the directive-level rule, the CJEU's reading of it, and the European Data Protection Board's guidance are covered in `references/eu/eprivacy-cookies.md`. This file covers what is specifically German: the § 25 consent standard and its two exemptions as German supervisory guidance reads them, the § 26 recognized-consent-management-service regime, and the practical tests German authorities apply to a banner, a cookie, or a consent-management platform.

## When it applies

- § 25 Abs. 1 Satz 1 reaches storing information in the end user's terminal equipment (Endeinrichtung), or accessing information already stored there, whenever that device connects, directly or indirectly, to a public telecommunications network. Terminal equipment is defined in § 2 Abs. 2 Nr. 6 TTDSG, which the German supervisory authorities' guidance quotes as "jede direkt oder indirekt an die Schnittstelle eines öffentlichen Telekommunikationsnetzes angeschlossene Einrichtung zum Aussenden, Verarbeiten oder Empfangen von Nachrichten; sowohl bei direkten als auch bei indirekten Anschlüssen kann die Verbindung über Draht, optische Faser oder elektromagnetisch hergestellt werden; bei einem indirekten Anschluss ist zwischen der Endeinrichtung und der Schnittstelle des öffentlichen Netzes ein Gerät geschaltet" (§ 2 Abs. 2 Nr. 6 TTDSG). That reach covers laptops, tablets, phones, and connected devices such as smart-home appliances, connected vehicles, and smart TVs, and stops only at a system with no connection to the public network, such as an isolated company network.
- The rule is technology-neutral and applies regardless of whether the stored or accessed information is personal data; it protects the device and the user's privacy in it, independent of any personal-data link (§ 25 Abs. 1). Besides cookies, it reaches web storage objects, device hardware identifiers, advertising IDs, phone numbers, SIM serial numbers, contacts and call lists, Bluetooth beacons, SMS communication, MAC addresses, automatic update mechanisms, and browser fingerprinting.
- Information that is merely transmitted automatically by the browser's own settings on calling up a service, such as the public IP address, the called-up URL, the user-agent string, or the configured language, is not "access to information already stored" and so falls outside § 25 Abs. 1. Actively reading out device properties, for example by script, to build a fingerprint is access within the meaning of the provision.
- § 25 governs only the storage/access step itself. Where personal data results from that step (the common case, for example a tracking cookie), § 25's specific rule takes precedence over the GDPR for the storage/access step under the Art. 95 GDPR collision rule, while the GDPR governs any processing that follows from reading the data, on its own legal basis (see `references/eu/gdpr-legal-bases.md`).
- Territorial reach follows the market-location principle: every provider established in Germany, or providing or participating in providing services or placing goods on the German market, is bound, regardless of where it is established.
- § 26 applies to a service that manages consent given under § 25 Abs. 1 and seeks recognition as independent and trustworthy; recognition depends on a statutory instrument (Rechtsverordnung) under § 26 Abs. 2. This file does not cover whether that instrument has been issued or what an operative recognition procedure looks like; treat the current status of that instrument as an open point for counsel (see Open points below) (§ 26 Abs. 1, 2).

## What the documents must say

- Obtain the end user's consent before any non-exempt storage or access, on the basis of clear and complete information given first; the consent and the information must meet the GDPR standard, not a separate, lighter one (§ 25 Abs. 1 Satz 1, 2). That means freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous consent, given before the access happens, and as easy to withdraw as to give.
- Where one banner button is meant to grant both the § 25 Abs. 1 storage consent and a later GDPR-governed processing consent, disclose both: name the follow-on processing purposes at the same point the storage consent is requested, and make it clearly recognisable that one act grants more than one consent. A banner that only names deploying cookies, without naming the follow-on processing, grants only the § 25 Abs. 1 consent.
- Where consent covers several purposes, give each one its own disclosure at the layer where the consent button sits; naming every purpose only on a deeper layer while the first-layer button already grants bundled consent does not satisfy the specificity standard.
- Show, at the first layer where consent is requested, the concrete processing purposes, whether individual profiles are built and enriched with data from other sites, whether data is processed outside the EEA, and how many controllers the data is disclosed to. Where third-party services are used, name each one individually rather than referring generally to "partners."
- Offer a reject option with the same visual and functional weight as the accept option wherever a reject option is required at all: where the banner blocks access to some or all of the site or app content, pending a decision, a first-layer reject option is required; where it does not block any content and no action is needed to keep using the service, a first-layer reject option can be dispensable, and where consent can only be given at a deeper layer in the first place, the same holds, subject to an individual-case check.
- Give a reject button, where one is offered, an unambiguous label so users know they are not granting consent; a short, clear phrase is enough.
- Implement a withdrawal mechanism reachable through the same channel used to give consent, exactly as easy to use as giving it.
- Where a service's "strictly necessary" exemption (§ 25 Abs. 2 Nr. 2) is claimed, document, for each cookie or similar item, which function of the service it supports, when storage or access may start (only once the user actually invokes that function), what the stored or read content is, how long it persists, and who can read it; claim the exemption only where every one of the item's purposes independently meets this test.
- For a service used under a prior contract or supplementary terms of use, such as a paid subscription, point to that document to establish the concrete scope of what the user expressly requested, rather than relying on the single act of calling up the service.
- Disclose the full Art. 13/14 GDPR information-duty content for any processing that follows a § 25 Abs. 1 access; the information duty under § 19 Abs. 3 TDDDG is in addition to, not a substitute for, that GDPR content.
- Where a service does not require an account and an access request under Art. 15 DS-GVO cannot be matched to stored data by name alone, ask the requester for another unique identifying feature to make that match, on the basis of Art. 11 Abs. 2 Satz 2 DS-GVO, rather than refusing the request; ask for further identity-verifying information only on concrete, case-specific grounds for doubting the requester's identity, never as a routine check (Art. 15 i.V.m. Art. 12 DS-GVO; see `references/eu/gdpr-rights-liability.md` for the general access-request mechanic).
- A consent-management service seeking § 26 recognition should disclose, in its own documentation: that it has no economic self-interest in whether consent is given and is structurally independent from parties that have such an interest; that its procedures and technical applications for obtaining and managing consent are user-friendly and competition-compatible; that it processes personal data and consent-decision information for no purpose beyond consent management; and that it has submitted a security concept supporting an assessment of its quality, reliability, and GDPR-level technical and organizational measures (§ 26 Abs. 1 Nr. 1 to 4).

## What the documents must not say

- Do not label a function as exempt from consent merely by a category name such as "reach measurement," "website optimisation," "fraud prevention," or "personalised services." Such labels are too indeterminate on their own; the exemption can be assessed only for one precisely defined configuration and purpose, and is lost as soon as further data points or evaluation outputs are added.
- Do not rely on a single navigational act, such as typing a URL or clicking a link, to treat every subpage or function behind it as expressly requested; a base service (Basisdienst, what the user is treated as wanting on consciously calling up the service) is expressly requested on that act, but an additional function or additional service (Zusatzfunktion, Zusatzdienst) integrated into it, such as a shopping cart, a chat box, or a wish list, is expressly requested only once the user actually and knowingly invokes it.
- Do not treat general measurement or analysis functions, such as visitor counting or A/B testing, as automatically part of the base service; users typically cannot consciously perceive or select them, so whether they are expressly requested depends on their concrete, user-oriented purpose, not on their being bundled with an obviously wanted base service.
- Do not claim the strict-necessity exemption for a cart or payment cookie before the corresponding user interaction has actually occurred (an item placed in the cart, a payment process started); mere browsing does not trigger it.
- Do not claim the strict-necessity exemption for a cookie carrying a unique identification marker (Cookie-UID) without checking whether the function actually needs individualisation; storing a consent decision, implementing load balancing, or storing a language or background-colour preference needs only a non-identifying value, not a unique identifier.
- Do not label a cookie a "security cookie" to claim the exemption without identifying the specific, user-oriented security function it protects; the exemption does not extend to cookies securing a third-party site or service the user did not expressly request.
- Do not claim the exemption for a multi-purpose cookie unless every one of its purposes independently meets the test; one non-qualifying purpose defeats the exemption for the whole cookie.
- Do not rely on pre-ticked boxes, silence, inactivity, or continued browsing or scrolling as a substitute for an affirmative consent act; none of these can constitute consent under § 25 Abs. 1, whatever the banner discloses.
- Do not pair a single prominent "accept all" option with a vague, multi-step "settings" or "details" option as the only alternative; where the two options do not carry an equivalent communicative effect, consent is defeated for lack of an equally easy, equally visible choice.
- Do not demote a reject button to a combined label such as "settings or reject" that leads only to a further layer; its label must be unambiguous about declining consent at the point it is offered.
- Do not place a reject option outside the visual frame of the banner, embed it in running text without visual or linguistic highlighting while the accept option is a prominent button, or make it visible only after scrolling past the consent text while accept is visible from the start.
- Do not require an email, fax, or letter detour, a contact form, or a search through the privacy notice to withdraw consent; a withdrawal link inside the privacy notice is permissible only where it leads directly to the withdrawal mechanism without a search step.
- Do not promise active, device-side deletion of an identifying cookie as satisfying the erasure right; deleting a cookie from a user's own device lies outside the operator's technical reach, and the GDPR storage-limitation duty is instead satisfied by giving the cookie a bounded, auto-expiring lifetime.
- Do not describe a consent-management platform as independent, for § 26 purposes, where it is operated by, or commercially tied to, a business whose revenue depends on consent being given; the independence criterion is a structural, not a marketing, standard.
- Do not rely on consent under Art. 49 Abs. 1 lit. a) DS-GVO as the transfer mechanism for personal data that regular or systematic tracking of user behaviour on a website or app sends to a third country; the scale and regularity of such transfers conflicts with the character of Art. 49 DS-GVO as an exception provision and with Art. 44 Satz 2 DS-GVO, so an adequacy decision or standard contractual clauses, backed by a case-by-case assessment of the third country's law and practice and, where needed, supplementary measures (see `references/eu/gdpr-processors-transfers.md`), is required instead. Where no adequate supplementary measures are available for a given third-party service, do not embed or use that service at all, rather than relying on a blanket consent-based transfer clause (CJEU, judgment of 16 July 2020, C-311/18, "Schrems II").

## Product behaviour it assumes

- A cookie and tracking-technology inventory that tags each item by its actual technical function, not by a category label, and records, for each item claimed exempt, the function it supports, its timing, content, duration, and the domain that can read it.
- A base service/additional function classification for the site or app's own components, so a consent flow does not treat a single act of calling up the service as consent to every function bundled behind it.
- A consent banner whose first layer names every processing purpose, every named third party, cross-site profiling, and non-EEA transfers, with no further scripts or third-party content loading while the banner is displayed and consent undecided, and with the legal notice and privacy notice reachable regardless of the banner's state.
- Per-purpose consent controls, each starting unselected, capable of individual refusal, with a reject option of equivalent visual and functional weight wherever one is required.
- A withdrawal mechanism reachable through the same interface used to give consent, with no extra step.
- Consent records sufficient to show which process was used to obtain consent and what was shown at the time, retained together with the banner or consent-management-platform configuration then in effect, rather than only the current configuration.
- A bounded, auto-expiring lifetime on every cookie or similar stored identifier, satisfying the storage-limitation duty regardless of whether the item carries a personal identifier.
- Where a consent-management platform seeks § 26 recognition, a build that keeps its consent-management processing separate from any other commercial processing, and documentation of its security concept, ready for independent assessment.

## Prescribed wording

None. §§ 25 and 26 fix a consent standard, two exemptions, and recognition criteria; neither section prescribes specific banner, button, or toggle text.

## Open points to raise

- Whether a § 26 Abs. 2 statutory instrument has since been issued, since that instrument fixes the detailed recognition procedure, the content of a recognition application and a security concept, the competent recognizing body, and the technical measures for software to honor consent settings and integrate recognized consent-management services; until confirmed, § 26 Abs. 1's four criteria describe what recognition will require, without an operative recognition procedure in place.
- The Bundesgerichtshof's Planet49 judgment of 28 May 2020 (I ZR 7/16) is cited here for the scope distinction between the device-protection rule and the GDPR and for the voluntariness standard; this file does not cover the judgment's own text, so read it directly rather than relying on this summary before citing it as authority. Separately, this file does not cover whether the EU ePrivacy Regulation, meant to replace the Directive this act transposes, has since been enacted; treat that as an open point for counsel, and see `references/eu/eprivacy-cookies.md` for the Directive-level rule this act transposes.
- Whether any product relying on the strict-necessity exemption for a session-scoped cookie can show, after the fact, exactly when a given usage event ended (the guidance treats a usage event as ending once the user actively closes the site or browser), since that is the operative trigger for the timing dimension of strict necessity.
- Whether a third-party consent-management platform integrated into the product's banner also provides marketing or analytics services; the guidance flags that a UID-carrying consent-status cookie set by such a platform raises the risk of reuse for the third party's own commercial purpose, which a document must not assume away without verifying the actual purpose of that specific cookie.
