# Software Directive (EU) 2009/24/EC

Citation: Directive 2009/24/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council of 23 April 2009 on
the legal protection of computer programs (codified version)
Version: original text, no amendment found
Official text: https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32009L0024
Law as of: 2026-10-20

This file covers copyright protection of software under EU law: what counts as a protected
computer program, the rightholder's exclusive rights, the user exceptions a licence cannot remove,
decompilation for interoperability, and how a one-off sale of a copy obtained over the internet
exhausts the rightholder's EU distribution right under the Court of Justice's reading of Art. 4(2)
and 5(1) in Case C-128/11, UsedSoft GmbH v Oracle International Corp., judgment of 3 July 2012. This
file does not cover the separate conformity and liability rules for digital content and digital services,
which sit in `references/eu/dcd.md`, or German copyright transposition, which is an open point
until a dedicated file exists in this skill.

## When it applies

- A computer program is protected by copyright as a literary work within the meaning of the Berne
  Convention, and "computer programs" includes the preparatory design material produced before the
  code itself (Art. 1(1)).
- Protection attaches to the expression of a program in any form; the ideas and principles
  underlying any element of a program, including those underlying its interfaces, are not
  protected (Art. 1(2)).
- The only eligibility test is originality in the sense that the program is the author's own
  intellectual creation; no other criterion may be applied (Art. 1(3)).
- The Directive also applies to a program created before 1 January 1993, without disturbing an act
  already concluded or a right already acquired before that date (Art. 1(4)).
- Where a software licence is sold for a one-off fee and grants the customer a permanent
  (unlimited-duration) right to use a copy, the transaction is classified as a sale that exhausts
  the rightholder's EU distribution right, whatever the contract calls itself; this classification
  turns on the combination of payment and unlimited duration, not on the document's chosen label,
  and applies equally whether the copy is delivered over the internet or on a physical medium such
  as a CD-ROM or DVD (Case C-128/11, paras. 45 to 49, 61).

## What the documents must say

- The rightholder's exclusive rights include the right to do, or to authorise, three restricted
  acts: the permanent or temporary reproduction of the program by any means and in any form, in
  part or whole, including the incidental reproduction that loading, displaying, running,
  transmitting or storing the program necessitates; the translation, adaptation, arrangement or any
  other alteration of the program and reproducing the result, without prejudice to the rights of
  the person who carries out the alteration; and any form of distribution to the public, including
  rental, of the original program or of copies of it (Art. 4(1), points (a) to (c)).
- A first sale of a copy in the Union by the rightholder, or with the rightholder's consent,
  exhausts the distribution right for that copy within the Union, except that the rightholder keeps
  the right to control further rental of the program or a copy of it (Art. 4(2)).
- Once the Art. 4(2) conditions for a first sale are met, the rightholder can no longer oppose
  resale of that copy, and a licence term that purports to prohibit further transfer does not block
  it: "notwithstanding the existence of contractual terms prohibiting a further transfer, the
  rightholder in question can no longer oppose the resale of that copy" (Case C-128/11, para. 77,
  quoted verbatim). A second, third, or any subsequent acquirer of a resold licence is a "lawful
  acquirer" under Art. 5(1) and may transfer and run the copy onto their own computer, even without a
  licence agreement running directly with the rightholder (Case C-128/11, paras. 80 to 85).
- An original acquirer who resells a copy for which the distribution right is exhausted must make
  its own retained copy unusable at the time of resale, to avoid infringing the rightholder's
  separate reproduction right under Art. 4(1)(a); a rightholder may use technical measures, such as
  product keys, to verify or enforce this (Case C-128/11, paras. 70, 78 to 79, 87).
- A licensee holding a group licence for more users than it needs cannot divide that licence below
  its own contracted minimum unit and resell only a sub-number of users it chooses; exhaustion
  covers resale of the whole licence, or of surplus whole units, not an arbitrary split (Case
  C-128/11, paras. 69 to 71, 86).
- A maintenance agreement (updates, patches) bundled with the original sale does not itself block
  exhaustion, since maintenance is a severable service contract; but once an update or patch merges
  permanently into the copy, exhaustion extends to the corrected and updated copy, not merely to
  the copy as first delivered, even after the maintenance term later lapses (Case C-128/11, paras.
  66 to 68).
- Exhaustion under Art. 4(2) and Case C-128/11 applies to a first sale in the European Union, by the
  rightholder or with the rightholder's consent (Case C-128/11, paras. 35 to 36, 77, operative part,
  points 1 to 2).
- Decompilation for interoperability is allowed without the rightholder's authorisation when
  reproducing the code and translating its form is indispensable to get the information needed to
  make an independently created program interoperate with others, and three conditions all hold:
  the licensee, another person with a right to use a copy, or someone authorised on their behalf
  does the acts; the interoperability information was not already readily available to that person;
  and the acts stay confined to the parts of the original program necessary for interoperability
  (Art. 6(1), points (a) to (c)).
- Information obtained through decompilation may be used only to achieve that interoperability, may
  be passed to others only when necessary for that interoperability, and may not be used to develop,
  produce or market a program substantially similar in its expression, or for any other
  copyright-infringing act (Art. 6(2), points (a) to (c)). Art. 6 itself may not be read to allow
  its application in a way that unreasonably prejudices the rightholder's legitimate interests or
  conflicts with normal exploitation of the program (Art. 6(3)).
- The Directive leaves other legal regimes untouched: patent rights, trade marks, unfair
  competition, trade secrets, protection of semi-conductor products and the general law of contract
  all continue to apply alongside it (Art. 8, first paragraph).

## What the documents must not say

- A licence may make the Art. 5(1) error-correction and use exception subject to a specific
  contractual provision, since that exception applies only "In the absence of specific contractual
  provisions" (Art. 5(1)); this is the one user exception in the Directive that drafting can narrow
  or displace, so a clause conditioning or removing it is not itself void.
- A licence must not prevent a person with a right to use the program from making a back-up copy,
  so far as that copy is necessary for the use the person is entitled to make; a contract clause to
  the contrary is void to that extent, by force of the Directive itself (Art. 5(2); Art. 8, second
  paragraph).
- A licence must not prevent a person with a right to use a copy from observing, studying or
  testing the program's functioning to determine the ideas and principles underlying any element of
  it, as long as this happens while performing an act (loading, displaying, running, transmitting or
  storing the program) that person is entitled to do; a no-reverse-engineering or no-study clause
  that reaches this conduct is void to that extent (Art. 5(3); Art. 8, second paragraph).
- A licence must not purport to remove the Art. 6 decompilation right by contract; any contractual
  provision contrary to Art. 6 is null and void (Art. 8, second paragraph).
- A licence must not rely on a "non-transferable" label, alone, to prevent resale once the Art.
  4(2) conditions for a first sale are met: the label does not change the sale classification, and
  a clause prohibiting further transfer does not block the resale that exhaustion permits (Case
  C-128/11, paras. 49, 77, 82 to 83). A rightholder wanting to avoid Art. 4(2) exhaustion needs to
  structure the arrangement itself differently, for example through a genuinely time-limited
  subscription or periodic payment tied to continued use, rather than relying on a label or an
  anti-resale clause layered on top of a one-off fee and unlimited-duration grant.
- The Court notes that loading and running a copy of a program that has been lawfully acquired is
  something recital 13 to the Directive says may not be prohibited by contract (Case C-128/11, para.
  76, citing recital 13 to Directive 2009/24).

## Product behaviour it assumes

- A reseller-facing flow (account transfer, licence reassignment, a marketplace for used licences)
  provides, or technically supports, a way for the reselling party to render its own retained copy
  unusable at the time of resale, and the rightholder may verify or enforce this through technical
  measures such as product keys (Case C-128/11, paras. 70, 78 to 79, 87).
- Group or volume licensing is packaged in indivisible minimum units (for example, a fixed number of
  seats per licence block), since a downstream licensee cannot split a group licence below its
  contracted unit and resell only part of it; pricing and packaging around indivisible units remains
  available to a rightholder as a lawful way to limit the scope of resale (Case C-128/11, paras. 69
  to 71, 86).
- Where updates or patches are delivered under a separate maintenance agreement, the product and the
  contract keep the maintained, updated copy and the original sale legally and practically
  separable if the rightholder wants exhaustion confined to the copy as first sold; once updates are
  installed into the same copy, the product cannot structurally prevent exhaustion from extending to
  the updated state (Case C-128/11, paras. 66 to 68).
- Where a rightholder wants a record of where exhaustion attaches, order and delivery records capture
  the location of the first sale, since that location is what the Union exhaustion rule turns on
  (Case C-128/11, paras. 35 to 36, 77).
- A back-up, study or decompilation request from a lawful user is routed to a path that does not
  require the rightholder's separate authorisation, since a licence clause that purports to block
  these specific acts has no effect to that extent (Art. 5(2), 5(3), 6; Art. 8, second paragraph).

## Prescribed wording

This file has no prescribed wording of its own: the Directive fixes no model form, label or set
phrase for a software licence, and the judgment in Case C-128/11 lays down an interpretive rule, not
wording a document must reproduce. Where the operative holding on sale, exhaustion or lawful
acquirer status is relied on in a document or an internal note, cite the judgment and paragraph
rather than paraphrase it as settled text.

## Open points to raise

- Whether a first sale made outside the European Union leaves the Union distribution right
  unexhausted, so that a rightholder selling worldwide could still segment Union from non-Union
  markets by the location of the first sale. Case C-128/11 states the affirmative rule (a first sale
  in the Union exhausts the Union distribution right) at paras. 35 to 36, 77 and in both operative
  rulings, but neither the Directive nor Case C-128/11 states or rules on the converse case of a
  first sale outside the Union, so this file does not settle whether segmentation by place of
  first sale is available.
- Whether the licensing structure in use for a given product combines a one-off fee with an
  unlimited-duration right to use the copy, since that combination is what triggers the Art. 4(2)
  sale classification regardless of the contract's own label (Case C-128/11, paras. 45 to 49); a
  genuinely time-limited or recurring-payment structure raises a different question this file does
  not resolve on its own.
- Whether a product's group or volume licence states its minimum resellable unit clearly enough
  that a licensee cannot plausibly read it as divisible below that unit (Case C-128/11, paras. 69 to
  71, 86).
- Whether maintenance, updates and patches are kept under a contract and a technical delivery path
  separate enough from the original sale to support a narrower reading of exhaustion, if that is
  the rightholder's intent (Case C-128/11, paras. 66 to 68).
- German transposition of this Directive, in Paragraphs 69c and 69d of the Law on copyright and
  related rights (Urheberrechtsgesetz), is covered separately in `references/de/urhg-software.md`;
  this file covers only the Directive and the Court of Justice's reading of it.
