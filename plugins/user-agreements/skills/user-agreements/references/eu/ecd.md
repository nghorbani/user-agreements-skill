# e-Commerce Directive (2000/31/EC)

Citation: Directive 2000/31/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council of 8 June 2000 on certain legal aspects of information society services, in particular electronic commerce, in the Internal Market (Directive on electronic commerce)
Version: consolidated text of 17 February 2024 (CELEX 02000L0031-20240217), with Art. 12 to 15 deleted by Regulation (EU) 2022/2065
Official text: https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:02000L0031-20240217
Law as of: 2026-10-20

This file covers only Art. 5, 6, 10 and 11 of the consolidated text: provider identification, commercial
communications, pre-order information and placing of the order. It does not cover Art. 1 to 4 (scope and
definitions), Art. 7 to 9, or Art. 16 onward. Articles 12 to 15, which set the mere-conduit, caching and
hosting rules for intermediaries, were deleted from the consolidated text by Regulation (EU) 2022/2065;
for a provider's own liability as an intermediary, consult that Regulation, not this file. None of Art.
5, 6, 10 or 11 carries its own sanction; enforcement runs through each Member State's national law that
transposes the Directive, so the provisions below set what a drafter must build, not the penalty for
missing it.

## When it applies

- Art. 5 and 6 apply to any information society service and to any commercial communication that is part
  of or constitutes one, without a consumer/business distinction (Art. 5(1), Art. 6).
- Art. 10 and 11 apply to a contract concluded through technological means, except where the parties agree
  otherwise and neither of them is a consumer; each paragraph states this carve-out on its own terms, so
  check it paragraph by paragraph rather than assuming it covers the whole Article (Art. 10(1), (2), Art.
  11(1), (2)). A label declaring the recipient a business does not switch off these duties by itself: the
  carve-out needs actual agreement between two parties who are not consumers.
- Art. 10(1), 10(2), the first indent of Art. 11(1), and Art. 11(2) do not apply to a contract concluded
  exclusively by exchange of electronic mail or by equivalent individual communications (Art. 10(4), Art.
  11(3)). This carve-out does not reach Art. 10(3) (storable, reproducible contract terms) or the second
  indent of Art. 11(1) (the deemed-receipt rule): those two duties apply even to an exclusively email-based
  contract.

## What the documents must say

- Render easily, directly and permanently accessible to recipients of the service and to competent
  authorities: the provider's name; its geographic establishment address; contact details, including an
  email address, that allow rapid and direct contact; the trade or similar public register the provider is
  entered in and its registration number, or an equivalent means of identification in that register, where
  the provider is so registered; the supervisory authority's particulars, where the activity needs an
  authorisation scheme; for a regulated profession, the professional body or similar institution the
  provider is registered with, the professional title and the Member State where it was granted, and a
  reference to the applicable professional rules and how to access them; and the VAT identification number,
  where the activity is subject to VAT (Art. 5(1)(a) to (g)). Build this as a standing page linked from
  everywhere, not a one-time sign-up step: "permanently accessible" rules out showing it only during
  onboarding.
- Wherever an information society service states a price, indicate clearly and unambiguously whether it
  includes tax and delivery costs (Art. 5(2)). The duty attaches to the price statement itself; a figure
  shown without that line is not cured by a full breakdown elsewhere on the site.
- Make a commercial communication clearly identifiable as such, and make the natural or legal person it is
  made on behalf of clearly identifiable, with no further condition attached to either point (Art. 6(a),
  (b)).
- Where the provider's home Member State permits promotional offers, such as discounts, premiums and
  gifts, or promotional competitions and games, make them clearly identifiable as such, and make the
  conditions to qualify or participate easily accessible, clear and unambiguous (Art. 6(c), (d)). This duty
  is conditional on the provider's establishment state permitting the mechanic in the first place; a terms
  document built for one jurisdiction should not assume the same promotional mechanic is lawful when
  reused in another.
- Before the order is placed, give the recipient, clearly, comprehensibly and unambiguously: the different
  technical steps to follow to conclude the contract; whether the concluded contract will be filed by the
  provider and whether it will be accessible to the recipient; the technical means for identifying and
  correcting input errors prior to placing the order; and the languages offered for concluding the
  contract (Art. 10(1)(a) to (d)).
- Indicate any relevant codes of conduct the provider subscribes to, and how they can be consulted
  electronically (Art. 10(2)).
- Make contract terms and general conditions given to the recipient available in a way that lets the
  recipient store and reproduce them, rather than display them once (Art. 10(3)).
- Where the recipient places an order through technological means: acknowledge receipt of the order
  without undue delay and by electronic means; and treat the order and the acknowledgement of receipt as
  received once the party they are addressed to can access them, not once sent or once read (Art. 11(1),
  first and second indent).
- Make available to the recipient appropriate, effective and accessible technical means allowing the
  recipient to identify and correct input errors, prior to placing the order (Art. 11(2)). This is the
  operative counterpart to the Art. 10(1)(c) disclosure: disclosing a correction step without actually
  providing a working one satisfies neither duty.

## What the documents must not say

- Neither Art. 10 nor Art. 11 lets a drafter disapply the Art. 10(1), 10(2) or Art. 11(1), (2) duties by a
  one-sided clause labelling the recipient a business; the carve-out in each of those paragraphs runs only
  through actual agreement between two parties who are not consumers (Art. 10(1), (2), Art. 11(1), (2)).
- No clause may claim the exclusively-email carve-out for the Art. 10(3) storable-terms duty or for the
  Art. 11(1) second-indent deemed-receipt rule: Art. 10(4) and Art. 11(3) name only Art. 10(1), 10(2), the
  first indent of Art. 11(1), and Art. 11(2) as disapplied for a contract concluded exclusively by email or
  equivalent individual communications.

## Product behaviour it assumes

- A standing, permanently linked legal-notice page carries the full Art. 5(1) provider identification
  list, kept current independent of the sign-up or onboarding flow.
- Every price display attaches its own tax-and-delivery line rather than relying on a separate page to
  state it once.
- The checkout or order flow surfaces the Art. 10(1) four-point disclosure, and any codes of conduct under
  Art. 10(2), before the order button, and gives the recipient a working, not merely disclosed, way to
  spot and fix input errors before placing the order (Art. 10(1)(c), Art. 11(2)).
- Contract terms delivered to the recipient are stored and reproducible (a downloadable or printable
  version, an emailed copy, a durable in-app record) in every channel, including one that runs exclusively
  by email (Art. 10(3), Art. 10(4)).
- Order placement through technological means triggers an electronic acknowledgement without undue delay,
  and the flow can identify the moment the order and the acknowledgement become accessible to the
  addressed party, since that moment, not the send or read time, is when each counts as received (Art.
  11(1)).

## Prescribed wording

None. Art. 5, 6, 10 and 11 fix what must be disclosed and when, not a specific form of words a document
must reproduce.

## Open points to raise

- Which Member State's national transposition governs a given deployment: Art. 5, 6, 10 and 11 set no
  sanction of their own, so the penalty for a missing disclosure depends on that state's implementing law.
- Whether any order in scope is concluded exclusively by email or by an equivalent individual
  communication, since that narrows which of Art. 10(1), 10(2) and Art. 11(1) first indent still apply.
- Whether any party on the other side of a contract is, by actual agreement, not a consumer, since only
  that agreement, not a one-sided label, can disapply the Art. 10(1), 10(2) and Art. 11(1), (2) duties.
