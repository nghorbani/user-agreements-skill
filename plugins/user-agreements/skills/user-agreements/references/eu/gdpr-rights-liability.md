# GDPR data subject rights, remedies and liability

Citation: Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 27 April 2016 on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data, and repealing Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation)
Version: original text, no substantive consolidation exists
Official text: https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32016R0679
Law as of: 2026-10-20

This file covers the data subject's rights (Art. 12 to 23) and the remedies, liability and fines that back them (Art. 77 to 84): what a privacy notice, terms document or data processing agreement must say about access, correction, erasure, objection and automated decisions, and what it may and must not say about complaints, court claims, compensation and fines. For the rest of the Regulation (scope, lawful bases, consent, controller and processor duties, transfers, supervisory authorities), see the sibling GDPR files this skill carries for those topics. Two Court of Justice judgments sit alongside the Regulation text here because they fix how two of its central provisions, Art. 22 on automated decisions and Art. 82 on compensation, actually work in practice: Case C-634/21, OQ v Land Hessen (SCHUFA Holding AG intervening), and Case C-300/21, UI v Österreichische Post AG.

## When it applies

- Every controller that handles a data subject rights request, or that makes an automated decision about a person, is bound by this file regardless of the lawful basis it relies on for the underlying processing (Art. 12(1)).
- The rights chapter binds a controller towards any data subject whose data it processes; Union or Member State law may narrow Art. 12 to 22 and 34, and the matching part of Art. 5, only for the closed list of public-interest grounds in Art. 23(1)(a) to (j) (national security, defence, public security, crime prevention and others), and only where the restriction respects the essence of the right and is necessary and proportionate (Art. 23(1), (2)).
- Articles 77 to 82 apply whenever a data subject believes their rights under the Regulation have been infringed; they are three separate remedy tracks, not three steps of one procedure, and a data subject can pursue more than one at once (Art. 77, 78, 79).
- Automated individual decision-making under Art. 22(1) reaches beyond the party that makes the final contract decision about a person: it also catches a party further up the chain whose automated output another party draws strongly on to decide about that person. A credit information agency that automatically establishes a probability value (a credit score) about a person and transmits it to a third party, who draws strongly on it to decide whether to contract with that person, is itself carrying out automated individual decision-making under Art. 22(1), whether or not it ever makes or sees the final decision (Case C-634/21, para 73 and the operative part). This reading applies to any scoring, ranking or similar automated-output business whose output another party relies on to decide about a person, credit scoring included.

## What the documents must say

### Transparency and the rights procedure (Art. 12)

- Give any information or communication required under Art. 13 to 22 and 34 in a concise, transparent, intelligible and easily accessible form, in clear and plain language, adapted for a child where the information addresses a child (Art. 12(1)).
- Act on a rights request without undue delay and within one month of receipt, extendable by two further months for complex or numerous requests; tell the data subject of any extension and its reason within the first month (Art. 12(3)).
- If a request will not be acted on, say so within one month, with the reasons, and tell the data subject of the right to complain to a supervisory authority and to seek a judicial remedy (Art. 12(4)).
- Information, communication and action under Art. 13, 14 and 15 to 22, 34 are free of charge; a reasonable administrative fee may be charged, or the request may be refused, only where it is manifestly unfounded or excessive, with the burden on the controller to show that character (Art. 12(5)).
- A reasonable doubt about the requester's identity lets the controller ask for additional identifying information (Art. 12(6)); this does not justify holding data on file beyond its normal retention period merely so a possible future request can be verified.

### The rights themselves (Art. 15 to 22)

- Access: confirm whether the controller processes the data subject's data and, if so, give access to the data and the Art. 15(1)(a) to (h) information list (purposes, categories, recipients, retention period or its criteria, the rights available, the right to complain, the data's source if not collected from the data subject, and the automated-decision-making disclosure below); the first copy is free, a reasonable fee based on administrative cost applies to further copies, and a request made electronically is answered electronically unless the data subject asks otherwise (Art. 15(1), (3)).
- Rectification: correct inaccurate data and complete incomplete data without undue delay on request, including by a supplementary statement (Art. 16).
- Erasure: erase data without undue delay on any one of six grounds, no longer necessary for its purpose; consent withdrawn with no other basis; a successful objection under Art. 21(1) or an absolute objection under Art. 21(2); unlawful processing; a legal obligation to erase; or data collected from a child for an information-society service under Art. 8(1) (Art. 17(1)). None of these apply where erasure would conflict with freedom of expression and information, a legal obligation or public-task processing, public-health reasons under Art. 9(2)(h), (i) and Art. 9(3), archiving, research or statistics under Art. 89(1) where erasure would make the purpose impossible or seriously impair it, or the establishment, exercise or defence of legal claims (Art. 17(3)(a) to (e)). Where the data was made public, take reasonable technical steps, weighed against available technology and cost, to tell other controllers processing it that erasure was requested (Art. 17(2)).
- Restriction: restrict processing on request in four situations, accuracy contested while it is verified; processing unlawful but the data subject opposes erasure and asks for restriction instead; the controller no longer needs the data but the data subject needs it for legal claims; or an objection under Art. 21(1) is pending verification (Art. 18(1)). Once restricted, the data may only be stored, or processed further with consent, for legal claims, to protect another person's rights, or for an important Union or Member State public interest (Art. 18(2)); tell the data subject before lifting the restriction (Art. 18(3)).
- Notification: tell every recipient of a rectification, erasure under Art. 17(1), or restriction, unless impossible or disproportionate, and name those recipients to the data subject on request (Art. 19).
- Portability: where processing rests on consent (Art. 6(1)(a) or Art. 9(2)(a)) or on a contract (Art. 6(1)(b)) and happens by automated means, give the data subject their provided data in a structured, commonly used, machine-readable format, and let them transmit it elsewhere, including directly between controllers where technically feasible (Art. 20(1), (2)). This does not cover processing necessary for a public task (Art. 20(3)) or adversely affect the rights of others (Art. 20(4)).
- Objection, general grounds: on grounds relating to the data subject's particular situation, stop processing under the public-task or legitimate-interest bases (Art. 6(1)(e), (f)), including profiling on those bases, unless compelling legitimate grounds override the data subject's interests or the data is needed for legal claims (Art. 21(1)).
- Objection, direct marketing: stop processing for direct marketing, including related profiling, the moment the data subject objects, with no override available; flag this right clearly and separately from other information, no later than the first communication with the data subject (Art. 21(2) to (4)). Where information-society services are used, let the data subject object by automated means using technical specifications (Art. 21(5)).
- Automated decisions: give the data subject the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing, including profiling, that produces legal effects or similarly significantly affects them, unless the decision is necessary for a contract between the data subject and the controller, authorised by Union or Member State law with suitable safeguards, or based on the data subject's explicit consent (Art. 22(1), (2)). Where the contract or consent ground is used, implement at least human intervention on the controller's part, a chance to express a view, and a chance to contest the decision (Art. 22(3)). Such a decision must not rest on the special categories of data in Art. 9(1) unless explicit consent (Art. 9(2)(a)) or a substantial-public-interest basis (Art. 9(2)(g)) applies with suitable safeguards (Art. 22(4)).
- Automated-decision disclosure: at the point data is collected from the data subject, or within the Art. 14 timing if not, disclose the existence of Art. 22(1) and (4) automated decision-making, with meaningful information about the logic involved and the significance and envisaged consequences for the data subject (Art. 13(2)(f), Art. 14(2)(g)); give the same information on an access request (Art. 15(1)(h)).

### Automated decision-making extends up the chain (Case C-634/21)

- Three cumulative conditions decide whether an automated output is an Art. 22(1) decision: an act evaluating personal aspects, read broadly enough to include a score itself; based solely on automated processing, including profiling as defined in Art. 4(4); and producing legal effects, or similarly significantly affecting, the person (Case C-634/21, para 43). A probability value calculated from a person's characteristics by mathematical and statistical methods satisfies the second condition on the Art. 4(4) definition (para 47); a score that leads the receiving third party to refuse the person a loan in almost all cases of an insufficient score satisfies the third (paras 48, 49).
- A scoring business whose output a third party draws strongly on to decide about a person needs its own Art. 22(2) legal basis for the scoring step itself, separate from whatever basis covers the downstream decision: contract necessity, explicit consent, or an authorising Union or Member State law with suitable safeguards (Case C-634/21, para 53). Where the national-law ground (Art. 22(2)(b)) is used, the statute itself, not merely downstream practice around the score, must lay down the suitable safeguards; a statute that regulates only the "use" of an already-established score, without addressing its establishment, may fail to qualify (Case C-634/21, paras 65, 71, 72). A business relying on a national scoring statute as its Art. 22(2)(b) basis should check, before relying on it, whether that statute in fact reaches the act of establishing the score and not merely its later use.
- The scoring business owes the Art. 15(1)(h) access right (meaningful information about the logic, significance and envisaged consequences) directly, even though it is not the party making the final downstream decision; trade-secrecy objections to disclosing individual scoring factors and their weighting do not remove this right (Case C-634/21, paras 56, 63, applied to the facts at para 16).
- A downstream business that itself auto-decides on the score (granting or refusing credit, tenancy or a similar contract with no human review) carries its own, separate Art. 22(1) exposure and its own Art. 13(2)(f), Art. 14(2)(g) and Art. 15(1)(h) duties for that decision, on top of the scoring agency's duties for the scoring step; the downstream party generally does not hold the scoring logic and so cannot supply it even where its own act meets the Art. 22(1) conditions (Case C-634/21, para 63).
- A Member State cannot use Art. 22(2)(b) to add conditions to the consent, contract-necessity or legitimate-interest grounds in Art. 6(1)(a), (b) or (f); that regulatory latitude is confined to the public-task and legal-obligation grounds in Art. 6(1)(c) and (e) (Art. 6(3); Case C-634/21, para 69). A Member State also cannot use a statute to pre-fix the outcome of the Art. 6(1)(f) legitimate-interest balancing test (Case C-634/21, para 70).
- Relying on an Art. 22(2)(b) national-law basis does not excuse a scoring activity from the ordinary Art. 5 processing principles or from satisfying an Art. 6(1) lawfulness ground in its own right, with the controller able to demonstrate that compliance under the Art. 5(2) accountability principle (Case C-634/21, para 67).

## What the documents must not say

- A rights section must not impose a fee on a first access request, nor promise a response along the lines of as soon as possible with no outer time limit; Art. 12(3) and Art. 15(3) fix the one-month clock and the free first copy.
- A clause must not claim a blanket right to charge for, or decline, any rights request; Art. 12(5) permits a fee or a refusal only where the request is manifestly unfounded or excessive, and only with the controller able to show that character.
- A document must not describe automated decision-making about a person in a vague one-liner that decisions may be automated, without disclosing the logic, significance and envisaged consequences, and, wherever the decision is solely automated with legal or similarly significant effect, without offering human intervention, a chance to state a view, and a chance to contest, as Art. 22(3) requires.
- A scoring, ranking or similar automated-output business must not describe its output as mere data transmission to a third party that makes its own decision, as if the output sat outside the Regulation; after Case C-634/21, that framing does not remove the business's own Art. 22(1) exposure, its own legal-basis duty, or its own Art. 15(1)(h) access duty (Case C-634/21, paras 52, 56, 63, 64).
- A notice relying on a national scoring or similar automated-decision statute as its legal basis must not assume that statute is valid merely because it exists and looks on point; where the statute regulates only downstream use of an automated output and not its establishment, it may not satisfy Art. 22(2)(b) (Case C-634/21, paras 65, 71, 72).
- A marketing clause must not bury the direct-marketing objection right inside a long general terms paragraph; Art. 21(4) requires it presented clearly and separately, no later than the first communication.
- A portability clause must not promise portability for processing done on the public-task or legitimate-interest basis; Art. 20(1) limits the right to consent- and contract-based automated processing.
- A complaint-and-remedy section must not conflate the supervisory-authority complaint (Art. 77), the judicial remedy against that authority (Art. 78) and the separate judicial remedy directly against the controller or processor (Art. 79) into one generic contact-us line; each is a distinct right the data subject holds.
- A notice referencing that a qualified not-for-profit body may bring a complaint or claim on the data subject's behalf (Art. 80) must not state or imply that this restricts or replaces the data subject's own right to act directly.
- A joint-controller or joint-processor liability clause must not cap any one party's exposure to data subjects below the entire-damage standard of Art. 82(4); that provision makes each one liable for the whole damage so the data subject is compensated in full, and an internal apportionment under Art. 82(5) is a matter between the parties, never a limit enforceable against the data subject.
- A privacy notice, terms document or data processing agreement must not say that any GDPR infringement by itself creates a payable compensation claim; Case C-300/21 holds that Art. 82(1) requires an infringement, actual damage, and a causal link between them, all three cumulative, and an infringement with no shown damage confers no compensation right (Case C-300/21, para 42, operative point 1).
- A document must not condition an internal remedy or compensation commitment on the damage crossing some seriousness or materiality threshold, framed as the Art. 82 standard itself; Case C-300/21 holds a national rule or practice that does this is incompatible with Art. 82(1) (Case C-300/21, para 51, operative point 2). This does not let a controller imply that any upset at all is compensable: the data subject still has to show the negative consequences actually amount to non-material damage, and merely unpleasant feelings typically associated with a breach, with nothing more, do not qualify (Case C-300/21, para 50).
- A document must not promise a single, fixed EU-wide compensation formula; quantum is set by each Member State's own compensation rules, bounded only by the EU-law principles of equivalence and effectiveness (Case C-300/21, para 59, operative point 3).
- A document must not suggest that a data subject can recover punitive or exemplary damages under Art. 82; the right is compensatory only, and "full and effective" compensation means covering the actual damage in its entirety, with no payment of punitive damages required to reach that entirety (Case C-300/21, para 58).
- A document naming the fine exposure for an infringement must not state a single ceiling for every possible breach; Art. 83 splits into two bands and a controller should not conflate them (see Dates below for the figures).
- A clause must not claim that adherence to an approved code of conduct or certification mechanism exempts the controller from a fine; Art. 83(2)(j) lists such adherence only as one mitigating factor among several an authority weighs, not a safe harbour.

## Product behaviour it assumes

- A working rights-request channel exists for access, rectification, erasure, restriction, portability and objection, with a one-month clock (extendable by two months), a logged extension notice where used, and a record of what was shown or given to a given data subject and when, since the controller carries the burden of proof for every duty in this file (Art. 12(3), (5)).
- An identity-verification step sits in front of an access or other rights request, built for online services and online identifiers, without the controller keeping personal data on file purely so it can verify a request that might come later (Art. 12(6)).
- A correction and erasure mechanism exists for any data that feeds an automated score or decision, reaching beyond the raw inputs collected directly from the person, matching the Art. 5(1)(d) accuracy principle applied to scoring in Case C-634/21 (para 67).
- A stated retention period exists for both the inputs collected and any score or similar automated output produced from them, matching the Art. 5(1)(e) storage-limitation principle applied to scoring in Case C-634/21 (para 67).
- Where the product scores, ranks or otherwise automatically evaluates a person and hands that output to another party who relies on it, the scoring step itself has a documented Art. 22(2) legal basis, separate from and in addition to whatever basis covers the underlying data collection, and, if any input is or is derived from Art. 9(1) special-category data, a separate Art. 9(2) basis for that input (Case C-634/21, para 55).
- An automated-decision record (the logic used, its significance, and its envisaged consequences) exists and can be produced on an access request directly from the party that runs the automated step, even where that party is not the one making the final downstream decision about the person (Case C-634/21, paras 56, 63).
- Where the contract-necessity or explicit-consent ground supports an automated decision, a human-review path exists that lets the person obtain intervention, state a view, and contest the outcome (Art. 22(3)).
- A complaint-and-remedy flow or notice names all three independent routes (supervisory-authority complaint, judicial remedy against the authority, judicial remedy against the controller or processor) rather than funnelling every rights dispute into one internal contact point (Art. 77, 78, 79).
- A compensation or liability process, internal or within a data processing agreement, tracks the three-condition structure (infringement, damage, causal link) and preserves the exculpation defence (a party proves it is not in any way responsible for the damage) rather than a looser rule that treats any breach at all as triggering liability (Art. 82(2), (3); recital 146, second sentence).

## Dates

- 25 May 2018: the Regulation's general application date (Art. 99(2)), and the date by which Member States had to notify the Commission of their Art. 83(9) and Art. 84(2) implementing provisions.
- 7 December 2023: date of the judgment in Case C-634/21, OQ v Land Hessen (SCHUFA Holding AG intervening); this reading of Art. 22(1) remains the governing interpretation as of 2026-10-20, with no later Court of Justice ruling or GDPR amendment known to supersede it. The national-law question left open in that case, whether Paragraph 31 BDSG in fact satisfies Art. 22(2)(b), was left to the referring court to decide and is not reported here as settled.
- 4 May 2023: date of the judgment in Case C-300/21, UI v Österreichische Post AG; this reading of Art. 82(1) remains the leading Court of Justice authority as of 2026-10-20, with no later ruling in this file's sources superseding it.
- The lower Art. 83(4) fine band (up to EUR 10 million or 2 percent of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher) and the higher Art. 83(5), (6) band (up to EUR 20 million or 4 percent, whichever is higher) are unchanged since the Regulation's original 2016 text; the data subject rights in Art. 12 to 22 sit in the higher band (Art. 83(5)(b)).
- 2026-10-20: law-as-of date for this file.

## Open points to raise

- Whether any automated scoring, ranking or similarly decisive output the product generates is handed to, or relied on by, a third party to decide about a person, since Case C-634/21 places the scoring step itself under Art. 22, alongside the third party's later act, with its own legal-basis and disclosure duties.
- Where such an automated output is relied on by a third party, how strongly: whether the pattern resembles SCHUFA's facts, an insufficient score leading to refusal in almost all cases (Case C-634/21, paras 48, 49), or is closer to one input among several a human reviewer weighs, since the third Art. 22(1) condition, legal or similarly significant effect, turns on this factual degree of reliance and is not met merely because an automated output exists and is passed on.
- If a national-law basis (Art. 22(2)(b)) is the intended ground for any such automated output, whether that national statute in fact regulates the act of establishing the output rather than only its later use, since a statute of the latter kind may not qualify as a valid basis (Case C-634/21, paras 65, 71, 72); this needs the specific national statute named before it can be checked.
- Whether any compensation or liability clause the product or its agreements state has been checked against the three-condition, no-seriousness-threshold, no-punitive-damages reading in Case C-300/21, since a clause drafted before that date may still carry language the judgment displaced.
- Which Member State's compensation rules would govern quantum for a given data subject's claim, since Case C-300/21 leaves the amount to national law, bounded by equivalence and effectiveness, with no EU-wide formula to point to.
