# Unfair Contract Terms Directive (93/13/EEC)

Citation: Council Directive 93/13/EEC of 5 April 1993 on unfair terms in consumer contracts (OJ L
95, 21.4.1993, p. 29), as amended by Directive (EU) 2019/2161
Version: consolidated 28 May 2022 (CELEX 01993L0013-20220528)
Official text: https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:01993L0013-20220528
Law as of: 2026-10-20

This file is the directive-level reference for standard-form consumer terms across every EU
member state: terms of service, EULAs, subscription agreements and any other clause set a trader
offers a consumer without individual negotiation. The directive sets a floor; member states may go
further, and several have (see Open points). It works alongside, not instead of, the Consumer
Rights Directive (`references/eu/crd.md`), the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive
(`references/eu/ucpd.md`) and Germany's own clause-control rules in the BGB
(`references/de/bgb-agb.md`).

## When it applies

- The directive covers a contract between a seller or supplier (any natural or legal person
  acting for purposes relating to a trade, business or profession, publicly or privately owned)
  and a consumer (a natural person acting for purposes outside such purposes) (Art. 2(b), (c)). The
  same person can be a seller or supplier for one contract and a consumer for another: a lawyer
  buying professional services is a seller or supplier, but taking out a private loan makes the
  same lawyer a consumer, and professional expertise does not change that (Art. 2(b), (c), as read
  in Guidance section 1.2.1.1). A body pursuing a public-interest or charitable task, a not-for-
  profit organisation, and a trader acting through an ancillary activity (a company lending to its
  own staff, a school granting a student loan) all count as a seller or supplier (Guidance section
  1.2.1.1).
- Only terms not individually negotiated are tested. A term drafted in advance, with no real
  opportunity for the consumer to influence its substance, is always treated as not individually
  negotiated, which is the normal case for a pre-formulated standard contract; negotiating one
  clause, or one aspect of a clause, does not exempt the rest of a document that remains, overall, a
  pre-formulated standard contract (Art. 3(2), first and second subparagraph). The trader, not the
  consumer, carries the burden of proving a standard term was individually negotiated, and the
  consumer's signature alone, whether at the end of the document or beside an individual clause,
  does not discharge that burden (Art. 3(2), third subparagraph; Guidance section 1.2.2).
- The directive does not require the consumer to pay money. It reaches a guarantee contract with
  no stated price and a contract with a social-media provider paid for in user-generated content and
  profiling rather than cash (Guidance section 1.2.1.2).
- A term that reflects a mandatory statutory or regulatory provision, or a provision or principle of
  an international convention the member states or the Union have joined (particularly in
  transport), falls outside the directive (Art. 1(2)). Read this narrowly: it covers only the exact
  question the mandatory rule regulates, never an entire clause merely because part of it restates
  a statutory duty, and it reaches a mandatory rule adopted after conclusion that replaces an unfair
  term, limited to that same question (Guidance section 1.2.3). A provision counts as mandatory
  here if it applies to the parties independently of their choice, or by default where the parties
  made no other arrangement (Guidance section 1.2.3).
- The directive applies alongside other EU consumer-protection law covering the same contract (the
  Consumer Rights Directive, the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive, sector rules on credit,
  travel, telecoms or energy); later sector-specific legislation displaces it only where that
  legislation says so explicitly, which is rare (Guidance section 1.2.4).
- For a consumer habitually resident in the EU contracting with a trader established outside the
  EU and EEA, Regulation (EC) No 593/2008 (Rome I) Art. 6(1) applies the law of the consumer's
  habitual residence whenever the trader directs commercial activity there, and the parties cannot
  contract out of the mandatory consumer-protection rules that law supplies. Art. 6(2) of this
  directive separately requires member states to stop a consumer losing this directive's own
  protection through a non-member-country choice-of-law clause wherever the contract has a close
  connection with a member state's territory, a broader test than Rome I's own Art. 6 (Art. 6(2);
  Guidance section 1.2.5).

## What the documents must say

- Every written term, whatever its subject, must be in plain, intelligible language (Art. 5, first
  sentence). This applies to all written terms, including the ones excluded from the unfairness test
  below.
- Where a written term's meaning is doubtful, the interpretation most favourable to the consumer
  prevails; this contra-proferentem rule does not apply within an Art. 7(2) collective action before
  a court or administrative body (Art. 5, second and third sentence).
- Plain, intelligible language is not satisfied by formal and grammatical correctness alone.
  Substantive transparency requires that the consumer can use clear, intelligible criteria to
  evaluate the term's real economic consequences, including how its mechanism works and how it
  relates to other terms in the contract; wording a consumer cannot use to estimate the actual cost
  fails the test even if every sentence parses correctly, and the burden of proving adequate
  disclosure sits with the drafter (Guidance section 3.3.1). Relevant factors include whether the
  consumer had a genuine chance to read the term (including any annex or referenced document)
  before conclusion, whether the wording and terminology suit the consumer actually addressed, and
  whether the presentation (visual clarity, font size, logical structure, prominence of important
  stipulations) keeps related terms whose joint effect must be read together from being scattered
  in a way that obscures that joint effect (Guidance section 3.3.1).
- For a loan, credit or long-term supply term, check that all information bearing on the extent of
  the consumer's commitment was communicated, so the consumer can estimate the total cost; for a
  foreign-currency loan, the exchange-rate risk and the possible rate variations must be set out,
  including in promotional material used during negotiation, with the burden of proving this again
  on the drafter (Guidance section 3.3.1). Where a sector-specific EU instrument imposes its own
  pre-contractual information duty (for example a credit annual percentage rate), meeting that duty
  is a floor for this directive's own transparency standard, not a ceiling: failing the
  sector-specific duty is decisive evidence against plain, intelligible language here, and meeting
  it does not by itself prove this directive's broader standard is also met (Guidance section
  3.3.2).
- A sanction clause (default interest, acceleration, a penalty for breach) must be proportionate to
  the importance of the obligation breached and the seriousness of the breach, benchmarked against
  the applicable default statutory rate or the default procedural position the clause departs from
  (Guidance section 3.4.3). Every sanction clause in a contract must be assessed together, not one
  at a time: a combination of individually modest sanctions can still be unfair as a whole, and this
  applies regardless of whether the trader has ever actually enforced every clause (Guidance section
  3.4.3).

## What the documents must not say

- A term that has not been individually negotiated must not, contrary to the requirement of good
  faith, cause a significant imbalance in the parties' rights and obligations to the consumer's
  detriment (Art. 3(1)). Both elements are required: a breach of good faith, and a significant
  imbalance that harms the consumer. Good faith here is an objective standard: whether the trader,
  dealing fairly and equitably with the consumer, could reasonably assume the consumer would have
  agreed to the term in individual negotiations (Guidance section 3.4.1). To gauge significant
  imbalance, compare the term against the supplementary (default) rules of national law that would
  apply without it; the imbalance is significant where the term seriously impairs the consumer's
  legal position under those default rules, whether by restricting a right, constraining how it can
  be exercised, or adding an obligation the default rules did not provide for, and this is not a
  purely quantitative comparison of value against cost (Guidance section 3.4.2).
- Unfairness is assessed at the time of conclusion, taking into account the nature of the goods or
  services, all other terms of that contract or a contract it depends on, and all the circumstances
  attending conclusion (Art. 4(1)). Circumstances arising only during performance do not count,
  except circumstances the trader knew or could reasonably have foreseen at conclusion that could
  affect future performance, such as foreign-exchange risk on a currency loan (Guidance sections
  3.4.1, 3.4.5).
- A term defining the main subject matter of the contract, or the adequacy of the price or
  remuneration against what is supplied in exchange, is excluded from the unfairness test only so
  far as it is in plain, intelligible language (Art. 4(2)). Because this is an exception to the
  general protective rule, it is read strictly: the term must be both correctly classified as main
  subject matter or price, and genuinely transparent, or the exclusion does not apply and the term
  is reviewed under Art. 3(1) like any other (Guidance section 3.2). Main subject matter means a
  term stating the contract's essential, characterising obligations, rather than an ancillary
  mechanism: a term requiring a foreign-currency loan to be repaid in that same currency is main
  subject matter,
  but the currency-conversion mechanism itself is ancillary and stays fully exposed to scrutiny
  (Guidance section 3.2.1). A price-variation mechanism is never covered by this exclusion, however
  transparent it is, because the exclusion reaches only the adequacy of the price itself, not a
  mechanism for changing it (Art. 4(2); Guidance section 3.2.2).
- Lack of transparency does not, by itself, make a term unfair: a term failing the plain-language
  test must still be separately assessed under Art. 3(1), and a fully transparent term can still be
  unfair on its content. Lack of transparency is, however, a significant factor pointing toward
  unfairness, in particular for a unilateral-change term or a choice-of-law term (Guidance section
  3.4.6).
- The Annex sets out an indicative, non-exhaustive list of terms that may be regarded as unfair
  (Art. 3(3)); see the Annex summary below. A term matching an Annex entry is not automatically
  unfair, and a term absent from the Annex can still fail the Art. 3(1) test (Art. 3(3); Guidance
  section 3.4.7).
- A choice-of-law clause naming the trader's home member state is unfair under Art. 3(1) unless it
  unambiguously tells the consumer that the mandatory consumer-protection rules of their own
  country of habitual residence under Rome I Art. 6(2) still apply; the same reasoning extends to a
  clause choosing a non-EU country's law. A clause governed by a non-EU country's law that removes
  mandatory protection the consumer would otherwise have under Rome I, or that fails to flag the
  consumer's residual protection under Art. 6(2) of this directive, risks being itself an unfair
  term (Guidance section 1.2.5).
- A clause must not purport to waive, in advance, the consumer's protection under Art. 6(1), whether
  framed as a signature acknowledgment, an acceptance clause, or any other declaration; such a
  waiver is ineffective before a dispute over the specific term has been settled (Guidance section
  4.1).
- A severability or "blue pencil" clause cannot, on its own, turn an inseverable stipulation into a
  severable one. A court may partially delete what looks like one term only where it in fact
  contains two or more functionally distinct stipulations, each of which stays clear and assessable
  once the other is removed; this depends on the content and function of each stipulation, not on
  how the drafting presents or arranges it (Guidance section 4.3.1). A court that finds a term
  unfair has no power to revise or reduce it (for example scaling down a disproportionate penalty to
  an acceptable level): the whole term is disapplied (Guidance section 4.3.1).
- A term replacing or renegotiating a term already found unfair, or a settlement or novation clause
  tied to that process, must not ask the consumer to give up restitution or other rights arising
  from the original term's unfair character; the new term must itself be tested under Art. 3 to 5
  like any other (Guidance section 4.3.1).
- A refund, overpayment or limitation clause must not cap or time-limit the consumer's restitution
  claim beyond the ordinary national rules on res judicata and reasonable limitation periods. The
  non-binding character of an unfair term, and the restitution that follows from it, is never
  subject to a later-imposed cut-off date, for example one confining restitution to amounts paid
  after a finding of unfairness (Guidance section 4.4).
- A jurisdiction, arbitration or ADR clause must not route consumer disputes to a forum materially
  distant from the consumer's residence, where this deters appearance; an arbitration clause must
  not exclude or hinder the consumer's access to courts or to effective judicial review of unfair
  terms; and an ADR-submission agreement concluded before a dispute has arisen does not bind the
  consumer if it would deprive them of the right to go to court instead (Guidance sections 5.4.2,
  5.7, citing Art. 10 of Directive 2013/11/EU). A clause obliging the consumer to submit to a court
  far from their domicile, making it hard for them to enter an appearance, falls within Annex point
  (q) (Guidance section 3.4.7).
- A terms document must not include a clause that purports to remove, limit or contract around the
  consumer's entitlement to have a court assess the fairness of a term of its own motion: this is a
  matter of public policy the parties cannot modify by agreement (Guidance sections 5.2.2, 5.3.1).

## Annex: indicative list of suspect term patterns (Art. 3(3))

The Annex lists terms whose object or effect may be regarded as unfair, lettered (a) to (q); it is
a checklist, not a closed blacklist, and the carve-outs below must be checked before concluding a
listed item applies.

- (a) excluding or limiting the trader's liability for a consumer's death or personal injury caused
  by the trader's act or omission.
- (b) unduly excluding or limiting the consumer's legal rights against the trader or a third party
  for total or partial non-performance or defective performance, including denying a set-off of a
  debt the consumer owes the trader against a claim the consumer holds against the trader.
- (c) binding the consumer to the contract while the trader's own performance stays conditional on
  something within the trader's own will alone.
- (d) letting the trader keep sums the consumer paid where the consumer decides not to conclude or
  perform the contract, with no equivalent compensation right for the consumer where it is the
  trader who cancels.
- (e) requiring a consumer who fails to perform to pay a disproportionately high compensation sum.
- (f) letting the trader dissolve the contract at its own discretion without giving the consumer the
  same right, or letting the trader keep payment for services not yet supplied when the trader
  itself dissolves the contract.
- (g) letting the trader terminate an indefinite-duration contract without reasonable notice, except
  for serious grounds. Carve-out: a financial-services supplier may reserve an unnotified, immediate
  termination right for an indefinite-duration contract where there is a valid reason, provided the
  supplier must inform the other party immediately (Annex point 2(a)).
- (h) automatically renewing a fixed-term contract unless the consumer objects, where the deadline
  set for the consumer's objection comes unreasonably early.
- (i) irrevocably binding the consumer to terms they had no real opportunity to become acquainted
  with before conclusion.
- (j) letting the trader alter contract terms unilaterally without a valid reason specified in the
  contract. Carve-outs: a financial-services supplier may reserve the right to alter, without
  notice, an interest rate or other financial-service charge where there is a valid reason, provided
  the supplier informs the other party at the earliest opportunity and the other party is free to
  dissolve the contract immediately; and any trader may reserve the right to alter unilaterally the
  terms of an indefinite-duration contract, provided the trader gives the consumer reasonable
  notice and the consumer is free to dissolve the contract (Annex point 2(b)). A price-amendment
  term specifically must state the reason for, and the method of, any price variation, with the
  consumer given the right to terminate the contract; a unilateral-change clause naming no
  contract-stated reason, no method, and no consumer termination right is exposed under both point
  (i) and point (j) (Guidance section 3.4.7, citing Case C-472/10 Invitel, para. 24).
- (k) letting the trader unilaterally alter, without valid reason, a characteristic of the product
  or service to be provided.
- (l) fixing the final price at delivery, or letting the trader raise the agreed price, without
  giving the consumer a matching right to cancel if the final price is too high against the price
  agreed at conclusion. Carve-out: a lawful price-indexation clause is not caught, provided the
  method by which the price varies is explicitly described (Annex point 2(d)). Unilateral
  price-variation mechanisms are, separately, never excluded from the ordinary unfairness test by
  Art. 4(2) regardless of how transparent they are (see above).
- Items (g), (j) and (l) all exclude transactions in transferable securities, financial instruments
  or other products or services priced by reference to stock-exchange, index or financial-market
  fluctuations the trader does not control, and contracts to buy or sell foreign currency,
  traveller's cheques or international money orders denominated in foreign currency (Annex point
  2(c)).
- (m) giving the trader the sole right to decide whether goods or services conform to the contract,
  or the exclusive right to interpret a contract term.
- (n) limiting the trader's duty to honour commitments made by its agents, or conditioning those
  commitments on a particular formality.
- (o) obliging the consumer to perform all its obligations even where the trader does not perform
  its own.
- (p) letting the trader transfer its rights and obligations under the contract, where this could
  reduce the consumer's guarantees, without the consumer's agreement.
- (q) excluding or hindering the consumer's right to take legal action or pursue another legal
  remedy, in particular by routing disputes exclusively to an arbitration scheme outside the law, by
  unduly restricting the evidence available to the consumer, or by shifting onto the consumer a
  burden of proof the applicable law would otherwise place on the other party.

## Consequence of an unfair term

- An unfair term is not binding on the consumer; the rest of the contract continues to bind the
  parties if it is capable of continuing in existence without that term (Art. 6(1)). The directive
  severs the offending term rather than voiding the whole contract where that remains workable.
- The term is treated, in principle, as never having existed, so a court's finding restores the
  consumer to the position they would have been in without it, from the moment the term entered the
  contract (ex tunc), rather than from the date of judgment; no prior court declaration is needed for
  the consumer to benefit, though a declaration gives certainty where fairness is disputed (Guidance
  section 4.2). Non-binding character does not depend on whether, or when, the consumer raised the
  point: a national rule barring a late-raised plea of unfairness, or one barring a court from
  finding unfairness of its own motion once a limitation period has expired, is precluded (Guidance
  section 4.2).
- Partial deletion of an unfair term is admissible only where it in fact contains separable
  stipulations that stay clear and assessable once one is removed; otherwise the whole term is
  disapplied (Guidance section 4.3.1, see above).
- Where deleting the unfair term would mean the contract cannot continue in existence, a court
  assesses that objectively as a question of national law, without regard to either party's economic
  preference; only where nullity would otherwise follow, and would be particularly unfavourable to
  the consumer, may the court substitute a supplementary provision of national law instead, judged
  against the consumer's interest as it stands when the question reaches the court (Guidance section
  4.3.2). Where continuation is legally impossible and voiding the contract would not be against the
  consumer's interests, national law may not prevent the consumer from relying on the contract's
  nullity (Guidance section 4.3.2). Where deletion of the term does not void the contract, case law
  gives no basis for a supplementary provision at all (Guidance section 4.3.3).
- A consumer, once informed by the court of a term's unfairness and its non-binding character, may
  decide not to invoke that protection, so the term applies after all; this requires the court to be
  satisfied the consumer fully understood the position and was not acting under misconception or
  pressure (Guidance sections 4.3.4, 5.5.4).
- Consumers who paid under an unfair term are entitled to reimbursement of what was not due, limited
  only by ordinary legal-certainty rules (res judicata, reasonable limitation periods); no member
  state may impose its own temporal cut-off on the restitutory effect of an unfairness finding, for
  example by confining restitution to payments made after the finding (Guidance section 4.4).
- A choice of a non-member country's law cannot be used to strip the consumer of this protection
  where the contract has a close connection with the territory of the member states (Art. 6(2)).

## Enforcement

- Member states must ensure adequate and effective means exist, in the interests of consumers and
  competing traders, to prevent the continued use of unfair terms in consumer contracts (Art. 7(1)).
  This includes letting persons or organisations with a legitimate interest under national law in
  consumer protection bring the question of whether a general-use term is unfair before a court or
  competent administrative body, independent of any individual consumer's own dispute, so that
  appropriate and effective measures can stop its continued use (Art. 7(2)). That action can target
  several traders or associations from the same economic sector jointly, where they use or recommend
  the same or similar general terms (Art. 7(3)).
- A term declared unfair in a collective injunction is not binding on the consumers who were
  parties to that action alone: it is not binding on every consumer who has, or later concludes, a
  contract with that same trader containing the same term; it is treated as unfair in all of that
  trader's future consumer contracts (Guidance section 6, citing Case C-472/10 Invitel). A national register of
  terms found unfair in final judgments, letting an enforcement authority fine another trader for an
  equivalent term, is compatible with Art. 8 in principle, provided that trader has an effective
  judicial remedy against both the equivalence finding and the fine (Guidance section 6, citing Case
  C-119/15 Biuro podróży 'Partner'). A collective injunction and an individual consumer's own action
  are complementary: a rule automatically suspending an individual action pending a parallel
  collective one is precluded, and a consumer running an individual action keeps the right to
  dissociate their claim from a parallel collective action over comparable terms (Guidance section
  6, citing Joined Cases C-381/14 and C-385/14 Sales Sinués and Drame Ba).
- National courts must assess a covered term's unfairness of their own motion (ex officio), even
  where the consumer never raised it, whenever they have the legal and factual elements needed to do
  so; this duty runs even more strongly where a consumer challenges the contract in substance
  without naming the unfair-terms provisions specifically (Guidance section 5.2.1). Wherever
  national law lets or requires a court to raise a public-policy point of its own motion, it must do
  the same for this directive's protection, which the Court of Justice treats as equivalent to
  public policy; this does not depend on first showing that, without it, there would be no effective
  protection (Guidance section 5.3.1).
- A remedy is ineffective wherever there is a significant risk consumers will not use it, whether
  from one serious procedural obstacle or several moderate ones together: short deadlines, lawyer
  costs or a representation requirement, a distant forum, a reasons requirement, or the consumer's
  own lack of knowledge of their rights (Art. 7(1); Guidance sections 5.4.1, 5.4.2). Where there is a
  significant risk a consumer will not object to a payment order, a court must check the terms'
  fairness before the order issues or, failing that, at the latest at the enforcement stage, and an
  earlier check that did not cover every term does not excuse a later court from reviewing the
  remainder, even where the earlier decision otherwise has res judicata effect (Guidance section
  5.4.1). Enforcement of a mortgage on a notarial deed may start without a prior judicial check only
  if the consumer can still challenge it, including through interim relief, under reasonable
  conditions, and ex officio control is guaranteed in the parallel declaratory proceedings (Guidance
  section 5.4.1).
- Ex officio control is proactive: courts must investigate, including by asking the parties for
  clarification, whether a party is a consumer, whether a term was individually negotiated, and
  whether disclosures were made; a procedural rule denying the court access to the contract itself
  does not excuse this duty (Guidance section 5.5.3). Where EU law requires ex officio control,
  national courts achieve it first through EU-conforming interpretation of national law and, failing
  that, by setting aside the conflicting national procedural rule (Guidance section 5.6). A
  last-instance court's failure to review a term's unfairness of its own motion can be a sufficiently
  serious breach of EU law to expose the member state to a damages claim (Guidance section 5.6).
  These procedural guarantees bind courts and tribunals; they do not extend to a notary affixing an
  enforcement clause, though the notary's role may still be taken into account when assessing the
  overall procedure's effectiveness (Guidance section 5.7).

## Minimum harmonisation and penalties

- Member states may adopt or keep provisions more protective of the consumer than this directive
  requires, compatible with the Treaty (Art. 8); this is a floor, not a ceiling, and national
  implementation is regularly stricter, including through a "black list" of terms always treated as
  unfair, a "grey list" of terms presumed unfair, extension of the unfairness test to individually
  negotiated terms or to price adequacy, or a rule that a lack of transparency alone invalidates a
  term (Guidance section 2.1).
- A member state adopting such stricter or scope-extending provisions must notify the Commission of
  them, and of any later change, in particular where the provision extends the unfairness assessment
  to individually negotiated terms or to price or remuneration adequacy, or adds its own list of
  unfair terms (Art. 8a(1)). The Commission must make that information easily accessible to consumers and
  traders, including on a dedicated website, and forward it to the other member states and the
  European Parliament (Art. 8a(2), (3)).
- National penalties for breach of the implementing provisions must be effective, proportionate and
  dissuasive (Art. 8b(1)); a member state may restrict penalties to terms expressly defined as unfair
  in all circumstances under national law, or to a trader's continued use of a term found unfair in
  a final Art. 7(2) decision (Art. 8b(2)). Where penalties are imposed under Art. 21 of Regulation
  (EU) 2017/2394, the statutory maximum fine must reach at least 4% of the trader's annual turnover
  in the member state or states concerned, or at least EUR 2 million where turnover data is
  unavailable (Art. 8b(4), (5)). The following criteria, non-exhaustive, must feed into how the
  penalty is set, where appropriate: the nature, gravity, scale and duration of the infringement; any
  mitigation the trader undertook; any previous infringements by the same trader; the financial
  benefit gained or loss avoided, where known; penalties already imposed on the same trader for the
  same infringement in other member states in cross-border cases, where known through the
  Regulation (EU) 2017/2394 mechanism; and any other aggravating or mitigating factor (Art. 8b(3)).

## Product behaviour it assumes

- Classification of each counterparty (seller or supplier, consumer) happens before any clause is
  drafted, and does not rely on a self-declared business-use-only checkbox as self-enforcing, since
  the classification is a functional, case-by-case test tied to the specific contract (Guidance
  section 1.2.1.1).
- Price-variation, unilateral-change and sanction clauses are reviewed for substantive transparency
  and proportionality as a matter of course, whether or not a specific jurisdiction's stricter rule
  applies, since neither escapes scrutiny under the directive's own minimum standard.
- Records capture what pre-contractual information was shown to a consumer and when, for any term
  relying on the Art. 4(2) exclusion or on a sector-specific disclosure duty, since the burden of
  proving adequate disclosure sits with the drafter (Guidance section 3.3.1).
- The dispute-resolution flow preserves a genuine court check of contested terms at some point
  before enforcement is completed against a consumer, including at the enforcement stage as a last
  resort, and does not route every dispute through a procedure with no such check (Guidance section
  5.4.1).
- No settlement, novation or renewal process asks a consumer to waive restitution or other rights
  tied to a term later found, or already found, unfair (Guidance section 4.3.1).
- A multi-member-state terms document is checked against each target member state's own black list,
  grey list or scope extension under Art. 8, in addition to this directive's own Annex, since
  national lists vary materially and can invalidate a term the directive's minimum standard would
  tolerate (Art. 8, 8a; Guidance section 2.1).

## Prescribed wording

None. Neither this directive nor the Commission's guidance on it fixes a form of words a contract
must reproduce; the directive states a test, a legal consequence, and an indicative list of clause
patterns, not phrases to copy.

## Dates

- National implementing measures apply to all contracts concluded after 31 December 1994; the
  directive does not reach contracts concluded on or before that date (Art. 10(1)).
- Member states had to notify the Commission of their Art. 8b penalty rules and measures by 28
  November 2021, and must notify any later amendment without delay (Art. 8b(6)).
- The ex officio control duty traces to the Court of Justice's ruling of 4 June 2009 and has been
  confirmed repeatedly since (Guidance section 5.2.1).
- The Commission Notice (2019/C 323/04) reflects Court of Justice case law published through 31 May
  2019 and does not cover developments after that date; it also predates the Directive (EU)
  2019/2161 amendments (Art. 8a, 8b, and the amended Art. 7(3), 8) that this file's statutory text
  already reflects, since those amendments became applicable from 28 May 2022.
- 2026-10-20: law-as-of date for this file.

## Open points to raise

- Which member states the product is sold into, since Art. 8 lets each one adopt stricter rules
  (a black list, a grey list, broader coverage of individually negotiated or price terms) than this
  directive's own minimum, and a term clearing the directive's Annex can still fail a national list;
  for any state other than Germany, the national transposition is an open point until named.
- Whether a settlement, novation or renewal process asks a consumer to release a restitution claim
  tied to a term that was, or may be, unfair. As of the Commission's guidance (31 May 2019), the
  Court of Justice had a reference pending on exactly this question (Case C-452/18 Ibercaja Banco,
  concerning a loan novation); the guidance records no outcome, so treat such a waiver as high risk
  rather than settled-safe until the question is confirmed as answered, and name any clause of this
  kind for review.
- Whether a jurisdiction, arbitration or ADR clause in the product's terms has in fact been tested
  against the effectiveness factors above (deadline length, fee level, representation requirement,
  forum distance) for the specific member states in use, since no single factor is disqualifying on
  its own but a combination can be.
