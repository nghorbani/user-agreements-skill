# GDPR lawful bases for processing (Art. 5 to 11)

Citation: Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 27 April 2016 on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data, and repealing Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation)
Version: original text, no substantive consolidation exists
Official text: https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32016R0679
Law as of: 2026-10-20

This file covers the lawfulness principle and the six Art. 6(1) bases a drafter chooses between: consent, contract necessity, legal obligation, vital interest, public task and legitimate interest; the Art. 7 consent conditions, Art. 8 child consent and Art. 9 special-category rules that sit next to them; and three European Data Protection Board guidelines cited here by their short number: Guidelines 05/2020 on consent under Regulation 2016/679; Guidelines 2/2019 on the processing of personal data under Art. 6(1)(b) GDPR in the context of the provision of online services to data subjects; and Guidelines 1/2024 on the processing of personal data based on Art. 6(1)(f) GDPR. It also covers Case C-61/19, Orange Romania SA v Autoritatea Nationala de Supraveghere a Prelucrarii Datelor cu Caracter Personal (ANSPDCP), judgment of 11 November 2020, on what a consent clause must do to prove consent. Processor duties, international transfers and data subject rights beyond the ones tied directly to a lawful basis are covered in `references/eu/gdpr-processors-transfers.md` and `references/eu/gdpr-rights-liability.md`.

## When it applies

- Every processing activity needs its own lawful basis under Art. 6(1) before it starts, chosen and documented in advance; a controller carries the burden of proving that basis held, not merely of asserting it (Art. 5(2); Art. 6(1)). None of the six bases ranks above another, and legitimate interest in particular is not a fallback used only because no other basis fits (Guidelines 1/2024, para. 9).
- Where several purposes exist, each needs its own basis, identified from the outset and stated to the data subject under Art. 13(1)(c) or Art. 14(1)(c) (Guidelines 1/2024, para. 10; Guidelines 2/2019, para. 17).
- Legitimate interest under Art. 6(1)(f) is closed to processing a public authority carries out in performing its own tasks; it stays open to a public authority acting outside those tasks under a Member State law that permits it (Art. 6(1), second subparagraph; Guidelines 1/2024, paras. 8, 98 to 99).
- Art. 8 child-consent conditions apply only where both hold together: the processing concerns an information society service offered directly to a child, and the basis relied on is consent (Art. 8(1); Guidelines 05/2020, para. 127). A service that states it is offered only to adults 18 or over is not "offered directly to a child", unless other evidence (site content, marketing) undermines that statement (Guidelines 05/2020, para. 130). A mixed online and physical-delivery service is split into its two components for this test; only the online component is an information society service (Guidelines 05/2020, para. 129).
- Art. 9's special-category prohibition (see Special categories of data below) applies regardless of which Art. 6(1) basis would otherwise cover the processing; a controller needs both an Art. 6(1) basis and an Art. 9(2) ground, assessed separately, for the same processing (Guidelines 2/2019, para. 21; Guidelines 1/2024, para. 40 footnote 47).

## What the documents must say

### The six lawful bases and how to pick one (Art. 6)

- Name the specific Art. 6(1) basis relied on for each processing purpose; a clause that says data is processed "as permitted by law" without naming a point (a) to (f) ground meets neither the purpose-limitation nor the transparency arm of Art. 5(1)(a), (b).
- The Regulation fixes a short German label for each Art. 5(1) principle in parentheses after its own text, routinely cited by that label alone in German privacy notices and processing agreements: "Rechtmäßigkeit, Verarbeitung nach Treu und Glauben, Transparenz" (Art. 5 Abs. 1 Buchst. a), "Zweckbindung" (Buchst. b), "Datenminimierung" (Buchst. c), "Richtigkeit" (Buchst. d), "Speicherbegrenzung" (Buchst. e), "Integrität und Vertraulichkeit" (Buchst. f), and "Rechenschaftspflicht" for the Art. 5 Abs. 2 accountability duty. Art. 7 to 9 carry no comparable fixed label of their own; use "Einwilligung" (consent), "Verantwortlicher" (controller) and the other Art. 4 defined terms instead when drafting in German.
- Consent (Art. 6(1)(a)) is the data subject's own freely given, specific, informed and unambiguous indication of wishes, given by a statement or a clear affirmative action (Art. 4(11)); see Consent below for its full conditions.
- Contract necessity (Art. 6(1)(b)) covers processing objectively necessary either to perform a contract with the data subject, or to take steps at the data subject's own request before entering one (Art. 6(1)(b); Guidelines 2/2019, para. 22); see Contract necessity below.
- Legal obligation (Art. 6(1)(c)) and public task (Art. 6(1)(e)) both need their basis laid down by Union or Member State law that fixes the purpose, serves a public-interest objective and is proportionate to it; a Member State may adapt how these two apply with more specific rules (Art. 6(1)(c), (e); Art. 6(2), (3)).
- Vital interest (Art. 6(1)(d)) covers processing necessary to protect the vital interests of the data subject or of another natural person.
- Legitimate interest (Art. 6(1)(f)) covers processing necessary for a legitimate interest of the controller or a third party, unless the data subject's interests or fundamental rights and freedoms override it, particularly where the data subject is a child (Art. 6(1)(f)); see Legitimate interest below.
- Where data collected for one purpose will be processed for a new, different purpose not covered by fresh consent or by a qualifying law, assess compatibility against the original purpose using the link between the two purposes, the context of collection, the nature of the data (special-category or criminal data weighs heavily), the consequences for the data subject, and any safeguard such as encryption or pseudonymisation (Art. 6(4)).
- A controller cannot present a processing activity to the data subject as resting on consent while actually relying, or later coming to rely, on a different basis for that same activity; nor can it retrospectively invoke legitimate interest to rescue processing after a problem surfaces with consent originally disclosed as the basis (Guidelines 05/2020, paras. 122, 123).

### Consent (Art. 7; Guidelines 05/2020)

- Demonstrate that the data subject consented; keep enough of a record to show the link between a given consent and the processing it covers, without collecting more than needed for that purpose (Art. 7(1); Guidelines 05/2020, paras. 104, 106, 107).
- Present a consent request bundled with other matters (a sign-up form, a set of terms) in a way clearly distinguishable from those other matters, in an intelligible and accessible form, using clear and plain language; a declaration mixing several purposes needs a separate opt-in per purpose, not one bundled tick (Art. 7(2); Guidelines 05/2020, paras. 55, 60, 71).
- Tell the data subject of the right to withdraw before they consent, and let withdrawal be as easy as giving consent, through the same interface used to give it, free of charge and without a service downgrade (Art. 7(3); Guidelines 05/2020, paras. 113, 114).
- When assessing whether consent was freely given, give utmost weight to whether performance of the contract, including a service, was made conditional on consent to processing not necessary for that contract; this is a factor the assessment must weigh heavily, not a flat ban on ever asking (Art. 7(4)). Consent is presumed not freely given where it does not allow separate consent per processing operation despite that being appropriate, or where contract performance is made dependent on consent that is not necessary for it (Recital 43).
- Consent must be a statement or a clear affirmative act, never inferred from silence, a pre-ticked box, inactivity or scrolling or swiping through a page; a controller must not obtain it through the same motion by which the data subject accepts a contract or general terms (Art. 4(11); Recital 32; Guidelines 05/2020, paras. 79, 81, 86).
- Disclose, before asking: the controller's identity, the purpose of each processing operation, what data will be collected and used, the right to withdraw, any use for Art. 22 automated decision-making, and any transfer risk absent an adequacy decision or Art. 46 safeguards; where several controllers rely on the same consent, name all of them (Guidelines 05/2020, para. 64, 65).
- Explicit consent, a higher standard needing an express statement (not merely an affirmative act), is required for special-category processing (Art. 9), for a third-country transfer absent adequate safeguards (Art. 49), and for solely automated decision-making including profiling (Art. 22); it may be given by a signed statement, an electronic form, an email, an uploaded signed document, an electronic signature, or an oral statement paired with a specific confirmation step (Guidelines 05/2020, paras. 91, 93 to 95, 98).
- Consent does not reduce any other Art. 5 duty: necessity, proportionality and minimisation apply independently of whether consent was obtained (Guidelines 05/2020, para. 5). A vague purpose such as general service improvement, marketing, IT security or future research fails the specificity the consent request needs (Guidelines 05/2020, para. 56).
- Consent obtained before the Regulation applied remains valid only to the extent it already meets the Art. 4(11) and Art. 7 standard; a presumed consent kept with no record of how and when it was given does not meet that standard, nor does one based on a more implied action such as a pre-ticked opt-in box, and either must be renewed (Guidelines 05/2020, paras. 166 to 169).

### Children's consent (Art. 8; Guidelines 05/2020)

- Below 16, a child's consent to an information society service offered directly to them is lawful only where given or authorised by the holder of parental responsibility, verified by reasonable effort given available technology; a Member State may lower this floor, never below 13 (Art. 8(1), (2)).
- Check the applicable floor for every Member State the service reaches; states differ on whether their law looks to the controller's main establishment or to the data subject's residence to pick the threshold, so one Member State's floor cannot be assumed to cover users elsewhere (Guidelines 05/2020, para. 131).
- A proportionate, low-risk age check (asking a new user to state a birth year, or confirm they are not a minor) is enough where the processing risk is low; a verification step must not itself demand excessive personal data (Guidelines 05/2020, paras. 135, 145).
- Once a child reaches the age of digital consent, tell them they may confirm, modify or withdraw the consent a parent or guardian gave or authorised while they were younger (Guidelines 05/2020, paras. 147, 149; Art. 7(3)).
- Art. 8 does not settle whether a minor can validly conclude the underlying contract; that question runs on each Member State's general contract law (Art. 8(3); Guidelines 05/2020, para. 151).

### Special categories of data (Art. 9)

- Processing of data revealing racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs, trade union membership, genetic data, biometric data used to identify a person, health data, or data on sex life or sexual orientation is prohibited by default (Art. 9(1)).
- Lift the prohibition only on one of ten named grounds: explicit consent (unless a law bars lifting it that way); employment, social-security or social-protection law necessity under an authorising law or collective agreement with safeguards; vital interest where the data subject cannot consent; a not-for-profit body's own members' data under safeguards; data the data subject manifestly made public; legal claims or a court acting judicially; substantial public interest under a proportionate law with safeguards; preventive or occupational medicine, working-capacity assessment, medical diagnosis, health or social care, or managing health or social-care systems, under a law or a contract with a health professional and subject to Art. 9(3) secrecy; public-health reasons under a safeguarded law; or archiving, research or statistics under a proportionate, safeguarded law (Art. 9(2)(a) to (j)).
- Name the exact ground relied on; a blanket "we may process sensitive data as needed" has no basis. Where the ground is explicit consent, obtain it separately from any general consent for the same relationship (Art. 9(2)(a)).
- Meeting an Art. 9(2) ground is a separate, cumulative requirement alongside the Art. 6(1) basis for the same processing, not a substitute for it; a controller relying on legitimate interest for special-category data must independently satisfy both Art. 9(2) and the full three-step Art. 6(1)(f) test (Guidelines 1/2024, para. 40 footnote 47). Contract necessity under Art. 6(1)(b) is never itself an Art. 9(2) ground (Guidelines 2/2019, para. 21).
- Criminal-conviction and offence data may be processed only under official-authority control, or under a Union or Member State law with appropriate safeguards; only an official authority may keep a comprehensive register of convictions (Art. 10).
- Where identification is no longer needed for the processing purpose, there is no duty to keep, obtain or process extra information just to comply with the Regulation; where the controller genuinely cannot identify the data subject, it says so where possible, and the access-through-portability rights (Art. 15 to 20) do not apply unless the data subject supplies identifying information to exercise them (Art. 11).

### Contract necessity (Art. 6(1)(b); Guidelines 2/2019)

- Show, case by case: a valid contract with the data subject exists under the applicable national contract law, and the specific processing is objectively necessary to perform it; if either element is missing, find a different basis (Guidelines 2/2019, para. 26).
- Necessity carries its own meaning in EU law, not whatever the contract text happens to permit; mentioning or listing a processing activity in the contract, even in its small print, does not by itself make it necessary: "the fact that some processing is covered by a contract does not automatically mean that the processing is necessary for its performance" (Guidelines 2/2019, para. 28).
- Test necessity against the contract's own rationale, its substance and fundamental object, using four guiding questions: the nature and distinguishing features of the service; the contract's exact rationale; its essential elements; and the mutual, reasonable expectations of an ordinary user and the controller, given how the service is promoted (Guidelines 2/2019, paras. 29, 32, 33).
- Where a contract bundles several separable services, assess necessity per service against what the data subject actually requested, not against the bundle; processing that serves only the controller's wider business model, rather than the specific requested service, falls outside Art. 6(1)(b), though consent or legitimate interest may still apply on their own terms (Guidelines 2/2019, paras. 36, 37).
- Four activities are generally outside Art. 6(1)(b): service improvement and new-feature analytics; fraud-prevention monitoring and profiling (legitimate interest or legal obligation fit better); behavioural advertising and profile-building from clickstream or purchase data, even where the advertising indirectly funds the service; and content personalisation whose purpose is to increase engagement rather than deliver the contracted service itself (Guidelines 2/2019, paras. 48 to 53, 56, 57). Prior consent is still owed under ePrivacy rules for the cookies that enable behavioural advertising (Guidelines 2/2019, para. 55).
- Foreseeable incident-handling within a normal contractual relationship, such as a payment reminder or correcting a delivery error, can still rest on Art. 6(1)(b); so can storing data for a defined post-performance period to cover a contractual warranty (Guidelines 2/2019, paras. 38, 39).
- At termination, stop Art. 6(1)(b) processing tied to the contract unless it serves winding-up (returns, final payment) or a separately identified, previously disclosed retention basis (a legal obligation under Art. 17(3)(b), or legal claims under Art. 17(3)(e)); swapping to a new basis only once the original one has lapsed is treated as unfair to the data subject's trust (Guidelines 2/2019, paras. 41 to 44).
- The second limb, pre-contractual steps, covers only processing that answers the data subject's own request toward a possible contract; it never covers unsolicited marketing or processing the controller or a third party initiates on its own (Guidelines 2/2019, paras. 45 to 47).

### Legitimate interest (Art. 6(1)(f); Guidelines 1/2024)

- Run and document all three conditions before processing starts, with the DPO involved where one is designated: a legitimate interest is pursued; the processing is necessary for it; and the data subject's interests or fundamental rights and freedoms do not take precedence (Art. 6(1)(f); Guidelines 1/2024, paras. 6, 12).
- An interest is legitimate only where it is lawful, clearly and precisely articulated (not a vague "for the greater good"), and real and present rather than speculative; the interest should relate to the controller's own actual activity, though a third party's interest (pursuing a legal claim, transparency disclosure not otherwise required) can independently qualify on the same three criteria (Guidelines 1/2024, paras. 17 to 23).
- Necessity asks whether the interest could be achieved just as effectively by a reasonably available, less intrusive means; if so, the processing is not necessary, and usefulness alone never substitutes for necessity (Guidelines 1/2024, para. 29).
- Balance, case by case: the data subject's interests and fundamental rights and freedoms; the impact of the processing, covering data sensitivity (special-category or criminal data weighs heavily), context (scale, the controller's relative position, data combination, accessibility, the data subject's vulnerability) and further consequences (discrimination, financial loss, exclusion, a chilling effect); and the data subject's reasonable expectations given the relationship, the place and context of collection, and the nature of the service (Guidelines 1/2024, paras. 32, 40 to 54).
- Measures the GDPR already legally requires (information duties, security, minimisation, honouring rights) do not count again as mitigating measures in the balance; only a safeguard that genuinely goes beyond those legal duties counts, and the balance must be redone once a mitigation is added (Guidelines 1/2024, paras. 34, 56 to 58). The controller carries the burden of showing the balance was properly done and favours the processing (Guidelines 1/2024, para. 59).
- Where the balancing assessment itself identifies high risk to the data subject, follow it up with a Data Protection Impact Assessment under Art. 35 (Guidelines 1/2024, para. 49).
- Name the specific legitimate interest pursued, and the Art. 6(1)(f) basis itself, under the ordinary transparency duties (Art. 12 to 14); tell the data subject that further balancing-test detail is available on request (Guidelines 1/2024, paras. 67, 68).
- Processing involving a child needs a recalibrated balance drawing on the Charter's best-interests standard; a controller's commercial interest will often be outweighed by a child's interests, and extensive profiling or targeted advertising aimed at children will generally fail the balance unless the controller can show no negative effect on the child (Guidelines 1/2024, paras. 92 to 96).
- Fraud prevention needs a specific, substantial type of fraud and data genuinely needed for it, assessed under storage limitation for retention; a generic "combating fraud" statement fails the transparency and documentation duty, and fraud processing that is itself legally required rests on legal obligation, not legitimate interest (Guidelines 1/2024, paras. 104 to 107).
- Direct marketing may rest on legitimate interest only where no stricter rule requires consent instead; the ePrivacy Directive requires the recipient's prior consent for unsolicited email, SMS or MMS marketing and for cookies or other terminal-equipment access used for it, precluding legitimate interest for those channels, except that an entity that lawfully obtained a customer's contact details selling them a product may market its own similar products to that customer without fresh consent, provided the customer was told of this at collection and on each message and can object clearly, easily and free of charge (Guidelines 1/2024, paras. 113 to 117).
- Intra-group administrative transfers and network or information security measures can rest on legitimate interest once the necessity and balancing tests are run for the specific purpose; group membership alone never substitutes for that assessment (Guidelines 1/2024, paras. 123, 126, 127).

### Objection and the right to withdraw (Art. 21; Art. 7(3))

- Where processing rests on public task or legitimate interest, the data subject may object at any time on grounds relating to their particular situation; the controller must stop unless it demonstrates compelling legitimate grounds overriding the data subject's interests, rights and freedoms, or the processing serves a legal claim (Art. 21(1)). That bar is materially higher than the original legitimate-interest finding: it needs grounds essential to the controller, not mere business benefit (Guidelines 1/2024, paras. 72 to 74).
- Where processing is for direct marketing, the objection is unconditional: no reason is needed, and no balancing or override is possible; the controller must always stop, and must bring this right explicitly to the data subject's attention, clearly and separately from other information, at the latest at first contact (Art. 21(2) to (4); Guidelines 1/2024, para. 122).
- Where consent is withdrawn, stop the processing it covered; if no other basis justifies continued retention, delete the data (Art. 7(3); Guidelines 05/2020, paras. 117, 119).

## What the documents must not say

- No clause may rely on "we process your data as permitted by law" without naming the Art. 6(1) point it relies on for each purpose.
- No consent clause may be bundled invisibly into a general terms-acceptance checkbox, or use a pre-ticked box, silence, inactivity, or scrolling and swiping as the indication of agreement (Art. 7(2); Recital 32; Guidelines 05/2020, paras. 79, 81, 86).
- No document may make contract performance conditional on consent to processing that is not necessary for that contract and call the resulting consent freely given (Art. 7(4); Recital 43).
- No clause may describe processing as "necessary for the performance of the contract" where it only serves service improvement, fraud-prevention profiling, behavioural advertising, or engagement-driven personalisation that is not integral to the contracted service itself (Guidelines 2/2019, paras. 48 to 53, 56, 57).
- No clause may state a legitimate interest as a vague purpose ("improving services", "for the greater good") without a concrete, present activity behind it, or invoke legitimate interest for a public authority's own task-performance processing (Guidelines 1/2024, paras. 17, 98, 99).
- No privacy notice for a free, advertising-funded online service may base personalised advertising built from the user's behaviour and interests on legitimate interest: a user of such a service cannot reasonably expect the operator to process their personal data for personalised advertising without consent, so the basis is consent under Art. 6(1)(a) (EDPB Guidelines 1/2024, Example 5). Plain contextual advertising that uses no profile is a separate purpose with its own basis.
- No clause addressed to children may self-certify age with no verification step, or rely on legitimate interest for extensive profiling or targeted advertising aimed at children without being able to show no negative effect on the child (Art. 8(2); Guidelines 1/2024, para. 95).
- No clause authorising special-category processing may rely on a blanket "we may process sensitive data as needed"; the exact Art. 9(2) ground must be named, and an explicit-consent ground must be obtained separately from general consent (Art. 9(2)(a)).
- No document may condition withdrawal of consent on a harder channel than the one used to give it, or downgrade the service once a non-necessary processing purpose's consent is withdrawn (Guidelines 05/2020, paras. 49 (example), 114, 115).
- No clause may tell the data subject a processing activity rests on consent while the controller in fact relies, or later comes to rely, on a different basis for it, and no clause may retroactively substitute legitimate interest once a consent problem surfaces (Guidelines 05/2020, paras. 122, 123).
- A consent clause stating only that the data subject "has been informed of, and has consented to" a specific collection does not, standing alone, prove valid consent; it fails wherever any one of three things is true: the box was ticked by the controller's own staff before signing, with no evidence the clause was actually read; the contract terms could mislead the data subject into thinking the contract cannot be concluded without that consent, even though the processing is not in fact necessary for it; or refusing is made harder than accepting, for instance by requiring a separate signed refusal form (Case C-61/19, Orange Romania, para. 52 and operative part).
- No sign-up flow or paper contract may impose any extra step, form or friction on a data subject who wants to refuse or withdraw consent; the burden of acting runs only toward establishing consent, never toward establishing refusal (Case C-61/19, para. 51).

## Product behaviour it assumes

- The consent flow for every distinct processing purpose has its own control, not one checkbox covering several purposes; granting consent and accepting contract terms are two different user actions, never the same click or signature.
- Withdrawal of consent is reachable through the same interface used to give it, at no cost and with no feature loss beyond what the withdrawn processing itself enabled, and triggers deletion of data that has no other basis.
- An age gate, or a parental-consent verification step, sits in front of any information-society-service signup that is or could be offered directly to a child, calibrated to the Member State floor that applies to each user, never a single hard-coded "16" if the service reaches a state with a lower floor.
- A documented legitimate-interest assessment, including the necessity and balancing analysis, exists before that basis is relied on for a given purpose, and is kept current if the processing changes; the summary is available to a data subject on request.
- A direct-marketing opt-out and a general Art. 21(1) objection control are both present, distinguishable from each other, reachable at any time without login friction, and the marketing one needs no justification from the user and no internal override.
- Records exist of which lawful basis was disclosed for which purpose, and of when and how each consent was obtained, since the burden of proof for all of this sits with the controller, not the data subject.

## Prescribed wording

No fixed-label wording is prescribed by the Art. 6 to 11 provisions or by the EDPB guidelines or the Orange Romania judgment covered in this file; Art. 4(11) and Art. 7(2) and (4) set conditions for what a consent request must achieve, not a fixed phrase to reproduce. Where a document needs a specific affirmative-consent phrase, "I, hereby, consent to the processing of my data" or an equivalent plainly worded first-person statement meets the clear-affirmative-action standard; an acknowledgment-style phrase such as "I know that..." does not (Guidelines 05/2020, para. 67 footnote 38, para. 96 example 17).

## Open points to raise

- Whether the retrieved text of EDPB Guidelines 1/2024 is the EDPB's final-adopted version or the public-consultation draft; the document's own running header reads "Adopted - version for public consultation" on every page, and this could not be resolved from the text or an official landing page.
- For a service reaching users in more than one Member State, which national floor between 13 and 16 applies, and whether that state's law looks to the controller's main establishment or the data subject's residence to pick it; this file states only the Art. 8(1) band, not any state's chosen figure.
- Whether a given special-category or criminal-data processing activity truly needs the Art. 9(2) ground stated, separately from whichever Art. 6(1) basis also applies to it, since the two assessments do not substitute for each other.
- Whether a legitimate-interest assessment has in fact been run and documented for each purpose this file would let rest on Art. 6(1)(f), since the file states the method, not a finished conclusion for any specific product feature.
