# Unfair Commercial Practices Directive (2005/29/EC)

Citation: Directive 2005/29/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council of 11 May 2005 concerning unfair business-to-consumer commercial practices in the internal market and amending Council Directive 84/450/EEC, Directives 97/7/EC, 98/27/EC and 2002/65/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council and Regulation (EC) No 2006/2004, as amended by Directive (EU) 2019/2161 and Directive (EU) 2024/825
Version: consolidated 2026-09-27 (CELEX 02005L0029-20260927)
Official text: https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:02005L0029-20260927
Law as of: 2026-10-20

This Directive governs commercial practices, not a contract's validity, formation or effect (Art. 3(2)). Use it to screen what marketing copy, a checkout flow or a terms document says and does, never as the source of the contract's substantive terms. Germany's transposition sits in the Gesetz gegen den unlauteren Wettbewerb and is outside this file; for Germany, read the German UWG reference alongside this one once it exists.

## When it applies

- The Directive reaches unfair business-to-consumer commercial practices before, during and after a transaction relating to a product, where "product" means any good or service, including immovable property, digital service and digital content, plus rights and obligations (Art. 3(1); Art. 2(c)).
- It is without prejudice to contract law generally, to Union or national rules on product health and safety, to the rules on court jurisdiction, and to the conditions of establishment, authorisation regimes or deontological codes of regulated professions (Art. 3(2), (3), (7), (8)).
- For financial services (as defined in Directive 2002/65/EC) and immovable property, member states may impose requirements more restrictive or prescriptive than this Directive; this is the one minimum-harmonisation field in an otherwise full-harmonisation instrument, so do not assume this Directive alone sets the ceiling for those two sectors, and check the relevant national transposition (Art. 3(9)).
- Member states may add proportionate, non-discriminatory, consumer-protection-justified rules for aggressive or misleading practices tied to unsolicited home visits or trader-organised sales excursions, and must notify the Commission of such rules (Art. 3(5), (6)).
- "Trader" reaches any natural or legal person acting for purposes relating to their trade, business, craft or profession, and anyone acting in the trader's name or on their behalf; it includes non-profits and public authorities once they carry out a commercial activity, such as selling ethically labelled products or discounted tickets. An apparent private seller can still be a trader: the test looks at profit motive, transaction frequency and volume, turnover, VAT status, whether the sale is organised, and expertise asymmetry against the buyer, weighed together rather than on any single factor (Art. 2(b)).
- A single incident affecting one consumer is enough to trigger the Directive; neither the commercial-practice definition nor the misleading-action or misleading-omission tests need recurrence or multiple affected consumers (Art. 2(d)).
- "Transactional decision" reaches far more than the purchase decision itself: entering a shop, clicking through a website, agreeing to a sales presentation, withdrawing from a contract, and switching provider all count, whether the consumer decides to act or to refrain from acting (Art. 2(k)).
- The benchmark reader is the average consumer, reasonably well informed, observant and circumspect, not the most credulous conceivable reader; where a practice foreseeably and specifically distorts the behaviour only of a group vulnerable by age, infirmity or credulity, review and draft against the average member of that group instead (Art. 5(3)).
- Where a practice is instead deliberately directed at a particular group of consumers, the benchmark is the average member of that targeted group, regardless of whether the group is itself vulnerable; a practice aimed at a defined audience is reviewed against that audience's own likely understanding, not the general average consumer (Art. 5(2)(b)).
- Where another EU-law provision has EU-law status, regulates a specific aspect of a commercial practice, and conflicts with this Directive on that aspect, the other provision prevails for that aspect only; this Directive remains the fallback for every aspect the sector rule leaves open, for example aggressive behaviour around an otherwise sector-compliant practice (Art. 3(4)).
- An information requirement that other EU law imposes on commercial communications, advertising or marketing is deemed material for this Directive's misleading-omission test; satisfying the Consumer Rights Directive's pre-contractual information duties normally also satisfies this Directive's invitation-to-purchase list, but this Directive still governs how that information is presented, so a technically complete disclosure framed misleadingly stays exposed (Art. 7(4), (5)).
- A reseller's trade-in, buyback or part-exchange flow, and any paid or free valuation the trader offers in connection with a sale to that same consumer, fall inside this Directive as part of the trader's own business-to-consumer sale; a trader simply buying stock from a consumer with no link to a sale or service the trader supplies to that consumer sits outside the commercial-practice definition.

## What the documents must say

### The unfairness test, run in order

- First check whether the practice matches one of the 31 per se points in Annex I: a match is unfair in all circumstances, with no professional-diligence or material-distortion test needed (Art. 5(5)).
- If no Annex I point matches, check misleading actions (Art. 6) and misleading omissions (Art. 7); then aggressive practices (Art. 8, 9). Only a practice clearing all three specific tests falls back to the general clause: contrary to professional diligence and materially distorting, or likely to materially distort, the average consumer's economic behaviour (Art. 5(2), (4)).
- The general clause is a free-standing safety net, not an extra hurdle layered on top of Articles 6 to 9 or Annex I: a practice already found misleading under Article 6 need not separately be shown to breach professional diligence.

### Misleading actions (Art. 6)

- A practice is a misleading action if it contains false information, or, even where every individual statement is factually correct, deceives or is likely to deceive the average consumer through its overall presentation, on: the product's existence or nature; its main characteristics, including availability, benefits, risks, composition, environmental or social characteristics, circularity aspects such as durability, reparability or recyclability, after-sale assistance and complaint handling, and test results; the trader's commitments and sales-process motives, including sponsorship claims; the price or pricing method; the need for a service, part, replacement or repair; the trader's identity, qualifications, status and affiliations; and the consumer's legal rights, including replacement or reimbursement rights under Directive 1999/44/EC (Art. 6(1)(a) to (g)).
- Review marketing copy for the impression it creates, not merely for whether each sentence is true: factually correct statements can still mislead through selective emphasis, layout or juxtaposition, and a correction notice buried below the fold or in a linked document does not neutralise a misleading headline impression.
- A main-characteristics claim must be accurate and substantiated on demand: a claimed storage capacity, interest rate, language support or resale status that does not match reality has been enforced as a misleading action on this ground, and an unsubstantiated "up to" benefit claim can be misleading on the same basis or an omission under the invitation-to-purchase list below.
- A reference price (crossed-out, *recommended retail*, or otherwise) must state, immediately and as prominently as the price itself, whether it is the trader's own genuine prior price or a third-party comparison, and must not be set at an unrealistic level to inflate the apparent discount; splitting a total price into components and highlighting only one in a way likely to create a false impression of a favourable price is the same problem in different form.
- A checkout that adds charges progressively through the purchase flow rather than disclosing them in the headline invitation-to-purchase price (drip pricing) carries the same exposure as splitting a total price into components: the unavoidable total belongs in the first price shown, not assembled screen by screen (Art. 6(1)(d); Art. 7(4)(c)).
- Also a misleading action: confusing comparative marketing or branding, invoice design or colour schemes that risk confusion with a specific competitor's marks or trade dress; non-compliance with a firm, verifiable code-of-conduct commitment the trader has publicly indicated it is bound by (the code need not be legally binding for this to apply; the trader's claimed compliance is the promise at issue); marketing a good in one member state as identical to the same good marketed elsewhere while its composition or characteristics differ significantly, without a legitimate and objective justification; a future environmental-performance claim made without a clear, objective, publicly available, verifiable and independently audited implementation plan; and advertising a consumer benefit that is irrelevant to, and not connected with, any actual feature of that specific product or business (Art. 6(2)(a) to (e)).
- Where a product recipe or formulation genuinely varies by market for a legitimate and objective reason (a national legal requirement, raw-material availability, a voluntary reformulation policy), the trader should still actively tell consumers about the difference, preferably through a channel beyond the label itself, such as a linked product page or point-of-sale notice; the label-only route is not automatically sufficient even where a justification exists.
- A code-of-conduct or trust-mark claim is a promise the trader must keep: before displaying a code logo or "member of" claim, confirm the code's specific, checkable obligations and current compliance with them, rather than membership status alone.

### Misleading omissions and the invitation-to-purchase list (Art. 7)

- A practice is a misleading omission if, in context, it omits material information the average consumer needs for an informed transactional decision, hides that information, gives it unclearly, ambiguously or too late, or fails to flag the practice's commercial intent where not already apparent (Art. 7(1), (2)).
- Once copy names a product and its price (an "invitation to purchase"), give, unless already apparent from context: main characteristics fitting the medium and product; the trader's geographical address and identity, and that of any principal the trader acts for; the tax-inclusive price or pricing method, plus delivery, freight or postal charges or the fact such charges may apply; payment, delivery and performance arrangements that depart from professional diligence; the existence of any withdrawal or cancellation right; and, for a product on an online marketplace, whether the third-party seller is a trader, per that seller's own declaration to the marketplace (Art. 7(4)(a) to (f)).
- A space- or time-limited medium (a short ad, a social post, a radio spot) does not excuse omitting material information outright: the medium's limits and any other steps the trader took to make the information available are weighed together, so a documented alternative-disclosure path (a linked full terms page that itself carries the Art. 7(4) list, a durable-medium follow-up) is the relevant mitigation, silence is not.
- Completeness on the Art. 7(4) list does not immunise the whole claim: the list sets a floor for what counts as material in an invitation to purchase, not a safe harbour against the general misleading-action or misleading-presentation tests.
- Where a search facility lets consumers query products from different traders or sellers by keyword or similar input, give general information on the main ranking parameters and their relative importance, in a specific section directly and easily accessible from the results page itself, not buried in a general terms document; this duty does not reach an online search engine as defined in Regulation (EU) 2019/1150 (Art. 7(4a)).
- Where the trader shows consumer reviews, disclose whether and how it checks that published reviews come from consumers who actually used or purchased the product; a generic disclaimer that reviews may be unverified does not satisfy this, the duty is to state whether and how verification happens (Art. 7(6)).
- A review feature that makes reviews available but suppresses negative ones without valid reason misrepresents the review set shown to consumers and risks a misleading omission on top of the Annex I point 23c exposure for fabricated or commissioned reviews (Art. 7; Annex I point 23c).
- Where the trader runs a comparison service covering environmental, social or circularity characteristics, disclose the comparison method, the products and suppliers compared, and how the comparison is kept current (Art. 7(7)).
- A multi-language market needs every required disclosure, including the standard terms and conditions themselves, translated into each targeted member state's language, rather than just the marketing copy; giving the headline offer in the local language while leaving other required material information only in a different language can itself be a misleading omission.
- A free-trial or subscription flow must disclose, before the consumer commits and in the same place as the headline offer rather than a later screen or a separate document: that signing up creates a recurring payment obligation; the amount of both the one-off and the recurring charge; the trader's identity and address; and the trigger date or condition for the paid period to start.
- Never describe a product as "gratis", "free", "without charge" or similar where the consumer must pay anything beyond the unavoidable cost of responding to the practice and of collecting or paying for delivery (Annex I point 20).

### Aggressive practices (Art. 8, 9)

- A practice is aggressive if, in context, by harassment, coercion (including physical force) or undue influence, it significantly impairs or is likely to significantly impair the average consumer's freedom of choice or conduct regarding the product, causing or likely to cause an otherwise-unwanted transactional decision (Art. 8).
- The factors weighed are: timing, location, nature or persistence of the practice; threatening or abusive language or behaviour; the trader's exploitation of a specific misfortune or circumstance grave enough to impair the consumer's judgement, of which the trader is aware; any onerous or disproportionate non-contractual barrier the trader imposes on a consumer trying to exercise contract rights, including terminating the contract or switching product or trader; and any threat to take action that cannot legally be taken (Art. 9(a) to (e)).
- Audit a cancellation or unsubscribe flow against the sign-up flow for asymmetric friction: an extra verification step, a hidden channel, a mandatory retention call, or an enforced delay that a consumer must clear only when leaving risks an Article 9(d) finding, and a termination process made deliberately burdensome enough to trap consumers in automatic renewal has been separately enforced on this ground. A friction point kept for a genuine, proportionate reason tied to an actual contractual obligation is not automatically caught; document that reason if the friction point stays.
- A cancellation flow that uses emotional messaging to make the consumer reconsider, without a reasoned, substantive change in terms (loss-framed reminders such as reminding the consumer what they will lose, or prompting them to feel guilty for leaving) risks undue influence under Article 8, alongside any Article 9(d) exposure from friction added at the same step.
- Never include a clause or process step threatening a consequence the trader cannot legally impose, such as legally unsupported debt-collection or blacklisting threats, or directing a consumer toward a court that has no jurisdiction over an unfiled claim (Art. 9(e)).

### The Annex I per se list

Annex I lists 31 practices Article 5(5) deems unfair in all circumstances: no professional-diligence test, no material-distortion test, and no actual-consumer-harm evidence is needed once the fact pattern matches. The same single list applies across every member state and can only change by revising the Directive itself.

Misleading points, in brief: false code-of-conduct, trust-mark, quality-mark, endorsement or public-authority-authorisation claims, including a sustainability label not based on a certification scheme or not established by a public authority (points 1, 2, 2a, 3, 4); unsubstantiated generic environmental claims, whole-product or whole-business claims that in fact cover only one aspect, and greenhouse-gas-offsetting claims presented as giving the product itself a neutral, reduced or positive climate impact (points 4a, 4b, 4c); bait advertising (inviting purchase at a price the trader cannot reasonably supply in that quantity and time) and bait-and-switch (inviting purchase, then refusing to show, take orders for or deliver the item, or showing a defective sample, to push a different product) (points 5, 6); false limited-availability or limited-terms urgency claims (point 7); promising after-sales service in the pre-transaction language, then providing it only in another language without clear prior disclosure (point 8); falsely claiming a product can legally be sold when it cannot, and presenting a statutory consumer right or a legally mandated product requirement as a distinctive feature of the trader's own offer (points 9, 10, 10a); undisclosed paid editorial promotion and undisclosed paid or payment-driven search-result ranking (points 11, 11a); materially inaccurate personal-security-risk claims tied to not buying, and passing a product off as another manufacturer's (points 12, 13); operating a pyramid scheme funded mainly by new-participant intake rather than product sales (point 14); false cessation-of-trade, gambling-assistance or illness-cure claims (points 15, 16, 17); passing off materially inaccurate market-condition or availability information to induce a worse-than-market deal (point 18); advertising a prize promotion without awarding the described prizes or a reasonable equivalent (point 19); calling something "gratis", "free" or "without charge" where the consumer must pay anything beyond the unavoidable cost of responding and of collecting or paying for delivery (point 20); marketing material styled as an invoice that falsely implies the consumer already ordered the product (point 21); falsely claiming or implying the trader is a consumer, or not acting for trade, business, craft or professional purposes (point 22); creating the false impression that after-sales service exists in a member state other than the one of sale (point 23); reselling event tickets acquired by automated means that circumvented a purchase-quantity limit or other applicable purchase rule (point 23a); claiming reviews come from consumers who actually used or purchased the product without reasonable, proportionate verification steps, and submitting, commissioning or misrepresenting reviews or social endorsements to promote a product (points 23b, 23c); withholding that a software update will negatively affect digital-element goods, digital content or digital services, or presenting an update as necessary when it only adds functionality (points 23d, 23e); and, on durability and repair: communicating about a good with a durability-limiting feature despite having information on that feature and its effect, falsely claiming a certain durability under normal use, presenting a good as repairable when it is not, inducing early consumable replacement without technical need, and withholding or falsely denying that using non-original consumables, spare parts or accessories will impair functionality (points 23f to 23j).

Aggressive points, in brief: creating the impression the consumer cannot leave until a contract is formed (point 24); personal home visits that ignore a request to leave or not return, except where national law justifies it to enforce a contractual obligation (point 25); persistent, unwanted telephone, fax, email or other remote-media solicitation, with the same contractual-obligation exception (point 26); demanding irrelevant documents for an insurance claim, or systematically failing to respond to relevant correspondence, to dissuade a consumer from exercising contractual rights (point 27); a direct exhortation to children to buy, or to persuade an adult to buy, the advertised product (point 28); demanding payment, return or safekeeping for unsolicited supplied products, except a conforming substitute under Article 7(3) of Directive 97/7/EC (inertia selling) (point 29); telling a consumer their job or livelihood is at risk if they do not buy (point 30); and falsely creating the impression the consumer has won, will win, or will win by a particular act, a prize or equivalent benefit that does not exist, or that claiming it requires paying money or incurring a cost (point 31).

A terms document, marketing script or sales-flow design must avoid triggering any of these 31 points regardless of intent or actual consumer harm; there is no professional-diligence or materiality defence once a fact pattern matches a listed point.

### Environmental claims and greenwashing

- A generic environmental claim (terms such as *environmentally friendly*, *eco-friendly*, *green*, *sustainable*, *climate friendly*, *carbon friendly*, *energy efficient*, *biodegradable*, *biobased* or similar) is unfair under Annex I unless the trader can demonstrate recognised excellent environmental performance relevant to that specific claim; a claim's full specification shown in clear, prominent terms on the same medium (same ad spot, same packaging, same web page) takes it out of the generic-claim category, an extra click away does not (point 4a; Art. 2(s), (p)).
- A claim covering an entire product or an entire business, where the underlying fact actually concerns only one aspect or one unrepresentative activity, is unfair outright; a claim about the whole business stays permitted when accurate, verifiable and not overstated (point 4b).
- Recognised excellent environmental performance must be relevant to the whole claim, not a single part of it; a claim combining environmental and other characteristics ("conscious", "sustainable", "responsible") cannot rest solely on environmental-performance evidence for the environmental half.
- An irrelevant benefit claim (accurate but beside the point for this product or business, such as a bottled-water brand claiming to be gluten-free, or paper sheets claiming to be plastic-free) is a misleading action where it could suggest the product or business is more beneficial to consumers, the environment or society than comparable products or traders (Art. 6(2)(e)).
- Greenhouse-gas-offsetting claims cannot be marketed as giving the product itself a neutral, reduced or positive climate impact; advertising investment in offsetting or carbon-credit projects as such stays permitted, provided it is not presented as making the product itself neutral (point 4c).
- A future-performance environmental claim (carbon neutral by a stated date, net zero) needs clear, objective, publicly available, verifiable commitments set out in a detailed implementation plan with allocated resources, verified by a third-party expert independent of the trader, free of conflicts of interest, able to monitor progress against milestones, with the expert's regular findings made available to consumers; lacking this, the claim is reviewed case by case under Article 6(2)(d) rather than treated as per se unfair (Art. 6(2)(d)).
- A comparative environmental, social or circularity-aspect claim needs disclosure of the comparison method, the products and suppliers compared, and how the comparison is kept current (Art. 7(7)).
- A sustainability label may be displayed only where it rests on a certification scheme meeting minimum transparency and credibility conditions (open, non-discriminatory terms; requirements set with relevant experts and stakeholders; a documented non-compliance procedure; independent third-party monitoring) or is established by a public authority; a label meeting neither is unfair outright (point 2a; Art. 2(r)).
- Supporting detail for any environmental claim must sit next to the claim itself, not behind an extra click or on a separate page; a qualification one click away counts as no qualification.
- Keep evidence for any environmental claim from the moment the claim is first used, and be ready to produce it to an enforcement authority on request; where expert evidence raises real doubt about the environmental impact, stop using the claim rather than continue to rely on disputed substantiation (Art. 12).

### Durability, reparability and early obsolescence

- Never deliberately design a product with a limited lifespan so it prematurely becomes obsolete or non-functional after a set period or a predetermined intensity of use (planned obsolescence).
- Where the trader has information, as producer or from a reliable source such as the software developer or a competent authority, that a software update will negatively affect the functioning of goods with digital elements, digital content or a digital service, do not withhold that information; do not present an update as necessary to keep a product in conformity where it only adds functionality rather than providing security (points 23d, 23e).
- Once information on a durability-limiting feature and its effects is available to the trader, do not use commercial communications for the affected good without disclosing that feature; a non-producer trader is not required to go looking for information the producer has not made available, the duty attaches once reliable information reaches the trader (point 23f).
- Never falsely claim a good has a certain durability, in usage time or intensity, under normal conditions of use; an occasional manufacturing fault, as distinct from a false claim about expected durability, stays governed by the separate conformity rules in Directive (EU) 2019/771, not this Directive (point 23g).
- Never present a good as repairable when repair is in fact not possible (point 23h).
- Never induce early replacement or replenishment of a consumable (such as a printer cartridge) before it is technically necessary (point 23i).
- Never withhold that using a non-original consumable, spare part or accessory will impair the good's functionality, and never falsely claim that it will when it will not (point 23j).
- These prohibitions are distinct from ordinary low-quality materials or manufacturing choices that happen to reduce durability without a deliberately introduced limiting feature; that distinct question stays governed by the conformity rules in Directive (EU) 2019/771.

### Substantiation, enforcement and penalties

- In Article 11 proceedings, a court or authority can require the trader to substantiate any factual claim about a commercial practice; failing to furnish evidence, or furnishing insufficient evidence, lets the claim be treated as inaccurate outright (Art. 12(a), (b)).
- Member states must give courts or authorities power to order the cessation of an ongoing unfair practice, or the prohibition of an imminent one, without needing proof of actual loss, damage, intent or negligence, available under an accelerated procedure with interim or definitive effect (Art. 11(2)).
- Once a final cessation decision exists, member states may also give courts or authorities the power to require publication of that decision and a corrective statement (Art. 11(2), third subparagraph).
- Penalties must be effective, proportionate and dissuasive; where a fine is imposed for a widespread infringement under Article 21 of Regulation (EU) 2017/2394, the ceiling is at least 4% of the trader's annual turnover in the member state or states concerned, or at least EUR 2 million where turnover data is unavailable, for example for a newly established company (Art. 13(1), (3), (4)).
- Six non-exhaustive factors are weighed, where appropriate, in setting a penalty: the infringement's nature, gravity, scale and duration; mitigation the trader already undertook; the trader's previous infringements; financial benefit gained or loss avoided; penalties already imposed on the same trader for the same cross-border infringement in other member states, where known through the Regulation (EU) 2017/2394 cooperation mechanism; and any other aggravating or mitigating factor (Art. 13(2)(a) to (f)).
- A consumer harmed by an unfair practice has a right to proportionate and effective remedies: compensation for damage suffered, and, where relevant, a price reduction or contract termination, without prejudice to any other remedy available under Union or national law; member states set the conditions for applying these remedies and may weigh the practice's gravity, the damage suffered and other relevant circumstances (Art. 11a).
- This Directive does not exclude member states encouraging code-owner control of unfair practices, but recourse to a code-owner body is never deemed equivalent to foregoing judicial or administrative recourse under Article 11; a terms document should not overstate a code's binding force or treat a trade body's complaint process as a substitute for statutory enforcement exposure (Art. 10).

## Product behaviour it assumes

- Checkout and subscription flows disclose any recurring payment obligation, its amount and its trigger, in the same screen as the headline offer, before the consumer commits.
- Cancellation, downgrade and provider-switching flows carry no more steps, verification or delay than the sign-up flow for the same relationship.
- A review or ratings feature states, next to the reviews themselves, whether and how the platform checks that reviews come from consumers who actually used or purchased the product.
- A review feature does not remove or hide negative reviews without a stated, valid, content-neutral reason.
- A keyword-search or comparison feature carries a dedicated, directly accessible section disclosing the main ranking parameters and their relative importance, separate from the general terms document.
- Any paid or payment-influenced placement within search or comparison results is disclosed in context, next to the affected result, rather than only in a general statement elsewhere on the page.
- A marketplace listing states whether the third-party seller is a trader or a non-trader, based on that seller's own declaration, for every invitation to purchase.
- An environmental or sustainability claim on a product page, in packaging copy or in a company or product name carries its qualifying detail in the same place as the claim, and the trader keeps dated substantiation ready to produce on request.
- A durability-limiting feature, once known to the trader, is disclosed rather than left to a generic durability statement; a consumable-replacement prompt (such as an ink-level warning) triggers only once genuinely necessary, not early for commercial reasons.
- Records exist of what material information was shown to a given consumer, and when, sufficient to support the trader if a factual claim is later challenged and the trader must furnish evidence of its accuracy (Art. 12).

## Open points to raise

- Germany's transposition of this Directive (the relevant UWG provisions) has no reference file yet; do not assume this Directive's text applies directly to a German consumer without checking the UWG wording.
- The Tiketa judgment of the Court of Justice (C-536/20, 24 February 2022) bears on whether an intermediary acting for a trader is itself a trader, a question this file's trader definition touches; the references here do not cover that judgment's holding, so for the full citation and its application to the trader/intermediary point under Art. 2(b), read `references/eu/crd.md`.
- Whether a court's duty to review a contract term of its own motion (its ex officio review duty under the Unfair Contract Terms Directive) extends to a UCPD breach once Article 11a applies is a question the Guidance notes as open as of its own drafting; confirm current Court of Justice case law before relying on either answer.
- The exact scope of the Article 3(5) carve-out for unsolicited home visits and sales excursions is set by each member state's own notified rules, not by this Directive's text; check the relevant national transposition before drafting against a specific home-visit or excursion-sales flow.
