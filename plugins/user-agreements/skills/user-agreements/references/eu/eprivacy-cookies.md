# ePrivacy Directive: cookies and terminal equipment consent

Citation: Directive 2002/58/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council of 12 July 2002 concerning the processing of personal data and the protection of privacy in the electronic communications sector, as amended by Directive 2009/136/EC
Version: consolidated 2009-12-19
Official text: https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:02002L0058-20091219
Law as of: 2026-10-20

This file covers the directive-level consent rule for storing or reading information on a user's device (Art. 5(3)), the CJEU's binding reading of it in Planet49, and the European Data Protection Board's guidance on its technical scope and on cookie banner design. The Directive sets the rule; national transposition law sets the exact wording and enforcement that applies in each member state. Germany's transposition is covered in `references/de/tdddg.md`. For any other member state, the national transposition is an open point: name the country and flag it rather than drafting against this Directive's text as if it applied directly.

## When it applies

- The rule applies to the processing of personal data in connection with publicly available electronic communications services over public communications networks in the Community (Art. 3 Directive 2002/58/EC), and the Directive particularises the general data protection framework for that sector while also protecting the legitimate interests of legal-person subscribers (Art. 1(1), (2) Directive 2002/58/EC).
- Art. 5(3) Directive 2002/58/EC applies whenever three things are all true: the operation concerns "information" (a category wider than personal data: it covers a MAC address, a one-way-derived identifier, or a virus just as much as personal data, stored by anyone, for any reason); the operation touches the "terminal equipment" of a subscriber or user, meaning a device connected or merely connectable to the interface of a public communications network, whether or not actually connected at the time; and the operation is a storage (placing information on a physical medium inside the equipment, any duration, any amount) or a gaining of access (an entity instructs software already on the device to send information back) (EDPB Guidelines 2/2023, paras 6 to 39). Storage and access are independent: either one alone triggers the rule, and they need not happen in the same communication or by the same party (EDPB Guidelines 2/2023, para 30, 31).
- The rule reaches far beyond classic browser cookies. It covers a tracking pixel or tracking link (distributing it is storage, loading it is the access), a client-side API that later sends out locally processed results, an IP address that actually originates from the user's device (true for a static outbound IPv4 and generally for IPv6, not automatic under carrier-grade NAT), an IoT device's intermittent or cached reporting to a server, and a unique or persistent identifier derived from the user's own data on the device and shared across controllers (EDPB Guidelines 2/2023, paras 47 to 63). Information that is processed only inside local apps or the browser and never leaves the device is not a gaining of access, but the moment it or a derivation of it is sent off-device, the rule applies (EDPB Guidelines 2/2023, para 44).
- Two situations need no consent: technical storage or access for the sole purpose of carrying out the transmission of a communication over an electronic communications network, and storage or access strictly necessary for an information society service the subscriber or user explicitly requested, to provide that service (Art. 5(3) Directive 2002/58/EC). Applying either exemption is a case-by-case, member-state-transposition question; neither limb is analysed further here (EDPB Guidelines 2/2023, para 4, 56).
- Once a cookie or similar item is placed, any later processing of the data it collects, including whether consent is a valid legal basis for that processing, is judged under the GDPR, not the ePrivacy rule; the GDPR's One-Stop-Shop mechanism never reaches the placement step itself, and a website's EU-wide accessibility alone does not make it cross-border processing under Art. 4(23)(b) GDPR (EDPB Cookie Banner Taskforce report, paras 1 to 5).

## What the documents must say

- Tell the subscriber or user, before storing or reading anything on their device for a purpose that needs consent, the purposes of the processing, clearly and in full, so they can easily work out the consequences of consenting (Art. 5(3) Directive 2002/58/EC; Planet49, para 74).
- Where the stored items collect information for advertising or a comparable third-party-facing purpose, also state the operating duration of each cookie or stored item, and whether any third party can access it, and if so which categories (Planet49, paras 75, 76, 80, 81). The duration requirement follows because Art. 10 Directive 95/46/EC's "at least" wording makes its disclosure list a floor, not a ceiling, not because duration is named outright (Planet49, para 78).
- Give each purpose its own control and its own accompanying text; do not let a tick, or the absence of one, on one purpose's control stand in for a different purpose. An indication of wishes must be specific to the processing actually at issue and cannot be inferred from wishes shown for something else, such as clicking a button to enter a contest (Planet49, paras 58, 59).
- Make every non-essential control start unticked, off, or otherwise not selected when first shown, and require a distinct affirmative action from the user to turn each one on (Planet49, paras 52, 57, 62, 63).
- Offer a genuine reject or refuse option alongside accept, on the same layer that carries any consent button, with visual prominence comparable to the accept option. A link buried in a paragraph of text, or a reject option placed outside the banner's visual frame without enough visual support to draw an average user's attention to it, does not satisfy this (EDPB Cookie Banner Taskforce report, para 6, 8, 14).
- Provide an easily accessible, always-available way to withdraw consent, through the same interface and with the same degree of ease as the mechanism used to give it in the first place; this is not one obligation but three cumulative conditions: withdrawal must be possible, possible at any time, and as easy as giving consent was (EDPB Cookie Banner Taskforce report, para 32, 34; Art. 7(3) GDPR). A withdrawal control must visibly reflect the change at the interface once used, not appear to do nothing while the change is, or is not, registered in the background (EDPB Guidelines 03/2022, Section 3.3, Obstructing, Dead end).
- Keep any control meant to withdraw consent reachable from a centralised, logically ordered, easily accessible location, ideally a self-explanatory URL, rather than spread across pages not clearly linked to data protection (EDPB Guidelines 03/2022, Section 3.3).
- Where a cookie's processing also relies on Art. 6(1)(f) GDPR legitimate interest for something beyond the placement itself, for example personalised content profiling or personalised advertising, present any objection to that legitimate-interest processing on the same layer and with the same prominence as the cookie refusal, not buried in a deeper layer where a user could believe they must refuse twice (EDPB Cookie Banner Taskforce report, paras 20 to 22).
- Be able to show, through the interface itself and supporting records, that a given user read the information provided and gave free consent, since the burden of proving properly collected consent sits with the controller, not the user, even for a quick interaction such as accepting cookies (Art. 7(1) GDPR; EDPB Guidelines 03/2022, Section 3.3, "Use case 3a").

## What the documents must not say

- Do not label a cookie or a processing activity "essential" or "strictly necessary" unless it meets that standard; there is no stable universal list, because cookie behaviour changes regularly, so the burden sits on the website owner to maintain and justify its own list to a competent authority on request (EDPB Cookie Banner Taskforce report, paras 26 to 30).
- Do not rely on legitimate interest, under Art. 6(1)(f) GDPR or any other basis, as the legal ground for the placement or reading of cookies itself; that step can only be justified by consent or one of the two Art. 5(3) exemptions. Non-compliant placement taints any GDPR processing built on it (EDPB Cookie Banner Taskforce report, para 24).
- Do not offer accept with no corresponding reject or refuse option on any layer that carries a consent button; a majority of supervisory authorities treat this as an infringement, though a minority disagree because Art. 5(3) does not itself use the word "reject" (EDPB Cookie Banner Taskforce report, paras 6 to 8).
- Do not pre-tick any category box, toggle or checkbox for a purpose that needs consent; a pre-ticked control that the user must deselect to refuse never gives valid consent, under the pre-GDPR regime or under the GDPR alike, and it makes no difference whether the stored information is personal data (Planet49, paras 57, 63, 68 to 71; EDPB Cookie Banner Taskforce report, para 10).
- Do not design a button's colour or contrast so that accept clearly dominates the available options, or so that an alternative option's text is unreadable to virtually any user against its background; there is no fixed numeric standard, and each banner needs a case-by-case check, but a contrast pairing that makes text unreadable has been flagged as manifestly misleading (EDPB Cookie Banner Taskforce report, paras 15 to 19).
- Do not design a cookie banner in a way that gives users the impression they must consent to access the site's content, or that otherwise pushes them toward consenting, for example by not allowing continued browsing without cookies from the first layer (EDPB Cookie Banner Taskforce report, para 13).
- Do not use humour or wordplay, such as a recipe-style framing of the word "cookies," that distracts from the data-protection meaning of a consent request, and do not let the consent request blend into unrelated content (EDPB Guidelines 03/2022, Section 3.3, Skipping, Look over there).
- Do not give a consent toggle a colour and position that contradict each other, for example colouring the "on" position red and the "off" position green, since this leaves consent ambiguous rather than unambiguous (EDPB Guidelines 03/2022, Section 3.3, Left in the dark, Conflicting Information).
- Do not require more clicks, more navigation, or a less visible placement to withdraw consent than the original consent flow required (EDPB Cookie Banner Taskforce report, para 34, 35; EDPB Guidelines 03/2022, Section 3.3, Obstructing, Longer than necessary).
- Do not repeat a consent prompt a user already declined through a recurring pop-up that blocks ordinary use of the service, especially where the refuse option is less visually prominent than accept (EDPB Guidelines 03/2022, Section 3.3, Overloading, Continuous prompting).
- Do not condition the provision of an information society service on consent to storage or access that is not strictly necessary for that service and treat that as satisfying the exemption in Art. 5(3) Directive 2002/58/EC; the exemption only covers access strictly necessary to provide the requested service, and the question whether conditioning an unrelated benefit on consent meets the separate "freely given" test under GDPR Art. 4(11), 7(4) remains open, since the Court declined to decide it (Planet49, para 64; Art. 5(3) Directive 2002/58/EC).

## Product behaviour it assumes

- A cookie or tracking-technology inventory that covers every operation within Art. 5(3)'s scope, beyond items named "cookies": tracking pixels and links, client-side APIs that send locally processed results off-device, IP-based tracking, IoT reporting, and persistent identifiers derived from the user's own data, each checked against the consent-or-exemption analysis (EDPB Guidelines 2/2023, paras 47 to 63).
- A documented, maintained list of which cookies and similar items the site or app places, with the purpose of each, updated as cookie behaviour changes, available to show a competent authority on request (EDPB Cookie Banner Taskforce report, paras 27 to 30).
- A consent management mechanism with purpose-level granularity: one control per purpose, each starting off, with its own accompanying disclosure of purpose, duration, and third-party access (Planet49, paras 58, 59, 75 to 81).
- A reject or refuse control on the same layer and with comparable prominence to any accept control, and continued access to site content without requiring consent from the first layer (EDPB Cookie Banner Taskforce report, paras 6 to 14).
- A withdrawal mechanism reachable at any time, through the same interface and the same number of steps used to give consent, located at a centralised, clearly linked place (EDPB Cookie Banner Taskforce report, para 34, 35; EDPB Guidelines 03/2022, Section 3.3).
- Consent records sufficient to show, per user, that the information was presented and an affirmative action was taken, to meet the controller's burden of proof under Art. 7(1) GDPR (EDPB Guidelines 03/2022, Section 3.3, "Use case 3a").
- Separate presentation of any Art. 6(1)(f) legitimate-interest objection from the cookie consent/refusal controls, with the same first-layer visibility, where legitimate interest is used for further processing beyond the placement step (EDPB Cookie Banner Taskforce report, paras 20 to 22).

## Prescribed wording

None of the sources for this file prescribe exact banner, button, or toggle wording comparable to a statutory model label; consent here must be specific, informed, and freely given in substance, with the drafter choosing the words (Planet49, paras 58, 74; EDPB Guidelines 03/2022, Section 3.3). Where a document also quotes GDPR Art. 4(11), 6(1)(a), 7, or Directive 95/46/EC Art. 2(h) for the definition of consent itself, see `references/prescribed-wording.md` for any entries already drawn from those definitions under other targets.

## Dates

- Directive 2002/58/EC remains in force in its 2009-12-19 consolidated form; a proposed ePrivacy Regulation to replace it has not entered into force (no further date to report).
- Planet49 was decided 1 October 2019 (Case C-673/17, Grand Chamber).
- EDPB Guidelines 2/2023 on the technical scope of Art. 5(3) ePD were adopted for public consultation on 14 November 2023 (v1.0) and finalised on 7 October 2024 (v2.0).
- The EDPB Cookie Banner Taskforce report was adopted 17 January 2023, recording the outcome of thirteen taskforce meetings.
- EDPB Guidelines 03/2022 on deceptive design patterns reached version 2.0 on 14 February 2023, after public consultation on version 1.0 of 14 March 2022.

## Open points to raise

- Whether conditioning an unrelated benefit, such as entry to a contest or promotion, on consent to a purpose that is not strictly necessary to deliver it satisfies the GDPR "freely given" test is left open by Planet49; the Court was not asked and declined to decide it. Flag this rather than drafting as if either answer were settled (Planet49, para 64).
- No fixed numeric colour-or-contrast standard exists for banner buttons; each design needs its own case-by-case check against whether it visually makes accept dominant or makes an alternative option unreadable (EDPB Cookie Banner Taskforce report, paras 17 to 19).
- The specific withdrawal mechanism (icon, link, settings page) is not mandated; only that it be easily accessible, available at any time, and as easy as giving consent. Confirm the product's actual mechanism meets that standard rather than assuming a particular UI pattern is sufficient (EDPB Cookie Banner Taskforce report, para 32, 34, 35).
- Member-state transposition of the two Art. 5(3) exemptions, and of the "reject option" question on which authorities split, is not analysed here; check the relevant national law, starting with `references/de/tdddg.md` for Germany, before relying on the Directive text alone (EDPB Guidelines 2/2023, para 4, 56; EDPB Cookie Banner Taskforce report, para 8).
