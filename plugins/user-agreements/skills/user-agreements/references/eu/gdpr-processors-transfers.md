# Processors and international transfers under the GDPR

Citation: Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (General Data Protection Regulation), Articles 4, 24 to 30 and 44 to 50; Commission Implementing Decision (EU) 2021/915 of 4 June 2021 (controller-to-processor standard contractual clauses); Commission Implementing Decision (EU) 2021/914 of 4 June 2021 (standard contractual clauses for transfers to third countries); EDPB Guidelines 07/2020 on the concepts of controller and processor in the GDPR, version 2.1
Version: GDPR original text, no substantive consolidation; both Decisions as adopted 4 June 2021; Guidelines 07/2020 version 2.1 of 20 September 2022
Official text: https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32016R0679
Law as of: 2026-10-20

This file covers who counts as a controller, joint controller or processor; what a processor contract (a data processing agreement, DPA) must contain; and what makes a transfer of personal data to a country outside the EEA lawful. It draws on the GDPR articles themselves, the Commission's two standard contractual clauses (one for controller-processor contracts, one for third-country transfers), and the EDPB's guidance on the controller and processor concepts. The clause-drafting counterpart to this file is the data processing agreement template; see `templates/data-processing-agreement.md`.

## When it applies

- Applies whenever personal data (any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person) is processed by an entity acting for another entity's purposes, or is transferred to a country or international organisation outside the European Economic Area (Art. 4(1), (2); recitals to Art. 44).
- A processor is "a natural or legal person, public authority, agency or other body which processes personal data on behalf of the controller" (Art. 4(8)). Two conditions must both hold: the processor is a separate entity from the controller, and it processes on the controller's behalf, serving the controller's interest rather than its own; an employee or a department of the same legal entity as the controller is never a processor, since that processing is simply the controller's own (EDPB Guidelines 07/2020, para. 76 to 78, 80).
- A controller is "the natural or legal person, public authority, agency or other body which, alone or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of the processing of personal data" (Art. 4(7)). What makes an entity a controller is its actual decisive influence over why data are processed and over the essential means (what data, how long, who has access, whose data), never the label a contract gives it; a standardised, take-it-or-leave-it service contract does not by itself turn the customer into anything but controller for its own purposes, nor the provider into a controller, as long as the provider processes only for the customer's purposes (EDPB Guidelines 07/2020, para. 28 to 30, 39, 40).
- Where two or more entities jointly determine the purposes and means of the same processing, they are joint controllers (Art. 26(1)). Joint participation can be a common decision or converging decisions that complement each other, are each necessary for the processing to happen, and have a tangible impact on purposes or means; a shared benefit from the same processing is only an indication of a joint purpose, never decisive on its own, and genuine joint controllership still requires each party to also take part in deciding the means (EDPB Guidelines 07/2020, para. 54, 55, 59, 60, 62). A provider merely paid for a service, with no purpose of its own in the processing, is a processor, not a joint controller, even if it chooses some of the technical means (EDPB Guidelines 07/2020, para. 62, 68).
- A processor that goes beyond the controller's instructions and starts deciding its own purposes and means becomes a controller for that processing and is treated as one, without prejudice to Articles 82 to 84 GDPR (Art. 28(10)).
- The transfer rules of Chapter V apply to any transfer of personal data to a third country or an international organisation, including an onward transfer from that country or organisation to another one, and every provision of the Chapter must be applied so that the GDPR's level of protection is not undermined (Art. 44).

## What the documents must say

### Controller and processor obligations generally

- The controller must implement technical and organisational measures appropriate to the nature, scope, context, purposes and risk of the processing, able to demonstrate compliance with the Regulation, and reviewed and updated as needed (Art. 24(1), (2)).
- The controller must use only a processor giving sufficient guarantees to implement appropriate technical and organisational measures so that processing meets the Regulation's requirements and protects the data subject's rights (Art. 28(1)). This duty does not end once the contract is signed: the controller should keep checking those guarantees, including through audits and inspections, for as long as the engagement runs (EDPB Guidelines 07/2020, para. 94, 99).

### The processor contract (Art. 28(3))

- Processing by a processor must rest on a contract or other legal act under Union or Member State law, in writing (including electronic form), binding the processor toward the controller, and fixing: the subject matter and duration of the processing; its nature and purpose; the type of personal data and categories of data subjects; and the controller's obligations and rights (Art. 28(3), chapeau; Art. 28(9)). A contract that only repeats the statutory list, without saying concretely how each duty will be met and what security level the actual processing needs, does not satisfy Art. 28(3) (EDPB Guidelines 07/2020, para. 95, 112, 113). The absence of such a contract, where no other legal act covers it, is itself an infringement, and both controller and processor are responsible for making sure it exists (EDPB Guidelines 07/2020, para. 103).
- The contract must bind the processor to process personal data only on the controller's documented instructions, including instructions on transfers to a third country or an international organisation, unless a Union or Member State law requires otherwise; where such a law applies, the processor must tell the controller of that legal requirement before processing, unless the law forbids this on important public-interest grounds (Art. 28(3)(a)). Where the controller's instructions do not permit a transfer outside the EU, the processor may not route the data there by any means: not directly, not through a third-country sub-processor, and not through its own non-EU division (EDPB Guidelines 07/2020, para. 119, 120).
- The processor must immediately tell the controller if, in its view, an instruction infringes the GDPR or another Union or Member State data protection provision (Art. 28(3), closing sentence after point (h)).
- The contract must bind the processor to: keep authorised staff under a confidentiality commitment or an equivalent statutory duty (Art. 28(3)(b)); take all Art. 32 security measures (Art. 28(3)(c)); respect the sub-processor conditions of Art. 28(2) and (4) (Art. 28(3)(d)); assist the controller, by appropriate technical and organisational measures where possible, with answering data subject rights requests under Chapter III (Art. 28(3)(e)); assist the controller with Art. 32 to 36 compliance, given the nature of the processing and the information available to the processor (Art. 28(3)(f)); at the controller's choice, delete or return all personal data after the services end, and delete existing copies, unless Union or Member State law requires continued storage (Art. 28(3)(g)); and make available to the controller all information needed to show compliance with this Article, and allow for and contribute to audits, including inspections, by the controller or another auditor it mandates (Art. 28(3)(h)).
- The contract must say which party decides deletion versus return of data at the end of the engagement, the timescale and manner of deletion, and must let the controller change that choice before the services end (EDPB Guidelines 07/2020, para. 139 to 142).
- Contract stringency scales with risk: a low-risk engagement does not need the same protections as a high-risk one, but every one of the eight Art. 28(3) elements must still be covered whatever the risk level (EDPB Guidelines 07/2020, para. 113).
- The contract may rest, in whole or in part, on standard contractual clauses the Commission adopts under Art. 28(7) or a supervisory authority adopts under Art. 28(8), without either option being preferred over an individually negotiated contract (Art. 28(6); EDPB Guidelines 07/2020, para. 104 to 106).
- An agreement that embeds the Art. 28 clauses inside a broader contract, such as a service-level agreement, should have those clauses clearly identified as such, for example in a dedicated annex or schedule (EDPB Guidelines 07/2020, para. 103).
- A contract must not impose audit or inspection costs on the controller in a way that is clearly disproportionate or excessive so as to deter it from exercising its Art. 28(3)(h) rights in practice (EDPB Guidelines 07/2020, para. 145). A change the processor proposes to standard terms embedding the DPA must be directly notified to, and approved by, the controller; publishing a change on the processor's website is not enough (EDPB Guidelines 07/2020, para. 110).

### Sub-processors

- A processor must not engage another processor without the controller's prior specific or general written authorisation; under a general authorisation, the processor must tell the controller of any intended addition or replacement, giving the controller the chance to object (Art. 28(2)). The contract should fix the timeframe for the controller's approval or objection and what happens after an objection, for example whether and when the processing stops; under general authorisation, the processor's silence within the agreed timeframe can count as authorisation, while under specific authorisation each proposed sub-processor needs its own written approval (EDPB Guidelines 07/2020, para. 155, 157, 158).
- Where a processor engages a sub-processor for specific processing on the controller's behalf, the same data protection obligations set in the controller-processor contract must be imposed on the sub-processor by a further contract or legal act, including sufficient guarantees for appropriate technical and organisational measures; the first processor stays fully liable to the controller for the sub-processor's performance if the sub-processor fails to meet those obligations (Art. 28(4)). A sub-processor contract should not carry over obligations that cannot logically apply to the narrower task given to the sub-processor, since this only creates uncertainty (EDPB Guidelines 07/2020, para. 160).

### Breach, security and impact assessment duties that tie into the processor contract

- The controller and processor must implement security measures appropriate to the risk, given the state of the art and cost, covering as appropriate pseudonymisation and encryption, ongoing confidentiality, integrity, availability and resilience, timely restoration after an incident, and regular testing (Art. 32(1)(a) to (d)).
- On a personal data breach, the processor must notify the controller without undue delay after becoming aware of it (Art. 33(2)); the controller must then notify the competent supervisory authority without undue delay, where feasible within 72 hours of becoming aware, unless the breach is unlikely to risk rights and freedoms (Art. 33(1)). A DPA clause on breach notification should commit the processor to notify without undue delay, not a looser standard, since the controller's own 72-hour clock to the authority starts running from the controller becoming aware, and a slow processor notification can put that deadline at risk.
- Before processing likely to result in a high risk, the controller must carry out a data protection impact assessment (Art. 35(1)); the processor must assist the controller with this and, where a DPIA shows high residual risk, with the Art. 36 prior consultation of the supervisory authority, given the nature of the processing and the information available to the processor (Art. 28(3)(f)).

### International transfers: the three tiers

- Tier 1, adequacy: a transfer may happen without specific authorisation where the Commission has decided the destination country, territory, sector or international organisation ensures an adequate level of protection (Art. 45(1)). The adequacy assessment weighs the rule of law and human-rights record including public-authority access to data, the existence of an independent, effectively functioning supervisory authority there, and the destination's international commitments on data protection (Art. 45(2)(a) to (c)). Adequacy decisions are reviewed at least every four years and can be repealed, amended or suspended, without retroactive effect, if the destination no longer qualifies (Art. 45(3), (5)).
- Tier 2, appropriate safeguards (used absent an adequacy decision), needs no specific supervisory-authority authorisation when it takes the form of: a legally binding, enforceable instrument between public authorities or bodies; binding corporate rules under Art. 47; standard contractual clauses the Commission adopts, or a supervisory authority adopts with Commission approval; an approved code of conduct together with binding, enforceable third-country commitments; or an approved certification mechanism with the same (Art. 46(1), (2)(a) to (f)). Ad hoc contractual clauses, or administrative-arrangement provisions between public authorities with enforceable data subject rights, need the competent supervisory authority's prior authorisation through the Art. 63 consistency mechanism (Art. 46(3), (4)).
- Binding corporate rules are approved by the competent supervisory authority through the consistency mechanism, provided they are legally binding on and enforced by every group member and its staff, expressly confer enforceable data subject rights, and cover: group structure and contacts; the transfers and data covered, their type, purposes and the data subjects and destination countries affected; their binding force internally and externally; application of the core principles (purpose limitation, minimisation, storage limits, data quality, by-design and by-default protection, lawful basis, special-category processing, security, onward-transfer conditions); data subject rights and how to exercise them, including the right to lodge a complaint and obtain redress and compensation; the EU-established member's acceptance of liability for a non-EU member's breaches, escapable only by proving that member was not responsible for what caused the damage; how this information reaches data subjects beyond the Art. 13/14 notices; the compliance monitor's tasks, training and complaint handling; the complaint procedure; an internal verification mechanism including audits, reported to the monitor and the controlling undertaking's board and available to the supervisory authority on request; how rule changes are reported internally and to the supervisory authority; the cooperation mechanism with the supervisory authority; reporting of adverse third-country legal requirements; and staff training (Art. 47(1), (2)(a) to (n)).
- Tier 3, case-by-case derogations (used only where tiers 1 and 2 are both unavailable): explicit, informed consent to the specific transfer, after being told of the risks from the absence of an adequacy decision and safeguards; necessity for a contract between the data subject and the controller, or pre-contractual steps at the data subject's request; necessity for a contract concluded in the data subject's interest between the controller and a third party; important public-interest reasons recognised in Union or Member State law; establishing, exercising or defending legal claims; protecting the vital interests of the data subject or others where the data subject cannot give consent; or a transfer from a register that Union or Member State law makes open to public or legitimate-interest consultation, limited to what that law's own consultation conditions allow (Art. 49(1), first subparagraph, points (a) to (g); Art. 49(4)). A transfer under the register ground must never cover the whole register or whole categories of the data it holds, and may happen only at the request of, or to, a person with a demonstrated legitimate interest where the register is meant for such consultation (Art. 49(2)).
- Failing all three tiers, a non-repetitive transfer affecting a limited number of data subjects may still happen for the controller's compelling legitimate interests, where these are not overridden by the data subject's interests or rights, provided the controller has assessed all the circumstances of the transfer and, on that basis, put suitable safeguards in place; the controller must then tell the supervisory authority of the transfer, and tell the data subject, in addition to the Art. 13/14 information, of the transfer and the compelling interests relied on (Art. 49(1), second subparagraph). The controller must document that assessment and those safeguards in its Art. 30 records (Art. 49(6)).
- Points (a), (b) and (c) of Art. 49(1), first subparagraph (consent, and the two contract-based derogations), and the second-subparagraph compelling-legitimate-interest fallback, do not apply to activities public authorities carry out in exercising their public powers; the important-public-interest, legal-claims, vital-interests and public-register derogations (points (d) to (g)) remain available to a public authority, and Art. 45 and 46 carry no equivalent carve-out (Art. 49(3)).
- A third-country court judgment or administrative decision ordering a controller or processor to transfer or disclose personal data is recognisable or enforceable only if it rests on an international agreement in force, such as a mutual legal assistance treaty, between the requesting country and the Union or a Member State, without prejudice to the other transfer grounds in this Chapter (Art. 48).

### The two Commission standard contractual clauses: scope and relationship

- The Art. 28(7) controller-to-processor clauses (Commission Implementing Decision (EU) 2021/915) fulfil the contractual-content requirements of Art. 28(3) and (4); they are a different instrument from, and cannot substitute for, the Art. 46(2)(c) transfer clauses, even though the transfer clauses also satisfy Art. 28(3) and (4) for a transfer from a controller to a processor, or from a processor to a sub-processor, located outside the GDPR's territorial scope.
- The Art. 46(2)(c) transfer clauses (Commission Implementing Decision (EU) 2021/914) provide appropriate safeguards under Art. 46(1) and (2)(c) for a transfer by an exporter subject to the GDPR to an importer not subject to it, and extend to set out Art. 28(3) and (4) controller-processor rights and obligations for a transfer from a controller to a processor, or a processor to a sub-processor (Clause 1(a), (b) of Decision (EU) 2021/914).
- Neither clause set discharges the exporter's other GDPR obligations on its own: using them does not by itself meet Chapter V transfer duties beyond what the clauses themselves cover, and a controller using them must still tell data subjects, under Art. 13(1)(f) and 14(1)(f), that it intends to transfer their data to a third country, referencing the safeguard used and how to get a copy of it or find out where it is available (Clause 1(e), (f); Clause 2(b) of Decision (EU) 2021/914, Recital 4).
- Both clause sets must not be substantively modified beyond selecting the applicable option (the clauses themselves, or, for the transfer clauses, the Module) and completing the Annexes with required information; a party may fold either clause set into a broader contract, or add further clauses or safeguards, only if those additions do not directly or indirectly contradict the clauses or detract from data subjects' fundamental rights or freedoms (Clause 2(a) of each Decision). Where the clauses conflict with a related agreement between the parties, existing or later, the clauses prevail (Clause 4 of Decision (EU) 2021/915; Clause 5 of Decision (EU) 2021/914).

### The controller-processor clauses (Decision (EU) 2021/915): processor duties

- The processor must process data only on the controller's documented instructions, immediately tell the controller if an instruction infringes applicable data protection law, and process only for the purposes and duration stated in the Annex unless the controller gives further instructions (Clause 7.1, 7.2, 7.3).
- The processor must implement at least the Annex-stated technical and organisational measures, restrict data access to staff who need it, and keep them under a confidentiality commitment; where sensitive data (the special categories of Art. 9 GDPR, plus criminal-conviction and offence data) is processed, it must apply specific restrictions or extra safeguards (Clause 7.4, 7.5).
- The processor must deal promptly and adequately with the controller's inquiries, make available all information needed to show compliance, and permit and contribute to audits at reasonable intervals or on indications of non-compliance, including premises inspections with reasonable notice; both parties must make this information, including audit results, available to the competent supervisory authority on request (Clause 7.6).
- Sub-processor engagement needs either the controller's prior specific written authorisation per engagement, filed a set time in advance with enabling information, or its general written authorisation from an agreed list with written notice of changes a set time in advance so the controller can object; the sub-processor contract must impose, in substance, the same data protection obligations the processor bears; the processor stays fully responsible to the controller for the sub-processor's performance; and the processor must agree a third-party-beneficiary clause with the sub-processor so that, if the processor has factually disappeared, ceased to exist in law or become insolvent, the controller can terminate the sub-processor contract directly and instruct erasure or return of the data (Clause 7.7).
- Any transfer by the processor to a third country or international organisation must rest on documented controller instructions or a specific legal requirement, and must comply with Chapter V of the GDPR (Clause 7.8(a)).
- The processor must promptly notify the controller of any data subject request and must not respond itself unless the controller authorises it; it must assist the controller in answering such requests and with DPIAs, prior consultation, data accuracy and Art. 32 security, following the controller's instructions (Clause 8).
- On a personal data breach, the processor must cooperate with and assist the controller so the controller can meet its own Art. 33 and 34 obligations, and must notify the controller without undue delay of a breach concerning data the processor processes, with at least a description of the breach's nature, a contact point, and the likely consequences and measures taken or proposed (Clause 9).
- Following termination, the processor must, at the controller's choice, delete all personal data and certify the deletion, or return it and delete existing copies, unless Union or Member State law requires continued storage; until then it must keep complying with the clauses (Clause 10(d)).

### The transfer clauses (Decision (EU) 2021/914): choosing a Module and its core duties

- A contract relying on these clauses must identify which Module applies: One, controller to controller; Two, controller to processor; Three, processor to processor; Four, processor to controller (Clause 1(b); the Module structure of Clause 8). It must complete Annex I (parties, transfer description, competent supervisory authority), Annex II (technical and organisational measures) and, for Modules Two and Three under specific sub-processor authorisation, Annex III.
- Across every Module, the importer must apply purpose limitation (processing only for the transfer purpose in the Annex, absent further instructions or a listed exception), keep data accurate and current, retain it no longer than necessary, and implement appropriate security measures including, where relevant, encryption or pseudonymisation, with a breach-notification chain to the exporter (and, for Modules Three and Four, onward to the controller) that includes supervisory-authority and data-subject notification where the exporter's own GDPR duties require it (Clause 8, Modules One to Four).
- Onward transfer by the importer to a third party outside the EU is allowed only where that party is bound by the clauses, or the destination benefits from an adequacy decision, Art. 46 or 47 safeguards apply, a binding instrument with the same protection level is in place, or the transfer is necessary for legal claims or to protect vital interests; Module One alone also allows the data subject's explicit, informed consent to that specific onward transfer (Clause 8.7 (Module One), 8.8 (Modules Two and Three)).
- For Modules Two and Three, sub-processing needs the exporter's (Module Two) or the controller's, as relayed by the exporter (Module Three), prior specific or general written authorisation, under the same two-option structure (specific authorisation per engagement, or general authorisation from an agreed list with an objection window) as the controller-processor clauses; the sub-processor contract must impose, in substance, the same obligations, and the importer stays fully responsible for the sub-processor's performance (Clause 9).
- Clause 10 sets the data subject rights duty that falls on the importer (Module One) or that the importer must help the exporter (and, for Module Three, the controller) answer (Modules Two to Four); Clause 11 requires the importer to give data subjects a transparent, accessible complaints contact point in every Module, with an optional no-cost independent dispute resolution mechanism.
- Clause 12 fixes liability: each party is liable to the other for damage from a clause breach, and the importer (and, for Modules Two and Three, also the exporter) is liable to the data subject for material or non-material damage from a breach of the subject's third-party beneficiary rights, with joint and several liability and an internal claim-back right where more than one party is responsible.
- Clause 13 names the competent supervisory authority (the exporter's own, its EU representative's, or that of a Member State where affected data subjects are, depending on the exporter's situation), and the importer must submit to that authority's jurisdiction and comply with its measures.

### Local law, government access and termination under the transfer clauses

- Before relying on the clauses, the parties must warrant they have no reason to believe the destination country's laws and practices applicable to the importer's processing, including disclosure requirements or public-authority access measures, prevent the importer from meeting its clause obligations; a law or practice that respects the essence of fundamental rights and freedoms and does not exceed what is necessary and proportionate in a democratic society to safeguard an Art. 23(1) GDPR objective is not treated as contradicting the clauses (Clause 14(a)). That warranty must take due account of the transfer's specific circumstances, the destination country's relevant laws and practices and their safeguards, and any supplementary contractual, technical or organisational safeguards (Clause 14(b)).
- The importer must promptly notify the exporter if it has reason to believe it is, or has become, subject to laws or practices not in line with Clause 14(a); the exporter then identifies appropriate measures, suspends the transfer if no adequate safeguards can be ensured or on supervisory-authority instruction, and may terminate the contract for the affected processing (Clause 14(e), (f)).
- On receiving a legally binding government request for the transferred data, or on becoming aware of direct government access to it, the importer must promptly notify the exporter and, where possible, the data subject, covering the data requested, the requesting authority, the legal basis and the response given; if destination-country law bars that notification, the importer must use best efforts to have the prohibition waived and document those efforts (Clause 15.1).
- The importer must review the legality of a disclosure request, including whether it stays within the requesting authority's granted powers, challenge it where there are reasonable grounds to consider it unlawful, seek interim measures to suspend its effects while challenging, and give only the minimum information a reasonable interpretation of the request requires (Clause 15.2). The clauses do not require, and a contract should not add, a blanket promise that the importer will never comply with a lawful government request; what Clause 15.2 requires is legality review, minimisation and, where warranted, a challenge.
- The exporter may terminate the contract, for the processing under the clauses, where a suspended transfer is not brought back into compliance within a reasonable time (in any case within one month), where the importer is in substantial or persistent breach, or where the importer fails to comply with a binding court or supervisory authority decision on its clause obligations (Clause 16(b), (c)). On termination, data transferred before it must, at the exporter's choice, be immediately returned or deleted in its entirety, including copies, with the importer certifying deletion and continuing to comply until that happens (Clause 16(d)).
- Either party may revoke its agreement to be bound where the Commission adopts an Art. 45(3) GDPR adequacy decision covering the transfer, or where the GDPR becomes part of the destination country's own legal framework, without prejudice to the other GDPR obligations that still apply to the processing (Clause 16(e)).

### Joint controller arrangements

- Joint controllers must determine and agree, in a transparent manner, their respective responsibilities for GDPR compliance, through an arrangement between them, in particular for data subject rights and the Art. 13/14 information duties; the arrangement may designate a contact point for data subjects (Art. 26(1)). It must duly reflect the joint controllers' actual roles and relationships toward data subjects, and its essence must be made available to them (Art. 26(2)). A data subject may exercise their GDPR rights against any joint controller, whatever the arrangement's internal terms say (Art. 26(3)).
- The GDPR does not specify the arrangement's legal form, but the EDPB recommends a binding document, such as a contract or other legally binding act, stating the allocation of tasks in clear and plain language, and recording the subject matter and purpose of the processing, the type of personal data and the categories of data subjects (EDPB Guidelines 07/2020, para. 171, 173 to 175).
- The allocation of responsibilities need not be equal among joint controllers, but some duties cannot be allocated away and must be met by each of them regardless of the internal split, for example purpose limitation and the security measures protecting a shared tool, and each joint controller's own Art. 30 record and, where Art. 37(1) applies, its own DPO designation (EDPB Guidelines 07/2020, para. 169, 170). Sharing data between joint controllers, or using it for an additional purpose beyond the joint processing, still needs its own lawful basis and compatibility check.

## What the documents must not say

- A DPA must not claim compliance by merely restating the GDPR's own wording or reciting a generic reference to applicable law as the transfer safeguard; it must name the concrete measures taken and, for a transfer, the actual mechanism relied on (adequacy decision, specific SCCs, BCRs, an Art. 49 derogation, or the compelling-legitimate-interest fallback).
- A terms document must not label a provider a processor while in substance letting it use the data for purposes of its own beyond the engaged service; doing so makes that provider a controller for the additional processing and is itself an infringement if the contract still calls it a processor.
- A joint controller arrangement must not say, or imply, that data subjects may contact only the designated contact point; they may still contact any joint controller directly, and requiring otherwise would impose an excessive burden on exercising GDPR rights.
- A terms document must not describe a genuinely joint arrangement (parties who together decide both the purpose and the essential means of a shared processing activity) as a simple controller-processor relationship; the facts, not the label, decide the role.
- A transfer clause must not claim a destination country's government-access regime is compatible with the standard contractual clauses without the Clause 14 warranty and its documented assessment; nor may it promise the importer will never comply with a lawful disclosure request, since Clause 15.2 requires legality review and minimisation instead of a blanket refusal.
- A sub-processing clause must not let sub-processors change at any time without notice; under general authorisation the controller must get advance notice and a real chance to object, and under specific authorisation each sub-processor needs its own written approval.
- Neither Commission clause set may be substantively rewritten beyond selecting the relevant option and completing the Annexes; a party that alters the clause text itself risks losing the Art. 28 or Art. 46 basis the clauses were meant to provide.

## Product behaviour it assumes

- The system keeps a current register of sub-processors and the authorisation basis for each (specific written approval per engagement, or a general list with an objection window and a notice-in-advance process), so a new sub-processor is never added without the controller or customer being told in time to object.
- The checkout, onboarding or admin flow captures, for each processing engagement, which party decides deletion versus return of data at the end of the engagement, and lets the controller change that choice before the engagement ends.
- The security and breach-notification process is built so the processor can notify the controller without undue delay (not on a fixed schedule), since the controller's own 72-hour clock to the supervisory authority runs from the controller becoming aware, which a slow upstream notification can jeopardise.
- Records capture, for each transfer of personal data outside the EEA, the destination country, the legal mechanism relied on (adequacy, named safeguard, or a specific Art. 49(1) derogation), and, where the compelling-legitimate-interest fallback or any Art. 49 derogation is used, the assessment and the notices given to the supervisory authority and the data subject.
- Audit and inspection rights under a DPA are implemented with a real process (how to request one, what it covers, how costs are split) rather than a clause that exists only on paper; the cost structure does not make exercising this right impractical for the controller.
- Where two or more parties genuinely decide together on the purpose and essential means of a shared processing activity, the product surfaces a joint controller arrangement, not a plain controller-processor contract, with its own record of which party handles which data subject right and how the essence of that arrangement reaches data subjects.

## Prescribed wording

None. Neither Art. 28 nor the transfer rules of Chapter V, nor either Commission clause set, fix exact sentences a drafter must use; they fix required content; the clause text itself (whichever option or Module applies) is the only wording fixed verbatim, and it is reproduced in full as the model text of `templates/data-processing-agreement.md` rather than repeated here.

## Dates

- Both Commission Decisions (controller-to-processor clauses, Decision (EU) 2021/915; transfer clauses, Decision (EU) 2021/914) were adopted 4 June 2021 and entered into force on the twentieth day after publication in the Official Journal.
- The transfer clauses repealed the earlier 2001 and 2010 Commission decisions on standard contractual clauses with effect from 27 September 2021; a contract concluded under those earlier decisions before that date was deemed to provide appropriate safeguards until 27 December 2022, provided the processing it covers is unchanged and the earlier clauses still ensure appropriate safeguards.
- Adequacy decisions under Art. 45 carry a periodic review of at least every four years; a pre-GDPR adequacy decision or transfer authorisation under the repealed Directive 95/46/EC remains valid until amended, replaced or repealed under the corresponding GDPR procedure.
- EDPB Guidelines 07/2020 reached version 2.1 on 20 September 2022 (minor corrections to version 2.0, adopted 7 July 2021); the concepts of controller and processor they interpret are themselves unchanged from Directive 95/46/EC.

## Open points to raise

- Whether any processing engagement in scope is, in substance, a joint controller relationship rather than a controller-processor one: this turns on whether the other party takes part in deciding the purpose and the essential means, not on what the contract calls it, and needs a case-by-case check against the actual facts of each engagement.
- Which transfer tier each cross-border data flow actually relies on (an adequacy decision, a named Art. 46 safeguard, or a specific Art. 49(1) derogation), and whether that mechanism is documented per flow rather than asserted generically; this file cannot settle it without knowing each flow's destination and legal basis.
- Whether a given destination country's government-access regime lets the Clause 14 warranty be given at all, which needs a transfer impact assessment specific to that country and that importer, not a generic statement.
- Whether the controller-to-processor clauses (Decision (EU) 2021/915) or an individually negotiated DPA is the better fit for a given processor relationship, since the GDPR treats neither as preferred; this is a drafting choice, not a point the law settles.
- Whether any processing in scope touches special categories of data or criminal-conviction data, which raises the sensitive-data restrictions in both clause sets and may call for extra safeguards beyond the baseline security measures.
