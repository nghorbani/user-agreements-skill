# Governing law and jurisdiction

Citation: Regulation (EC) No 593/2008 (Rome I); Regulation (EU) No 1215/2012 (Brussels Ia, recast)
Version: Rome I, original text, consolidation not checked; Brussels Ia, consolidated 2015-02-26
Official text: https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32008R0593
Law as of: 2026-10-20

## When it applies

- Rome I governs which country's substantive law applies to a contract in a cross-border dispute.
  Brussels Ia governs which country's courts may hear that dispute. A terms document needs both a
  governing-law clause and, where it sets one, a jurisdiction clause, and the two questions are
  settled under different instruments, each with its own rules below.
- Rome I lets the parties choose the governing law expressly, or by a choice clearly shown by the
  contract's terms or the surrounding circumstances, for the whole contract or part of it, and lets
  them change that choice later (Art. 3(1), (2)).
- Where every other element of the situation sits in one country other than the chosen law's
  country, the choice does not displace that other country's non-derogable rules; where every other
  element sits in one or more member states, the choice does not displace non-derogable Union law
  (Art. 3(3), (4)).
- Absent a valid choice, the law is fixed by contract type: sale of goods, the seller's habitual
  residence; services, the provider's habitual residence; a right in rem or tenancy in immovable
  property, where the property sits (a short-term private tenancy of up to six consecutive months
  instead follows the landlord's residence, where the tenant is a natural person resident in the
  same country); franchise, the franchisee's residence; distribution, the distributor's residence;
  sale by auction, where the auction takes place, if that place can be fixed; and a defined
  multilateral trading-system contract, that system's own governing law (Art. 4(1)(a) to (h)).
  Anything not covered, or covered by more than one of those points, follows the residence of the
  party who must perform the contract's characteristic obligation, then a manifestly closer
  connection to another country, then the closest connection overall (Art. 4(2) to (4)). Carriage of
  goods and carriage of passengers sit outside this residual chain: they follow their own rules under
  Art. 5, not Art. 4 (Art. 4(1)).
- A contract for the carriage of goods, absent a choice of law, is governed by the carrier's
  habitual-residence law, provided the place of receipt, the place of delivery or the consignor's
  habitual residence also sits in that country; where none of those three does, the law of the
  agreed place of delivery applies instead (Art. 5(1)). A contract for the carriage of passengers,
  absent a choice of law, is governed by the law of the passenger's habitual residence, provided the
  place of departure or the place of destination also sits in that country; where neither does, the
  law of the carrier's habitual residence applies instead; and, in either case, a manifestly closer
  connection to another country displaces that result (Art. 5(2), (3)). For passenger carriage, the
  parties' own choice of law is narrowed to the law of one of five countries: the passenger's
  habitual residence, the carrier's habitual residence or place of central administration, the place
  of departure, or the place of destination (Art. 5(2)).
- A consumer contract (a natural person contracting for a purpose outside their trade or
  profession, with a professional) is governed by the consumer's habitual-residence law whenever the
  professional pursues commercial activity in that country, or directs such activity there by any
  means, and the contract falls within that activity's scope (Art. 6(1)). Five categories sit
  outside this rule: a service supplied to the consumer only in a country other than their
  residence; carriage other than an inclusive-price package of travel and accommodation; a right in
  rem or tenancy in immovable property other than a timeshare use right; a financial instrument or
  specified securities and fund terms, so far as these do not amount to a financial service; and the
  Art. 4(1)(h) trading-system contract (Art. 6(4)(a) to (e)). Where none of the activity conditions
  is met, the ordinary Art. 3 and 4 rules apply instead (Art. 6(3)).
- A contract within Brussels Ia's consumer-contract section is one of three kinds: an instalment-
  credit sale of goods; a loan or other credit financing such a sale; or, in every other case, a
  contract concluded with a trader who pursues commercial activity in the consumer's member state of
  domicile, or directs such activity there by any means, where the contract falls within that
  activity's scope (Brussels Ia Art. 17(1)(a) to (c)). A carriage contract is outside this section
  unless it is an inclusive-price package combining travel and accommodation (Art. 17(3)). A trader
  with no member-state domicile but a branch, agency or other establishment in one is treated as
  domiciled there for a dispute arising from that establishment's own operations (Art. 17(2)).
- Within that section, a consumer may sue the trader in the trader's member state of domicile, or at
  the consumer's own choice in the courts of the consumer's own domicile; the trader may sue the
  consumer only in the consumer's own member state of domicile (Art. 18(1), (2)). A counter-claim in
  the court already seized of the original claim is unaffected (Art. 18(3)).
- Outside the consumer section, parties to any contractual relationship may agree that a member
  state's court or courts are to have jurisdiction over disputes connected with it; the named court
  has that jurisdiction unless the agreement is null and void as to its substantive validity under
  that member state's own law, and the jurisdiction is exclusive unless the parties agreed otherwise
  (Art. 25(1)). A jurisdiction clause forming part of a contract is treated as independent of the
  contract's other terms, so a challenge to the contract's own validity is not by itself a ground to
  contest the jurisdiction clause (Art. 25(5)).
- Whatever governing law a contract chooses, the chosen law never displaces the overriding mandatory
  provisions of the law of the forum, the court actually hearing the dispute; those provisions apply
  regardless of the contract's own choice-of-law clause (Art. 9(2)). A court may also give effect to
  the overriding mandatory provisions of the country where the contract's obligations are or were to
  be performed, where those provisions render that performance unlawful (Art. 9(1), (3)).

## What the documents must say

- A governing-law clause naming the trader's home member state must disclose, in plain and
  intelligible language, that the consumer still keeps the mandatory consumer-protection rules of
  the law that would apply without the clause, ordinarily the consumer's habitual-residence law
  under Rome I Art. 6(1) where the trader directs activity there (Case C-191/15, VKI v Amazon,
  ECLI:EU:C:2016:612, paragraph 71 and operative part 2). This follows from the general rule that a
  clause whose effect a mandatory rule fixes must disclose that rule to the consumer; silence about
  it is itself a transparency defect (same judgment, paragraph 69).
- A jurisdiction clause relying on Brussels Ia Art. 25(1) must take one of three forms: in writing or
  evidenced in writing; in a form according with practice the parties have established between
  themselves; or, in international trade, in a form according with a usage the parties knew or ought
  to have known and that is widely known and regularly observed by parties to that kind of contract
  in that trade (Art. 25(1)(a) to (c)). An electronic communication that provides a durable record of
  the agreement is equivalent to writing for this purpose (Art. 25(1)(a); Art. 25(2)).
- A jurisdiction clause presented by click-wrapping, where a purchaser clicks a box to accept general
  terms and a further, separate click on a hyperlink opens those terms in a new window, provides a
  durable record for this purpose as long as the method makes it possible to print and save the text
  of the terms before the contract is concluded; it does not matter whether the window containing
  the terms opens automatically (Case C-322/14, El Majdoub v CarsOnTheWeb.Deutschland, judgment of
  21 May 2015, paragraphs 39, 40 and operative part). This judgment interprets Article 23(2) of the
  predecessor Brussels I Regulation (EC) No 44/2001, not Article 25(2) of the recast Brussels Ia
  text cut for this file. Comparing the two statutory texts directly, the two articles use the same
  wording ("Any communication by electronic means which provides a durable record of the agreement
  shall be equivalent to 'writing'"), so the reasoning should apply equally to the current text, but
  the Court has not itself ruled on the recast wording (see Open points).
- A jurisdiction clause must be the subject of a genuine consensus between the parties, clearly and
  precisely shown, as a separate and logically prior question from the form question above; the form
  test does not relax the consensus requirement (El Majdoub, paragraphs 29, 30, citing Case
  C-106/95, MSG, and Case C-543/10, Refcomp).

## What the documents must not say

- A governing-law clause must not create the impression, by what it says or what it leaves out, that
  only the named law governs the contract, without the disclosure above (VKI v Amazon, paragraph 71).
- A terms document must not fix the consumer's forum to a single named court in place of the two fora
  Brussels Ia Art. 18(1) already gives the consumer (the trader's domicile, or the consumer's own
  domicile); such a clause cannot narrow the consumer's choice and cannot be imposed before a dispute
  has arisen, except through one of the three routes below.
- The consumer-contract jurisdiction protection may be departed from by agreement only in three
  cases: an agreement entered into after the dispute has arisen; an agreement giving the consumer
  additional fora beyond those the section already allows; or an agreement between a consumer and
  trader who share a member state domicile at the time of contracting, naming that state's courts,
  and valid under that state's own law (Art. 19(1) to (3)). A clause fixing a different forum in
  advance, outside these three routes, does not bind the consumer.
- A jurisdiction or choice-of-law clause must not be used to redirect a collective injunction against
  unfair terms to the trader's own home-country law; the trader cannot manufacture a manifestly
  closer connection to that law merely by naming it in its own terms (VKI v Amazon, paragraphs 46,
  47; this is a Rome II point, and Rome II is not covered in these references, so treat the wider
  point as an open point for counsel. It is noted here only because the same judgment also decides
  the two governing-law and transparency rules above).

## Product behaviour it assumes

- The checkout or account-creation flow presents the governing terms, including any jurisdiction
  clause, through a mechanism that lets the user print or save the text before the contract is
  concluded; an affirmative click to accept, with a further click needed to open the terms in a new
  window, is a sufficient pattern and need not display the terms automatically (El Majdoub,
  paragraph 39 and operative part).
- Before a governing-law clause is finalised for a given market, the product records which member
  states the trader in fact pursues commercial activity in, or directs commercial activity to (by
  language options, currency, delivery or marketing reach), since Rome I Art. 6(1) and Brussels Ia
  Art. 17(1)(c) both look at activity actually carried on, not at a disclaimer in the terms.
- Where the terms are shown to consumers across more than one member state, the governing-law clause
  carries the mandatory-protection disclosure above, rather than a single undifferentiated
  statement of the trader's home law.
- A jurisdiction clause states expressly whether the named court's jurisdiction is exclusive or
  non-exclusive, since Brussels Ia Art. 25(1) makes it exclusive by default only where the parties
  have not agreed otherwise.

## Prescribed wording

None. Neither Regulation sets a form of words a contract must reproduce; both state a choice-of-law
or jurisdiction test and its consequence, not a phrase to copy. The disclosure duty the Court of
Justice requires in VKI v Amazon (paragraph 71) is a drafting obligation stated in the Court's own
words describing the defect, not a sentence prescribed for a trader to insert.

## Dates

- Brussels Ia Art. 25 applies in its consolidated 2015-02-26 form; Rome I's consolidation status was
  not checked for this file (see Open points).
- El Majdoub was decided 21 May 2015, interpreting Article 23(2) of Regulation (EC) No 44/2001
  (Brussels I), the predecessor to Article 25(2) of the recast Brussels Ia text.
- VKI v Amazon was decided 28 July 2016.

## Open points to raise

- Whether a court has since confirmed that Article 25(2) of Brussels Ia carries the El Majdoub
  reasoning on click-wrapping and durable records: the two articles share the same wording, but the
  judgment itself interprets only the predecessor Brussels I provision, and these references do not
  cover a later ruling on the recast wording directly; treat it as an open point for counsel.
- Whether Rome I's consolidation status matters for a specific contract: the cut text notes
  consolidation was not checked, so a drafter relying on an amendment to Rome I since its original
  2008 text should confirm the current consolidated text separately.
- Which member states a given terms document in fact reaches, since both the Rome I Art. 6(1)
  consumer-contract floor and the Brussels Ia Art. 17(1)(c) consumer-jurisdiction section turn on
  activity actually pursued or directed there, not on a stated scope.
