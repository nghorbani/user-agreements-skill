# Product Liability Directive (Directive (EU) 2024/2853)

Citation: Directive (EU) 2024/2853 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 23 October 2024 on liability for
defective products and repealing Council Directive 85/374/EEC
Version: original, no amendment found
Official text: https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32024L2853
Law as of: 2026-10-20

This file is the directive-level reference for no-fault liability toward natural persons harmed by a defective
product, including software, an AI system, a digital manufacturing file, or a digital service the product cannot
work without. It repeals and replaces Council Directive 85/374/EEC, and it sets a single standard across the Union:
a member state may not keep or introduce a more protective or a less protective rule on anything this Directive
covers (Art. 3). It covers who answers for a defect, how a claimant proves one, what an operator can raise as a
defence, and how long a claim can be brought. It does not set a prescribed consumer notice or a model cancellation
label; those duties live in `references/eu/crd.md` and in Germany's own distance-selling rules
(`references/de/bgb-fernabsatz.md`). It works alongside the Unfair Contract Terms Directive
(`references/eu/uctd.md`) for the separate question of whether a liability clause is also an unfair term, and
alongside the General Product Safety Regulation and the Digital Services Act for market-surveillance and
illegal-content duties this file does not restate.

## When it applies

- The Directive applies to a product placed on the market or put into service after 9 December 2026 (Art. 2(1)).
  Directive 85/374/EEC is repealed from that date but continues to apply to a product placed on the market or put
  into service before it (Art. 21). A trader with an existing catalogue checks each product's own placement date
  against this cutoff, since the two regimes can apply to the same trader at once.
- A product is any movable, even if it is integrated into, or inter-connected with, another movable or an
  immovable; this expressly includes electricity, digital manufacturing files, raw materials, and software
  (Art. 4(1)).
  Software, including firmware, an operating system, an application, and an AI system, is a product whatever its
  mode of supply: stored on a device, accessed over a network or through cloud technology, or supplied as a
  service (recital 13). A developer or producer of software, including an AI system provider, is treated as a
  manufacturer (recital 13).
- Mere information is not a product: the content of a digital file, such as a media file, an e-book, or the bare
  source code of software, sits outside the Directive (recital 13). A digital manufacturing file is a product only
  because it carries the functional information needed to drive automated machinery or tools (a computer-assisted
  design file for a drill, lathe, mill, or 3D printer); the digital file as a storage medium is not what makes it a
  product, the automated-production function is (Art. 4(2); recital 16).
- A digital service the product cannot function without, a "related service", is treated as a component of the
  product whenever it sits within the manufacturer's control (Art. 4(3), (4), (5); recital 17). The Directive names
  the continuous supply of traffic data to a navigation system, a health-monitoring service reading a product's own
  sensors, a smart-fridge temperature-control service, and a voice-assistant control service as examples (recital
  17). A bare internet access service is not a related service, and a manufacturer does not answer under this
  Directive for a connectivity failure as such, though a product that loses safety the moment connectivity drops
  can still be defective on that ground (recital 17).
- Free and open-source software developed or supplied outside a commercial activity is outside scope, since
  software developed or supplied that way is by definition not placed on the market (Art. 2(2); recital 14).
  Developing or contributing to such software, and supplying it through an open repository, is not itself making it
  available on the market, unless that occurs in the course of a commercial activity; supply by a non-profit body is
  likewise not commercial by itself (recital 14). The exclusion does not survive a price or a personal-data
  exchange: once the software is supplied for payment, or for personal data used for anything beyond keeping that
  same software secure, compatible, or interoperable, the supply is commercial and the Directive applies
  (Art. 2(2); recital 14). Where open-source software supplied outside a commercial activity is later integrated by
  a manufacturer, as a component, into a product placed on the market commercially, that integrating manufacturer
  can be held liable even though the original software developer never placed a product or component on the market
  (recital 15).
- Three further carve-outs: damage from a nuclear accident covered by an international convention a member state
  ratified (Art. 2(3)); and, left wholly unaffected rather than excluded, Union personal-data law, any national-law
  right in contract or in non-contractual liability on a ground other than this Directive's own defectiveness
  standard, and any special liability system that existed in national law on 30 July 1985 (Art. 2(4)).

## What the documents must say

- Where the manufacturer sits outside the Union, the terms name the importer or the authorised representative
  established within the Union, so an injured person always has a reachable operator to claim against (Art. 8(1),
  first subparagraph, point (c)). A fulfilment service provider only steps into that role where no importer or
  authorised representative is established in the Union (Art. 8(1), first subparagraph, point (c)(iii)); say so
  plainly where the business model relies on one.
- Where the terms cover software, firmware, an AI feature, or a connected service supplied alongside a physical
  product, state that this software or service is a component of the product for liability purposes once it sits
  within the manufacturer's control; do not describe it as outside the manufacturer's responsibility (Art. 4(1),
  (3), (4)).
- Where the terms promise software updates or upgrades, state what is supplied and for how long. A manufacturer
  that retains the ability to supply updates, itself or through a third party, is treated as retaining control of
  the product (Art. 4(5), point (b)), and is not exempted from liability for a defect caused by a failure to
  supply a security update or upgrade needed to maintain safety, so long as the update's supply or installation was
  within the manufacturer's control (Art. 11(2), point (c)). The Directive itself imposes no duty to supply any
  update or upgrade at all (recital 51); this exposure only ever attaches once a manufacturer chooses to supply
  updates, or keeps the ability to.
- A limitation-of-liability section states that nothing in the terms limits or excludes the manufacturer's, or any
  other liable operator's, statutory liability toward an injured natural person under this Directive. Any contrary
  contractual provision is simply ineffective against that person (Art. 15); there is no deviation-acceptance
  mechanism here that disclosure and separate consent could use to validate a limitation.
- A section on how long a claim can be brought reflects, rather than tries to shorten, the statutory periods: three
  years from the injured person's awareness of the damage, the defectiveness, and the liable operator's identity
  (Art. 16(1)), and, separately, 10 years from placing the product on the market or putting it into service, or
  25 years where a personal injury's symptoms were medically slow to emerge (Art. 17). A clause that starts either
  clock running before its statutory trigger, or that shortens either period, is void against the injured person
  under the same Art. 15 anti-waiver rule.
- Terms for a product designed for upgrade, refurbishment, third-party repair, or continuous learning (an
  AI-enabled product), or for a marketplace offering reconditioned goods, distinguish ordinary repair and routine
  updates, which do not reset liability, from a substantial modification, which makes the modifying party a
  manufacturer of the modified product and starts a new expiry period once that modified product is made available
  on the market or put into service (Art. 4(18); Art. 8(2); Art. 17(1), point (b)). A party other than the original
  manufacturer that makes a substantial modification is exempted from liability only for the part of the product
  the modification did not affect (Art. 11(1), point (g)); terms for a refurbishing or modifying business do not
  assume blanket protection for the whole product once a substantial modification has occurred.
- A marketplace or platform operator drafting its own terms checks the same test a distributor faces before
  assuming it is a mere host: it is treated the same as a distributor once its presentation of a listing, or its
  handling of a transaction, would lead an average consumer to believe the product is supplied by the platform
  itself, or by a trader acting under the platform's authority or control, and the platform then fails to identify
  a relevant economic operator established in the Union within one month of being asked (Art. 8(4); recital 38). A
  plain statement that the platform only hosts third-party listings does not by itself defeat that test.

## What the documents must not say

- No clause may limit or exclude an economic operator's liability toward the injured person under this Directive,
  by any wording, however prominent or separately accepted (Art. 15). This reaches a financial ceiling set by a
  national-law provision just as much as a contractual cap (recital 56); a terms document cannot borrow a cap
  acceptable under a different liability regime and apply it here.
- No liability section may describe the damage this Directive compensates more narrowly than Art. 6(1): death or
  personal injury, including medically recognised psychological harm (Art. 6(1), point (a)); damage to, or
  destruction of, property, other than the defective product itself, a product damaged by its own integrated
  defective component within the manufacturer's control, or property used exclusively for professional purposes
  (Art. 6(1), point (b)); and destruction or corruption of data not used for professional purposes (Art. 6(1),
  point (c)). Material losses from any of these are always covered, and non-material losses are covered where
  national law allows (Art. 6(2)); a clause narrowing either coverage is the same liability limitation Art. 15
  already bars.
- No clause may shorten the three-year limitation period or the 10-year (or 25-year) expiry period, or start either
  clock running earlier than its statutory trigger event; either move is a liability limitation barred by Art. 15
  just as a cap would be (Art. 16; Art. 17).
- No clause may describe a related service, software (including an update or upgrade), a missing security update
  or upgrade necessary to maintain safety, or a substantial modification as outside the manufacturer's liability
  once any of these sits within the manufacturer's control; Art. 11(2) blocks the ordinary defence that the defect
  arose only after the product left the manufacturer's control, for exactly these four cases (Art. 11(2), points
  (a) to (d)).
- No clause may rely on a list of warnings or disclosed side effects to make an otherwise unsafe product count as
  safe. Defectiveness turns on the safety the public at large is entitled to expect, not on what the presentation
  disclosed, so a warning cannot cure an unsafe design (Art. 7(2), point (a); recital 31).
- No clause may claim that supplying a later, better product, or an update or upgrade, shows the earlier version
  was defective, and no clause may use that same fact to argue the other way, that the new version is somehow
  exempt from scrutiny (Art. 7(3)).
- No clause describing a licence waiver between manufacturers may be presented as reaching the injured person's own
  claim. The one waiver the Directive allows runs only between a manufacturer that integrates software and a
  qualifying microenterprise or small-enterprise software manufacturer, over the right of recourse between them; it
  never limits or excludes liability toward the injured person (Art. 12(2); recital 54).
- Where two or more economic operators are liable for the same damage, they are jointly and severally liable to the
  injured person, who can claim the full amount from any one of them (Art. 12(1); recital 53). An indemnity or
  allocation clause between a manufacturer and a component supplier may describe how they settle that amount between
  themselves, a recourse question left to national law (Art. 14), but no such clause may be presented as limiting
  either party's own full liability to the injured person.

## Product behaviour it assumes

- The product, or the trader's own process, can identify every economic operator in the Art. 8(1) chain on request:
  the manufacturer, and, for a non-Union manufacturer, the importer or the authorised representative established in
  the Union, with a fulfilment service provider named only where neither of those is established in the Union
  (Art. 8(1)).
- A distributor's process answers an injured person's identification request within one month, naming an upstream
  economic operator or its own supplier; failing to do so within that month is what makes the distributor itself
  liable (Art. 8(3)).
- Where the manufacturer supplies or keeps the ability to supply software updates or upgrades, the update process
  distinguishes a security update necessary to maintain safety from an ordinary feature update, and logs whether
  each update was supplied and whether it was installed, since liability for a lack of a security update depends on
  whether the supply or the installation was within the manufacturer's control (Art. 4(5), point (b); Art. 11(2),
  point (c); recital 51). The same installation log also matters the other way: where an injured person negligently
  failed to install an update or upgrade that would have mitigated or avoided the damage, the operator's own
  liability may be reduced or disallowed on that ground (Art. 13(2); recital 55), unlike a third party's own act or
  omission, which never reduces the operator's liability (Art. 13(1)).
- The product and its support process can show, for a dispute, that a given version was free of the defect alleged,
  or that the defect arose only after the product left the manufacturer's control, since an operator must prove
  this itself to raise the Art. 11(1), point (c) exemption (Art. 11(1), point (c)).
- Where the product can keep learning or acquiring new features after it is placed on the market or put into
  service, the design and the underlying algorithms are built to prevent hazardous behaviour developing on their
  own; a manufacturer that designs for this kind of adaptation stays liable for harmful behaviour that develops
  (Art. 7(2), point (c); recital 32).
- Where the product relies on another inter-connected product (a smart-home system, an accessory, a companion
  device), the safety assessment accounts for the reasonably foreseeable effect that other product has on this one
  (Art. 7(2), point (d)).
- The refurbishment, repair, or modification process records whether a given change meets the substantial-
  modification threshold (a change to the product's original performance, purpose, or type not foreseen in the
  original risk assessment, that also changes the nature of a hazard, creates a new one, or raises the level of
  risk, where no sector-specific product-safety threshold already answers the question) (Art. 4(18)), since that
  record is what lets the modifying party show which part of the product its own Art. 11(1), point (g) exemption
  covers.
- Where the trader is, or might be treated as, a distributor-equivalent platform under Art. 8(4), the platform's own
  process can show, on request, how a listing or a transaction is presented, since that presentation, not a
  disclaimer elsewhere in the terms, is what the average-consumer test in recital 38 turns on.

## Prescribed wording

This file has no prescribed wording of its own; the Directive sets no model form, label, or fixed phrase a trader's
terms must reproduce. Any prescribed label used elsewhere in a checkout, cancellation, or notice flow is covered in
`references/eu/crd.md`, in `references/de/bgb-fernabsatz.md`, and in `references/prescribed-wording.md`, not here.

## Dates

- 9 December 2026: date from which the Directive applies to a product placed on the market or put into service
  (Art. 2(1); Art. 21).
- 9 December 2026: date from which Directive 85/374/EEC is repealed; it continues to apply to a product placed on
  the market or put into service before that date (Art. 21).
- 9 December 2026: deadline for a member state to bring its transposing laws, regulations, and administrative
  provisions into force, and to inform the Commission (Art. 22(1)).
- 9 December 2026: deadline for a member state that wants to keep an existing development-risk-defence derogation
  under Art. 18(1) to notify its text to the Commission (Art. 18(1)).
- 9 December 2030, then every five years: Commission evaluation of the Directive's application, reported to the
  European Parliament, the Council, and the European Economic and Social Committee (Art. 20).
- 2026-10-20: law-as-of date for this file.

## Open points to raise

- Which member state's law governs the claim, since whether the development-risk defence (Art. 11(1), point (e))
  is available at all depends on whether that member state has used the Art. 18 derogation to maintain or introduce
  liability for specific product categories regardless of the state of the art; this file does not track which
  states have notified a derogation, and a dedicated reference file for a specific member state's transposing
  measure is an open item until one exists in this skill.
- Whether a given software or related-service supply counts as "within the manufacturer's control" on its actual
  facts (an authorisation, a consent, or a presentation that makes a third-party component look like the
  manufacturer's own), since Art. 4(5) turns this on conduct, not on a label the terms choose to apply, and a
  product-specific review is needed before relying on either side of that line (Art. 4(5); recital 18).
- Whether the trader's own marketplace or platform offering meets the Art. 8(4) presentation test on its actual
  listings and transaction flow, since that is a fact-specific, average-consumer question this file states the
  standard for but cannot answer for a specific product page (Art. 8(4); recital 38).
- Whether a specific product's catalogue includes items placed on the market before 9 December 2026 that remain
  under Directive 85/374/EEC alongside items placed on the market after that date under this Directive, since the
  two regimes can require different terms language for the same trader at the same time (Art. 21).
